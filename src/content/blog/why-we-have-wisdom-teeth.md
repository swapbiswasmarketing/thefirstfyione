Your other adult teeth are already in place when, [in your teens or early 20s](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/wisdom-tooth-removal/), a last molar tries to shove its way in at the very back of each side of each jaw. Sometimes it arrives fine. Sometimes it comes in crooked, gets stuck, or has to be cut out. Which raises a fair question: why do we have wisdom teeth at all?

## So why do we have wisdom teeth?

We have wisdom teeth because our primate ancestors had them: [apes have wisdom teeth, and so do monkeys](https://theconversation.com/bad-molars-the-origins-of-wisdom-teeth-104835), as Julia Boughner, an evolutionary developmental anthropologist at the University of Saskatchewan, points out. The tooth is normal. Most of the trouble comes from the jaw it lands in.

A [2011 global study of human lower jaws](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22106280/) by Noreen von Cramon-Taubadel found that hunter-gatherers have consistently longer and narrower mandibles than farmers, and argued that lighter chewing makes the jaw grow and develop differently. That, she wrote, explains why there is often a "mismatch between the size of the lower face and the dentition." The wisdom tooth, arriving last, is the one that runs out of room.

## The teeth that arrive late

"Wisdom teeth" is the everyday name for the third molars, and [most adults have four](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisdom_tooth), one in each back corner. The name [probably translates the Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisdom_tooth) *dens sapientiae*: the tooth turns up at an age when a person is presumably wiser than the child who grew the others.

The trouble they cause is ancient news. In [*History of Animals*](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/history_anim.2.ii.html), in D'Arcy Wentworth Thompson's translation, Aristotle says the wisdom teeth "come at the age of twenty years" and describes cases of very late eruption "causing great pain in their coming."

They also start late. [Wisdom teeth often don't start growing until age nine](https://theconversation.com/bad-molars-the-origins-of-wisdom-teeth-104835), Boughner writes, while second molars start developing around age three. She names lack of space at the back of the jawbone as the main reason wisdom teeth get impacted, and her team found that when they form and emerge very late, the first and second molars have already claimed most of that space.

## A tougher menu built a longer jaw

Bone and teeth answer to chewing in different ways. Jaw bone can reshape itself during childhood as the work of chewing changes, while ["dental tissue does not remodel,"](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0117301) as a 2015 study in *PLOS ONE* puts it.

One experiment used rock hyraxes, small mammals whose faces, like ours, do not jut forward. In a [2004 study led by Daniel Lieberman](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15183669/), chewing raw food produced strains as much as two-fold higher at some sites than chewing cooked food, and hyraxes raised on cooked food grew about 10% less in the lower and rear parts of the face.

Stone tools changed chewing before cooking did. [Zink and Lieberman](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26958832/) reported in 2016 that if meat made up a third of an early human diet, chewing cycles per year would have fallen by nearly 2 million, and that slicing meat and pounding tubers with stone tools would cut the chewing force needed by an additional 12%. That combination, they argue, is what first allowed smaller teeth and chewing muscles to evolve in *Homo*; cooking was apparently uncommon until 500,000 years ago.

Farming pushed the same way. [Von Cramon-Taubadel found](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22106280/) that the lower jaw, unlike the rest of the skull, tracks how a population feeds itself rather than its genetic ancestry. The [*PLOS ONE* study](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0117301) measured the same 292 individuals across 21 archaeological populations from the Near East, Anatolia and Europe, and found that the jaws of early farmers changed shape in ways their teeth did not.

## Jaws shrank and teeth did not keep pace

That mismatch shows up as crowding at the back. A [2016 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26561441/) of 49 studies covering 83,484 people aged 17 and over put the worldwide rate of third molar impaction at **24.40%**, with lower wisdom teeth impacted more often than upper ones.

A wisdom tooth without room to come through may be [blocked by another tooth](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/wisdom-tooth-removal/), which the NHS calls impacted, or partly covered by the gum, and the NHS lists what can follow:

- pain and swelling
- infection of the gum around the tooth (pericoronitis)
- food getting stuck, tooth decay and gum disease
- a cyst or a dental abscess

Crowding is not purely modern. Dental crowding has been reported in an early modern human from Qafzeh cave in Israel, dated to about 100,000 years ago, and when it turned from a rare condition into a common one "remains to be investigated," the [*PLOS ONE* authors](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0117301) write. Hunter-gatherer teeth also wore down far more, but the poor fit between jaw and teeth "cannot be attributed to a reduction in dental wear," they note, citing earlier work, because young hunter-gatherers with little wear yet would then show more crowding.

## Missing wisdom teeth are nothing new

Some people never grow one or more wisdom teeth at all: the tooth simply never forms, a condition called agenesis. It is common. A [2015 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25883107/) of 92 studies and 63,314 people put the worldwide rate at **22.63%**, with study estimates ranging from 5.32% to 56.0% and large differences between regions, and found women 14% more likely than men to lack at least one.

It is also old. A [*Homo erectus* lower jaw from Chenchiawo in China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lantian_Man), dated to between 710 and 684 thousand years ago, is missing its third molars, the first such case found in an extinct human species.

Whether humans are evolving away from wisdom teeth is disputed. A [2025 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41536660/) of archaeological evidence from China, which also reports congenital absence in Lantian Man, calls third molar degeneration "a key marker of human dental evolution." Asked whether evolution is weeding them out, Boughner answers ["But I don't think so."](https://theconversation.com/bad-molars-the-origins-of-wisdom-teeth-104835) Impacted wisdom teeth rarely kill anyone, she argues, and to be selected against they would have to remove people from the gene pool before they had children; she also doubts that specific "impaction genes" exist. Space is not the whole story either. "Scientists still can't explain why some wisdom teeth become impacted," [she writes](https://theconversation.com/bad-molars-the-origins-of-wisdom-teeth-104835).

## Removing healthy wisdom teeth is still argued over

The question most readers face is whether a wisdom tooth that is stuck but causing no trouble should come out anyway. In England and Wales the official answer has been no since March 2000, when [NICE](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ta1/chapter/1-Guidance) advised that "The practice of prophylactic removal of pathology-free impacted third molars should be discontinued in the NHS." Surgery is reserved for teeth with evidence of disease, such as unrestorable decay, an abscess or a cyst; a first bout of gum infection around the tooth does not qualify ["unless particularly severe,"](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ta1/chapter/1-Guidance) but a second does.

The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/wisdom-tooth-removal/) puts it plainly today: "If it's not causing problems, the tooth will usually be left where it is and monitored at your routine dental check-ups."

NICE's answer is under review. It [says it is updating](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ta1) the guidance after a 2015 look at the evidence, and a [2020 NIHR-funded review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32589125/) found the evidence on impacted lower wisdom teeth "very limited", though its exploratory model suggested that "prophylactic removal may be the more cost-effective strategy."

Cochrane has not settled it either. Its [2020 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32368796/) found only two eligible studies, one trial and one cohort, and very low-certainty evidence from the cohort that keeping such teeth may be associated with a higher long-term risk of gum disease around the neighboring second molar. Its advice is shared decision-making between patient and clinician, with regular checks if the teeth stay.

> "Insufficient evidence is available to determine whether asymptomatic disease-free impacted wisdom teeth should be removed or retained." - [Cochrane review, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32368796/)

In the United States, "millions of mostly young people continue to have prophylactic extraction of their wisdom teeth," the [American Public Health Association](https://www.apha.org/policy-and-advocacy/public-health-policy-briefs/policy-database/2014/07/24/14/29/opposition-to-prophylactic-removal-of-third-molars-wisdom-teeth) wrote in a 2008 policy opposing the practice, which it called "not evidence based." The impaction meta-analysis made a related point: ["The percentage of individuals with impacted M3s is much smaller than the percentage that undergoes clinical treatment for M3 problems."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26561441/) None of this settles a single mouth, which is a conversation to have with a dentist.

So the wisdom tooth is less a design flaw than a latecomer: an ordinary primate molar that forms last and erupts last, into a jaw grown on softer food than the diet it was built for. Aristotle already knew it could hurt. What to do about one that does not is still being argued. Strictly FYI.
