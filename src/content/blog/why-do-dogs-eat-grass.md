A dog working its way along a grass verge can look choosy. It noses past one tuft, stops at another and only then tears off a few blades. Owners notice the fussiness too: in a survey of plant-eating dogs published in 2008, several respondents said their dogs seemed very ["selective" in regard to the type or portion of grass eaten](https://gwern.net/doc/dog/2008-sueda.pdf).

Sometimes the dog throws up a little later, and that is the part people remember. Eating grass in order to vomit is the explanation Aristotle gave in the History of Animals, written around [350 BC](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/history_anim.8.viii.html).

## Most grass-eating dogs look normal first and do not regularly vomit

Grass eating is a normal habit for most dogs, not a response to feeling ill. In a 2008 study of **1,571** plant-eating dogs by Karen Sueda, Benjamin Hart and Kelly Cliff, [only 9% were reported to frequently appear ill before eating plants and only 22%](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65de42de7c7033e84bea5ee4) to frequently vomit afterwards. The explanation the authors favor is an instinct inherited from wild ancestors, perhaps one that once helped sweep out intestinal worms.

## Aristotle wrote the sick-dog theory down twice

In D'Arcy Thompson's translation, Aristotle says of the fierce, jag-toothed carnivores: ["These carnivorous animals never eat grass except when they are sick, just as dogs bring on a vomit by eating grass and thereby purge themselves."](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/history_anim.8.viii.html)

He returns to it in a passage on quadrupeds that "help themselves in clever ways," where ["Dogs, when they are ill, eat some kind of grass and produce vomiting."](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/history_anim.9.ix.html) On the same page, wild goats in Crete wounded by arrows are said to go looking for dittany, a plant [supposed "to have the property of ejecting arrows in the body."](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/history_anim.9.ix.html)

The modern version adds a second guess. Clients ask vets whether animals eat grass because they are sick, Hart wrote in a 2009 article for veterinarians, and ["Do they eat plants because they have a dietary deficiency?"](https://www.dvm360.com/view/why-do-dogs-and-cats-eat-grass)

## The UC Davis surveys checked both guesses against owners

The team at the University of California, Davis asked three groups. In a pilot, 25 veterinary students all said their dogs ate grass, [none reported observing signs of illness](https://www.dvm360.com/view/why-do-dogs-and-cats-eat-grass) beforehand, and only 8% said their dogs regularly vomited afterward. At the university's teaching hospital, [79% of 47 owners](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65de42de7c7033e84bea5ee4) reported that their dog had eaten grass or other plants.

The third group came through an internet survey aimed at owners of plant-eating dogs. To be counted, an owner had to spend [at least 6 h per day](https://gwern.net/doc/dog/2008-sueda.pdf) with the dog and have seen it eat plants at least 10 times.

[Of 3,340 surveys returned, 1,571 met enrollment criteria](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65de42de7c7033e84bea5ee4), and among those dogs 68% ate plants daily or weekly and grass was the plant 79% ate most often. The other plants owners named were mainly berries, sticks and leaves.

For each group, the paper's discussion gives these shares of owners reporting [dogs that "appeared normal before eating plants"](https://gwern.net/doc/dog/2008-sueda.pdf) and no regular vomiting afterwards:

| Group | Looked normal beforehand | No regular vomiting afterwards |
|---|---|---|
| Pilot, 25 veterinary students | 100% | 87% (92% by the results section) |
| Teaching-hospital clients | 88% | 82% |
| Internet survey, 1,571 dogs | 92% | 78% |

The paper's own numbers shift between sections. The abstract says 9% of the internet-survey dogs frequently looked ill first, and the results section says 8%. The pilot's 8% regular vomiters, given in both the results section and Hart's article, leave 92% with no regular vomiting, not the discussion's 87%.

The diet guess fared no better: in Hart's account, there was no indication that dogs fed mainly table scraps or raw food ate grass more than dogs on a balanced commercial diet, or that dogs getting less fiber ate more plants. The 2008 paper calls this a provisional conclusion until fiber, vitamins and minerals are studied in detail. Hart's summary of the vomiting: ["Vomiting seems to be incidental to, rather than caused by, plant eating."](https://www.dvm360.com/view/why-do-dogs-and-cats-eat-grass)

## Aristotle was not wholly wrong

Some dogs do fit the old story. In the internet survey, Hart wrote, dogs that showed signs of illness before eating plants were [more likely to vomit afterward](https://www.dvm360.com/view/why-do-dogs-and-cats-eat-grass) than dogs that did not, and the authors allow that stomach or gut discomfort sometimes sets the behavior off.

One dog cuts against the survey on diet. A 2007 case report from Konkuk University in Seoul describes an **11-year-old** miniature poodle that had been [eating plants and then vomiting daily for 7 years](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17675815/), and stopped three days after it was moved to a high-fiber diet. The authors called it "a possibility" that a deficient diet, especially in fiber, relates to plant eating.

## Wolves eat grass too, and some of their grassy droppings held worms

Adolph Murie went through **1,174** wolf scats for The Wolves of Mount McKinley, [published in the National Park Service's fauna series in 1944](https://npshistory.com/series/fauna/5/index.htm), and found [coarse blades of either sedge or grass in 21 scats](https://npshistory.com/series/fauna/5/chap2.htm). "Grass is eaten at all seasons," he wrote. ["Some of the droppings containing grass also contained several round worms, so seemed to act as a scour."](https://npshistory.com/series/fauna/5/chap2.htm)

He also watched a black male wolf eat grass for a few minutes. The wolf left a watery scat in the road, ["which indicated his stomach was not quite normal,"](https://npshistory.com/series/fauna/5/chap2.htm) and later vomited up some of the grass.

The habit runs across the meat-eaters. A 2020 review in the Belgian Journal of Zoology combed [352 studies](https://belgianjournalofzoology.eu/index.php/BJZ/article/view/73) and found evidence of fibrous plant tissue eaten by 124 species of the Carnivora, and of grass by 95. Nine studies found intestinal parasites alongside grasses or sedges in scats, ["suggesting these abrasive or hairy plant tissues help to expel intestinal parasites."](https://belgianjournalofzoology.eu/index.php/BJZ/article/view/73)

## Chimpanzees offer a parallel for the worm-sweeping idea

Chimpanzees practice leaf swallowing, which a 2025 review in Biological Reviews describes as ["the folding and swallowing, without chewing, of rough, hispid leaves"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12586310/) that pass out as an undigested mass. The same review says the behavior was later linked to the physical expulsion of adult nematodes and tapeworm segments.

Sueda and her co-authors, who trace the worm idea in canids back to Murie, suggest as one possibility that dogs kept, from wild canid ancestors, an innate habit of periodically expelling intestinal parasites. Young coyotes and young dogs are more often infected with intestinal parasites than adults, the authors note, and in the survey [a younger age was significantly associated with](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65de42de7c7033e84bea5ee4) eating plants more often, being less likely to eat mainly grass, and regularly looking ill first or vomiting after less often.

The 2008 paper suggested laboratory studies of whether plant eating reduces intestinal parasites in dogs. In a [2021 study of cats](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8300339/), Hart's group found in two owner surveys that 91 and 94 percent of cats appeared normal before eating plants, though 27 to 37 percent vomited frequently afterwards, and it called the matching worm experiment in cats not feasible. Short-haired cats ate plants as often as long-haired ones, which argues against the hairball explanation, and the authors judged plant eating in cats presumably innate, an instinct inherited from wild ancestors.

> "... if a health maintenance function is eventually demonstrated for plant eating by canids, the behaviour would represent the first documented example of herbal medicine in carnivores." - [Sueda, Hart and Cliff, 2008](https://gwern.net/doc/dog/2008-sueda.pdf)

Aristotle's sick dogs exist, but in the owner surveys they were a small minority. Murie's grass-eating wolf, with its upset stomach, fits Aristotle better than most of those dogs did. Strictly FYI.
