Inside one whale shark caught off eastern Taiwan in 1995 were [**304 embryos**](https://int-res.com/abstracts/esr/v12/n2/p117-124), some still in egg cases and others hatched and swimming free. The female was **10.6 m** long, and her embryos ranged up to what a 2010 paper on the litter calls ["hatched and free-swimming near-term animals."](https://int-res.com/abstracts/esr/v12/n2/p117-124)

Whale sharks had long been thought to lay eggs, on the strength of [a single egg case from the Gulf of Mexico](https://www.int-res.com/articles/esr2010/12/n012p117.pdf) that carried a live 37 cm embryo, reported in 1955. The Taiwan female was [the first pregnant female whale shark](https://www.int-res.com/articles/esr2010/12/n012p117.pdf) to be found, according to that paper. Her litter, its authors write, ["established that whale sharks develop by aplacental yolk-sac viviparity, with embryos hatching from eggs within the female."](https://int-res.com/abstracts/esr/v12/n2/p117-124)

## Do sharks lay eggs?

Some sharks lay eggs, but most do not. Of the [**more than 500 species** of shark](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/do-sharks-lay-eggs.html), the Natural History Museum in London says, the majority give birth to live young. In the most common live-bearing pattern, [used by about 40 percent of shark species](https://extension.msstate.edu/sites/default/files/newsletter/Winter2024-GulfCoastFisherman.pdf), the pup grows on a yolk sac inside the mother and is born free-swimming.

## Three ways sharks are born

SeaWorld's guide to sharks and rays sorts them into [three types of embryonic development](https://seaworld.org/animals/all-about/sharks-and-rays/care-of-young/). The examples below also draw on the Natural History Museum and the papers cited further down:

| Mode | What happens to the egg | Sharks that do it |
|---|---|---|
| Oviparous (egg-laying) | A gland wraps the egg in a case, and the mother leaves the case in the sea | Horn sharks, swell sharks, small-spotted catsharks, zebra sharks |
| Ovoviviparous (live birth without a placenta) | The mother keeps the egg; the embryo sheds a thin membrane and grows in the uterus | Whale sharks, makos, sand tigers |
| Placental viviparity | When the yolk runs out, the yolk sac attaches to the uterine wall and feeds the embryo | Hammerheads, blue sharks, lemon sharks |

In ovoviviparous sharks, SeaWorld says, the shell is often just a thin membrane, and sometimes several eggs share one, a bundle it calls a candle. Sharks are still fish, with skeletons made of cartilage, but the Natural History Museum lists the hammerhead, blue and lemon sharks as species that produce young [in this mammal-like way](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/do-sharks-lay-eggs.html), through a yolk sac that becomes a pseudoplacenta.

## Mermaid's purses come with anchors

The egg-layers' empty cases wash up on beaches, where they are [sometimes called mermaid's purses, and occasionally Devil's purses](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/do-sharks-lay-eggs.html). When a case is first laid, it is [soft and pale, and it hardens and darkens in a few hours](https://seaworld.org/animals/all-about/sharks-and-rays/care-of-young/).

Some shark cases have long tendrils that grip seaweed or a rocky seafloor, which makes the egg [less likely to be washed away](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/do-sharks-lay-eggs.html) by currents. Shark cases generally carry curly tendrils or fibers, while the cases of skates, which are rays, are usually squarer, with horns at the corners.

Horn sharks anchor theirs another way. According to Monterey Bay Aquarium, the females produce [spiral egg cases](https://www.montereybayaquarium.org/animals/animals-a-to-z/horn-shark) and wedge them into crevices, and each pup takes **six to nine months** to hatch. Port Jackson sharks have been seen [carrying their egg cases in their mouths](https://seaworld.org/animals/all-about/sharks-and-rays/care-of-young/), possibly to drop them in a hiding spot, which SeaWorld counts as about the only shark parental care humans have observed.

The Shark Trust's Great Eggcase Hunt began in 2003 after a chance find on a beach in Devon. Its volunteers have since logged [**more than 600,000 eggcases**](https://www.sharktrust.org/great-eggcase-hunt), with records of 49 species from 30 countries.

## In a sand tiger, the first to hatch in each uterus eats the rest

Some live-bearers keep feeding after the yolk runs out. [Shortfin mako](/blog/what-is-the-fastest-fish/) mothers produce unfertilized eggs that the embryos eat, a habit called oophagy, or egg eating, which is [limited to a few species, among them great white sharks](https://extension.msstate.edu/sites/default/files/newsletter/Winter2024-GulfCoastFisherman.pdf).

Sand tigers go a step further. The Florida Museum describes [the two largest embryos that were fertilized first](https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/discover-fish/sharks/shark-biology/) consuming the other embryos of the litter. A sand tiger female has two uteri, and in sharks like this [usually only one embryo survives in each uterus](https://seaworld.org/animals/all-about/sharks-and-rays/care-of-young/).

That cannibalism can also decide which male becomes the father. A 2013 study in Biology Letters, led by Demian Chapman of Stony Brook University, profiled the DNA of **15 sand tiger mothers** and their litters, [from two to nine embryos per litter](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23637391/). The team conservatively estimated that nine of the females, **60 percent**, had mated with more than one male, yet the hatchlings had more than one father in only **40 percent** of litters.

The gap came from [three females that were initially fertilized by multiple males but only produced hatchlings with one of them](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23637391/). Inside a sand tiger, the authors concluded, ["males can be excluded even after fertilizing ova."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23637391/)

## The Taiwan whale shark's tested embryos had one father

Because the litter ran from egg cases to near-term pups, it suggested fertilization over a long stretch, possibly by different males. When Jennifer Schmidt and colleagues genotyped [**29 of the embryos**](https://int-res.com/abstracts/esr/v12/n2/p117-124), about a tenth of the litter and spanning most of its sizes, all were full siblings with the same father.

Young of so many ages from one male led the authors to suggest that ["female whale sharks may have the ability to store sperm for later fertilization."](https://www.int-res.com/articles/esr2010/12/n012p117.pdf) Their own probability analysis allows that a second male siring less than 10 percent of the litter could have gone undetected.

## A zebra shark kept laying after her mate was gone

Zebra sharks lay eggs. At Reef HQ Aquarium in Townsville, Australia, a wild-caught female was put in with a captive male in 1999, and after a separation the pair [began mating in 2006](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep40537). From 2008 she produced several litters of viable young each year until 2013.

After a last mating in 2012 the male was separated from her for good, and [young from that mating came in the 2012/13 season](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep40537). She laid no eggs in 2013/14 and laid again in 2014/15, when every embryo that began to develop died. From eggs laid in the 2015/16 season, three juveniles hatched between February and April 2016.

Stored sperm could have explained it, since sperm storage for up to 45 months has been reported in a related carpet shark, [the paper notes](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep40537). The DNA said otherwise: the embryos and hatchlings from those last two seasons each carried two copies of one of their mother's gene variants at every marker tested, with no sign of a father.

The 2017 study in Scientific Reports, led by Christine Dudgeon of the University of Queensland, called it [the first demonstration of an intra-individual switch from sexual to parthenogenetic reproduction in a shark species](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28091617/). Her daughter, never housed with a mature male, also began reproducing without mating.

> "Elasmobranch fishes may flexibly adapt their reproductive strategy to environmental circumstances." - [Dudgeon and colleagues, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28091617/)

Virgin births in sharks were already known. A decade earlier, a hammerhead, the bonnethead Sphyrna tiburo, gave [the first genetic evidence for chondrichthyan parthenogenesis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17519185/). Virgin birth is now confirmed in both egg-laying and live-bearing sharks.

Most sharks never leave an egg on the seabed, and one that does turned out not to need a male. The zebra shark at Reef HQ was years past her last mating when three of her eggs hatched with no sign of a father in their DNA. Strictly FYI.
