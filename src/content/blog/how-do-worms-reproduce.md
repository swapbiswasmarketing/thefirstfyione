You can tell an adult earthworm from a young one by a swollen band about a third of the way down its body, nearer the head than the tail; the [Earthworm Society of Britain](https://www.earthwormsoc.org.uk/lifecycle) says adults are easily recognized by it. The band is [commonly called the saddle](https://www.earthwormsoc.org.uk/frequently-asked-questions), and its scientific name is the clitellum.

The saddle makes the cocoons that earthworm eggs are laid in, and the eggs are fertilized inside those cocoons, after the two worms have parted, rather than inside the worm.

## How do worms reproduce?

Earthworms are **hermaphrodites**, each with male and female organs, but most species still mate. Two worms lie head to tail and swap sperm, storing it in pouches called spermathecae. Later the saddle, a band toward the front, forms a mucus sheath that slides off over the head, gathering eggs and stored sperm, and becomes **the cocoon where fertilization happens**.

## Both sexes in one body, and still a pair

In a mating, both partners act as male and female at once, which is why the Earthworm Society of Britain calls earthworms **simultaneous hermaphrodites**. The two worms produce a slime tube, grip each other with structures on their saddles called tubercula pubertatis, and exchange sperm. Each stores its partner's sperm "for use later," and then they separate.

Charles Darwin summed up the arrangement in his [1881 book on earthworms](https://archive.org/details/b21901818):

> "The two sexes are united in the same individual, but two individuals pair together." - Charles Darwin, *The Formation of Vegetable Mould, through the Action of Worms*

## Nightcrawlers mate after courting at each other's burrows

The [nightcrawler](https://extension.psu.edu/earthworms), Lumbricus terrestris, has mated on camera, which is more than anyone can say for [European eels](/blog/how-do-eels-reproduce/). Visa Nuutinen and Kevin Butt recorded **15** full mating sequences in the laboratory and [published them in 1997](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7998.1997.tb05826.x).

A worm found a partner by following a trail across the soil surface. The two then visited each other's burrows, a visit usually meaning **30 to 50 seconds** with the front segments pushed inside, and a single worm made anywhere from **1 to 17** visits. Visitors "normally retained their posterior segments in their own burrows."

Then the pair lay belly to belly in an S-shape for **69 to 200 minutes**, against the few seconds of the [cloacal kiss most birds use](/blog/how-do-birds-mate/). Other worms often touched a mating pair, and those matings were shorter than undisturbed ones.

Darwin described the outdoor version. In the pairing season, he wrote, worms from adjoining burrows expose most of their bodies "for an hour or two in the early morning." He judged that "their sexual passion is strong enough to overcome for a time their dread of light."

## The eggs are fertilized after the worms part

Mating and fertilization happen at different times. A mucus sheath forms around the saddle and travels along the body "until it comes off the head end," [the Earthworm Society of Britain explains](https://www.earthwormsoc.org.uk/lifecycle), and "fertilisation of the egg occurs within the cocoon."

Cocoons tend to be lemon-shaped. In the earthworm family Lumbricidae a cocoon holds **1 to 20** eggs depending on the species, though most species put just one egg in each. Hatching time varies with species and conditions; Penn State Extension puts it at **1 to 5 months** under ideal conditions, and some species wait out droughts in the soil as cocoons.

One mating can supply months of cocoons. In a 1998 study, Butt and Nuutinen let nightcrawlers that had never mated meet under video observation, then isolated each worm after it mated. [Mated worms produced cocoons for up to **12 months**](https://doi.org/10.1139/z97-179), while unmated ones produced none. Of the cocoons laid in the first five months after mating, **62 to 76 percent** hatched. That fell to 11 percent for cocoons laid in the sixth month, and cocoons laid after that failed to hatch. Mated worms made a median of **5** viable cocoons each. That fits Penn State's note that, despite having both sets of organs, earthworms typically "still need a partner to reproduce."

## About 40 percent of earthworm species can skip the partner

Some earthworms reproduce without mating at all. The Earthworm Society of Britain calls this parthenogenesis: a single worm producing young from unfertilized eggs. About **40 percent** of earthworm species can reproduce this way, according to a [2025 paper in Molecular Ecology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12329633/) led by Irene de Sosa, though sexual reproduction is still the most common type.

One such species is Aporrectodea trapezoides, found around the world and usually parthenogenetic, although sexual forms of it have been described sporadically. Dissection tells the two forms apart. In a sexual worm, the paper explains, the spermathecae are full of sperm that is iridescent under a stereomicroscope, while in a parthenogen they are "invariably empty."

## Two worms from one cut is mostly a myth

The idea that a halved earthworm grows into two worms does not hold for the common species. Asked exactly that, the Earthworm Society of Britain [answers](https://www.earthwormsoc.org.uk/frequently-asked-questions) "Unfortunately not." A worm cut behind its saddle may regrow a tail, but **the severed tail end dies**, because the brain lies between the head and the saddle.

How much a worm can regrow depends on the species and on where the cut falls. A [2021 review of annelid regeneration](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8392629/) in the journal Genes says the ability varies widely, "sometimes even between closely related species." In Eisenia foetida, the manure or red worm, head regeneration weakens as more front segments are removed and fails past a certain number.

By contrast, juveniles of the earthworm Perionyx excavatus cut through the trunk were "able to regenerate the entire head" in a [2020 laboratory study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7564270/).

## Most earthworms live months, though some have lived 10 years in protection

A hatchling may take **3 to 12 months** to become sexually mature, according to [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/earthworms). Most earthworms live only a few months because of the many threats they face, the guide says, but they have been observed to live for **10 years** in a protected environment.

The saddle that marks an adult earthworm builds the cocoon in which its eggs finally meet a partner's sperm, sometimes months after the two worms parted. Strictly FYI.
