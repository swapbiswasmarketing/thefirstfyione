Roosters that went [14 days without a sunrise](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/130318-rooster-crow-circadian-clock-science) kept up their predawn crowing anyway, on a near-24-hour rhythm that [only slowly weakened](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2013.02.015). They lived in groups of four at Nagoya University, each bird in its own cage, in a light-tight, sound-tight room under [a constant dim glow of less than 0.1 lux](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4512148/), in experiments first reported in Current Biology in 2013 and reanalysed in Scientific Reports in 2015. In each group, the top-ranked rooster almost always crowed first.

The usual explanation for crowing is the sunrise. Those experiments found two things running the dawn call instead: a clock inside each bird, and the pecking order.

## Why do roosters crow?

Roosters crow to claim territory and advertise their status, to rivals and to their own group; crowing is "a warning signal advertising territorial claims," Takashi Yoshimura of Nagoya University told [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/130318-rooster-crow-circadian-clock-science). The call is inherited from the wild red junglefowl, the chicken's ancestor. The dawn timing comes from **an internal circadian clock rather than the sunrise itself**, and in a lab study of roosters housed in one room, the top-ranked bird crowed first almost every morning.

## The first crow comes two hours before the light

In 2013, Tsuyoshi Shimmura and Takashi Yoshimura reported in [Current Biology](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2013.02.015) that roosters kept on 12 hours of light and 12 of dim light began crowing about **two hours before the lights came up**, which the paper notes is consistent with what wild red junglefowl do. Under dim light around the clock [for 14 days](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/130318-rooster-crow-circadian-clock-science), the birds kept crowing when their clocks judged dawn was due, [on a cycle of **23.7 hours**](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2013.02.015), though the rhythm gradually faded over the fortnight.

A rhythm that keeps going once every outside time cue is gone is called free-running, the mark of an internal clock. [The 2015 paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4512148/) reanalysed the same birds by rank and gave a finer figure: predawn crowing began an average of **1.83 hours before the lights came on**.

## The sunrise explanation runs in the wrong order

The popular story has the rising sun waking the rooster and the rooster answering it, a reasonable farmyard guess that gets the first crow wrong. Light can trigger a crow, but the crowing starts before any light arrives and keeps a near-24-hour rhythm when the light never changes.

The sun also works through the clock. The daily cycle of light and dark is "the most effective synchronizer for the circadian clock", in [the 2015 paper's words](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4512148/), so sunlight sets the rooster's clock and the clock sets off the crow.

Outside prompts can start a rooster crowing too, but his clock sets how readily he answers. In the 2013 tests, crowing set off by half an hour of light or a recording of familiar roosters crowing [was observed during early subjective day](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2013.02.015), the start of the birds' internal day, and the same prompts drew no crowing at all in their subjective night. "The crowing was mostly induced in the morning," Yoshimura told [ABC Science](https://www.abc.net.au/science/archive/articles/2013/03/19/3717985.htm). He said the findings suggest the clock regulates a rooster's sensitivity to outside cues as well as when he crows.

## The top rooster crows first and the rest wait

Chickens in small groups form a linear, fixed pecking order, and the 2015 study found it carries over to the dawn call. Across three groups of four inbred roosters, **the top-ranked rooster started the crowing on [97.6% of mornings](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4512148/)**. The others followed **within a few tens of seconds**, in descending order of rank.

With the top bird removed, **the second-ranked rooster crowed first [92.7% of the time](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4512148/)**, though a lower-ranked bird sometimes got in ahead. The authors suggest the new leader was less able to hold the others back.

The subordinates can crow: prompted by light or a recording of crowing, they crowed as much as the leader did. What he held down was their predawn crowing, while each subordinate's clock kept its own time: body-temperature rhythms differed from bird to bird even as the crowing stayed in step.

> The lower-ranked roosters, [the 2015 paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4512148/) concludes, "are patient enough to wait for the top-ranking rooster's first crow every morning and thus compromise their circadian clock for social reasons."

[A 2025 study by Dominic Wright and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12079126/) in Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B notes, citing a 1995 study, that the main indicator of a rooster's rank appears to be "the frequency with which a male crows, not the composition of the actual call".

## The crow is a territory claim kept from the junglefowl

[The same study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12079126/) describes crowing as "the main territorial announcement of the ancestral red junglefowl" and "a reliable indicator of the status, size and health of the emitter". A wild junglefowl group typically holds a small territory around a roosting tree, and the males announce its borders with loud crowing, "emitted mainly during dawn and dusk", [the authors write](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12079126/).

Domestication, [a gradual slide from red junglefowl to chicken](/blog/what-came-first-the-chicken-or-the-egg/), kept the call and edited it. [The study's](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12079126/) domestic White Leghorns had a longer, lower last syllable than red junglefowl recorded in northern India, with more formants, the resonance peaks in a call's sound. Junglefowl kept captive in Sweden for many generations had last syllables as long, though, so the study cannot pin the change on domestication alone.

Deliberate breeding goes further: some of Japan's long-crowing chickens, [the Naganakidori breeds such as Koeyoshi and Tomaru](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4117491/), can hold a single crow for **more than 15 seconds**.

## Testosterone switches the crow on

Crowing is a testosterone-dependent behaviour, and "it follows that roosters crow but not hens," as [a 2019 Nagoya study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408447/) puts it. In that study, male chicks given testosterone implants grew prominent combs and began to crow. "Although both sexes have the capacity to crow, typically only the male does," writes the University of Kentucky's Jacquie Jacob for the [Cooperative Extension poultry site](https://poultry.extension.org/articles/poultry-anatomy/avian-reproductive-female/sex-reversal-in-chickens-kept-in-small-and-backyard-flocks/).

The same team looked for the switch in the nucleus intercollicularis, a midbrain region thought to be the upstream centre for crowing. There, testosterone turned up a gene for a receptor for cholecystokinin, a molecule that works as a neurotransmitter in the brain, and chicks given gastrin, which activates the same receptor, crowed more often than chicks given saline. None of it has to be learned: [the paper notes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408447/) that **a rooster develops the full ability to crow even if he is raised in isolation or surgically deafened from hatching**.

## His ear canal shuts when he crows

Crowing reached **142.3 decibels** at the entrance of the rooster's own ear canal in [a 2018 study in the journal Zoology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29373167/), which [strapped a small recorder around the necks of three roosters](https://repository.uantwerpen.be/docman/irua/305db6/152516_2018_12_27.pdf), its microphone right beside the ear opening. That figure is the peak of one bird's average over his first 11 crows, and his single loudest crow reached 143.8 dB, [the authors' manuscript](https://repository.uantwerpen.be/docman/irua/305db6/152516_2018_12_27.pdf) shows.

| Measurement | Sound level |
|---|---|
| At the entrance of the rooster's own ear canal | 142.3 dB |
| Half a metre from a different rooster | 102 dB |
| Level that can damage inner-ear hair cells almost at once | above 120 dB |

Micro-CT scans of one rooster head and one hen head suggest part of how a rooster keeps his hearing. With the beak opened wide, the rooster's outer ear canal closed completely while the hen's only narrowed, and the authors suggest a bursa-like slit, more developed in roosters, may explain the difference. It is one of several protections, [the authors note](https://repository.uantwerpen.be/docman/irua/305db6/152516_2018_12_27.pdf): opening the beak wide also slackens his eardrum, and after an accidental overload a bird's hearing can recover in about 100 hours. Unlike mammals, birds can also grow new hair cells, though [a 1999 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10516389/) concluded that the quick recovery after loud sound appears to come from repair of the surviving hair cells rather than from new ones.

The rooster on the fence at first light has usually been at it for a while. A clock inside him started the crowing well before the light, the pecking order probably decided whether he went first if he had rivals, and his ear canal may well have shut each time he opened his beak to crow. The sun turned up last. Strictly FYI.
