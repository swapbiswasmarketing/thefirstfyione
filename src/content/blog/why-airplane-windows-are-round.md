Look out the window next time you fly. The pane is a rounded rectangle, soft at every corner, and the story usually told about it is a disaster: the first jet airliner, the de Havilland Comet, had square windows, cracks grew from their sharp corners, and the planes fell apart until engineers rounded them off.

The physics in that story holds up. The history is muddled, and the 1955 inquiry report on the Comet crashes tells a stranger and more useful version.

## Why are airplane windows round?

Airplane windows have rounded corners because any hole in a pressurized fuselage gathers stress at its edge, and [the tighter the corner, the higher that stress climbs](https://www.fracturemechanics.org/ellipse.html). Every flight pumps the cabin up and lets it down again, and a corner that is overstressed on each of those cycles is where a fatigue crack begins.

**Metal fatigue** is [the initiation and propagation of cracks in a material due to cyclic loading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatigue_(material)), and once a crack has started it grows a small amount with each cycle until the part breaks. A cabin skin does not need to be overloaded to fail this way. It needs one spot stressed too high, a thousand or so times.

The British engineer Charles Inglis put numbers on the corner problem. Studying fractured hull plates on ships, he noticed that [rivet holes along the path of a crack were often deformed into an elliptical shape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Inglis_(engineer)), and in 1913 he published the mathematics of stress around an elliptical hole.

His formula, as [a fracture-mechanics teaching site lays it out](https://www.fracturemechanics.org/ellipse.html), reproduces the familiar result that a round hole in a stretched plate triples the stress at its edge, and shows that the stress at the tip of an elliptical hole is "predicted to go to infinity as the ellipse flattens to form a crack." A window corner behaves the same way. A generous radius lets the load bend gradually around the opening, while a tight one forces the whole turn through a small patch of metal. Inglis called [his paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Inglis_(engineer)) "Stresses in a Plate Due to the Presence of Cracks and Sharp Corners."

## Two Comets broke up over the Mediterranean in 1954

The Comet [became the world's first commercial jet airliner when it entered service with BOAC](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co202453/section-of-upper-fuselage-of-comet-g-alyp-showing-fatigue-cracks) (British Overseas Airways Corporation) in May 1952. Less than two years later the type had a problem nobody could explain.

On 10 January 1954, Comet G-ALYP broke up after leaving Rome and fell into the sea near the island of Elba, [killing all 35 people on board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BOAC_Flight_781). On 8 April, Comet G-ALYY, flying a charter for South African Airways, came apart climbing out of Rome toward Cairo, [killing all on board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_African_Airways_Flight_201): a South African crew of seven and fourteen passengers.

## A water tank at Farnborough found the weak corner

To learn whether a Comet cabin could wear out, investigators at the Royal Aircraft Establishment in Farnborough sank a whole fuselage, G-ALYU, in a tank, filled it with water and pumped the pressure up and down to simulate flight after flight. [As the FAA's account quoted by Aerossurance explains](https://aerossurance.com/safety-management/comet-misconceptions/), water is "a much less compressible fluid," so a failure would not tear the cabin apart the way the in-flight break-ups had. G-ALYU had made **1,230 pressurized flights** in service, and after **1,830** more in the tank, 3,060 in all, [its fuselage failed](https://aerossurance.com/safety-management/comet-misconceptions/) at the corner of a squarish forward escape hatch window.

Once the cracked cabin had been repaired, strain gauges fixed near the window corners showed why: with the cabin holding a pressure difference of 8.25 pounds per square inch, the highest stress in the skin near a window corner was probably **over 40,000 pounds per square inch**, [the inquiry report records](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/2022-10/G-ALYP_Report_0.pdf), and the general level of stress in those regions was significantly higher than had been believed.

The design had been aimed at a life of 10,000 pressurization cycles, yet [according to Aerossurance](https://aerossurance.com/safety-management/comet-misconceptions/) G-ALYP had made only 1,290 pressurized flights when it broke up, and G-ALYY had made 900.

## The crack started at a radio aerial window in the roof

The tank showed how a Comet could fail. The wreckage had to show where G-ALYP did. A section of its upper fuselage, now [in the Science Museum Group collection](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co202453/section-of-upper-fuselage-of-comet-g-alyp-showing-fatigue-cracks), is catalogued as showing "fatigue cracks around automatic direction finding hatches," the small windows that held the aerials of the ADF (automatic direction finding) radio.

Lord Cohen judged that the crack that brought down Comet G-ALYP probably began [near the starboard aft corner of the rear ADF window](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/2022-10/G-ALYP_Report_0.pdf), one of two radio aerial windows in the cabin roof, where experts found that fatigue had existed "at the edge of the countersunk hole through which a bolt passed."

> It is my opinion that the fundamental cause of the failure of the cabin structure was that there existed around the corners of the windows and other cut-outs a level of stress higher than is consistent with a long life of the cabin.

That is Lord Cohen in his Court of Inquiry report, [published on 12 February 1955](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/written_answers/1955/feb/14/comet-aircraft-inquiry-report), and his formal answer on the cause was one line: [the structural failure of the pressure cabin brought about by fatigue](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1955/feb/16/comet-aircraft-accidents-report-of). His sentence covers windows and cut-outs in general, and the two failures his investigators could trace began at the corner of a radio aerial window in the roof and at the corner of the window in a forward escape hatch.

## The square passenger window is where the story goes wrong

The shape of the passenger windows [has been commonly misunderstood and cited as a cause of the fuselage failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Havilland_Comet), yet the inquiry "made no observations or recommendations regarding the shape of the windows." Speaking for the government in the Commons, John Boyd-Carpenter summed up [the court's recommendations](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1955/feb/16/comet-aircraft-accidents-report-of) as guarding against fatigue in pressure cabins, research into pressure cabin design and more intensive flight testing, and none of those he named concerned window outlines.

The Comet 1's windows were not sharp squares either. [The same article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Havilland_Comet), citing a video presentation by Paul Withey of the University of Birmingham, notes that the Douglas DC-6 and DC-7 "featured rectangular passenger windows with subtle rounded corners nearly identical to those on the early Comet 1 and 1A, yet experienced no such structural failures."

Window corners still mattered. In 1953, before the Mediterranean crashes, de Havilland pressurized [a test section of the forward cabin](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/2022-10/G-ALYP_Report_0.pdf) about 16,000 times at working pressure until its skin cracked in fatigue at the corner of a window, a result taken as an ample margin of safety. That section had earlier been loaded to as much as twice the working pressure, and the FAA's lessons-learned account, [as Aerossurance quotes it](https://aerossurance.com/safety-management/comet-misconceptions/), says the cold-worked metal at the window corners would "mask the true fatigue vulnerability of the production Comet."

The fix de Havilland offered the court was broader than a new window outline. It proposed [to use thicker gauge materials in the pressure cabin area](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/2022-10/G-ALYP_Report_0.pdf) "and to strengthen and re-design windows and cut-outs, and so lower the general stress" until local stress concentrations at rivets, bolt holes and accidental cracks would not be a danger. The refit that followed used thicker-gauge skin and rounded windows and panels, [the Comet article records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Havilland_Comet), while the escape hatch cut-outs, where G-ALYU failed, kept their rectangular shape.

## Jetliners settled on a slightly larger corner radius

The shape you see from your seat today came with the next generation: [the Boeing 707 and Douglas DC-8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Havilland_Comet) "adopted the rectangular rounded-corner windows common on modern airliners," with a slightly larger corner radius than early designs. The wider lesson is now written into US airliner certification rules, which require that [an evaluation of the strength, detail design, and fabrication](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/25.571) "must show that catastrophic failure due to fatigue, corrosion, manufacturing defects, or accidental damage, will be avoided throughout the operational life of the airplane."

So the rounded window is real engineering wrapped in a slightly wrong legend. The Comet did teach the industry about fatigue around cut-outs, but the hole that failed first was a small aerial window in the roof, and the cure was a cabin skin stressed low enough that no corner, rivet or bolt hole would be a danger. The soft corner you lean against at cruising altitude is the part of that lesson you can see. Strictly FYI.
