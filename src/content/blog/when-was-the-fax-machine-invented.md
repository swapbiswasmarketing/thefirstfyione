"Two machines, one of which may be considered at London, and the other at Portsmouth." That is how Sheet 6, the sixth of seven sheets of drawings in Alexander Bain's [British patent No. 9745](https://archive.org/details/patentsforinvent1766grea/page/79/mode/1up) of **27 May 1843**, introduces a way to copy a page of type from one city to another by electricity. Bain signed the specification six months later, on **27 November 1843**, and [a later collection of telegraph patents](https://archive.org/details/the-leading-telegraph-patents-including-original-and-reissued-patents-of-s.-f.-b.-morse/page/153/mode/1up) reprints it.

[The patent's title](https://archive.org/details/patentsforinvent1766grea/page/79/mode/1up) promises "Certain Improvements in Producing and Regulating Electric Currents, and Improvements in Electric Time-pieces, and in Electric Printing and Signal Telegraphs." The copier comes seventh of [nine improvements claimed](https://archive.org/details/the-leading-telegraph-patents-including-original-and-reissued-patents-of-s.-f.-b.-morse/page/153/mode/1up) for clocks and telegraphs, after a battery of "positive and negative substances placed in the earth": "The arrangement for taking copies of surfaces at distant places by means of electricity, as shewn in Sheet 6." The trick that kept its two pendulums in step, "The method of making pendulums regulate each other," is claimed separately, third on the same list.

## When was the fax machine invented?

The fax machine was invented in **1843**, when the Scottish clockmaker Alexander Bain [patented](https://archive.org/details/patentsforinvent1766grea/page/79/mode/1up) a way to copy a page of type over electric wires. By 1851 a rival copier had been [tested between telegraph stations](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1851_Great_Exhibition:_Official_Catalogue:_Class_X.:_Frederick_Collier_Bakewell), its maker claimed. A [commercial service linked Paris and Lyon](https://www.slhf.org/sites/default/files/publications/slhf12_alexanderbain.pdf) in **1865**, 11 years before the telephone was patented, and fax machines filled offices in the **1980s**.

## Two pendulums copied a page of type in dots

Bain was apprenticed to a clock and watch maker in Wick in **1830** and moved to London in **1837**, according to a [2012 study](https://www.slhf.org/sites/default/files/publications/slhf12_alexanderbain.pdf) by the physicist Ivan Ruddock, who notes that the copier "was part of Bain's 1843 patent."

At the sending end, [the specification](https://archive.org/details/the-leading-telegraph-patents-including-original-and-reissued-patents-of-s.-f.-b.-morse/page/153/mode/1up) has the message set in metal printer's type. The type pressed against a frame packed with short insulated wires, each cut "into lengths of about an inch." A pendulum, "continually kept in motion" by clockwork, swept a steel spring across the other ends, so current flowed only through wires touching type.

At the receiving end, a matching pendulum swept a matching frame pressed against, [in the patent's words](https://archive.org/details/the-leading-telegraph-patents-including-original-and-reissued-patents-of-s.-f.-b.-morse/page/153/mode/1up), "two thicknesses of damp paper previously saturated with a solution composed of equal parts of prusiate of potash and nitrate of soda." Current crossing the paper marked it, "which will produce a copy of the printing surface of the types in a series of small dots." At the end of each swing, clockwork let both frames drop a step. Bain added that "a copy of any other surface composed of conducting and non-conducting materials, can be taken by these means."

## The Electric Printing Telegraph was a different machine in the same patent

A [FaxBurner blog post](https://faxburner.com/blog/when-and-who-invented-the-fax-machine-a-brief-history-of-faxing/) gives the copier a name the patent gives a different machine: "He called it the Electric Printing Telegraph," it says of Bain's 1843 invention. That phrase in the patent's title belongs to its printing telegraph, which, in the [official abridgment's summary](https://archive.org/details/patentsforinvent1766grea/page/79/mode/1up), moved a **type wheel** up to the paper and used "a blackened ribbon" to make the characters visible.

In **April 1844** the [Mechanics' Magazine](https://archive.org/details/mechanicsmagazi19unkngoog) headlined a working machine "Mr. Bain's Electric Printing Telegraph." It was shown at Nine Elms station in London, sending signals to and from Wimbledon, six miles off, and "every message (indicated by numbers) was transmitted and returned correctly." It printed from types set round the rim of a wheel and drew its current from metal plates buried in the ground or sunk in water, the battery of the patent's first claim.

The 1843 specification reports no trial of the copier, and [Ruddock judges](https://www.slhf.org/sites/default/files/publications/slhf12_alexanderbain.pdf) that "The idea was probably not pursued by Bain." The later record shows he came back to it.

He took out a [US patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5957A/en) for an "Improvement in copying surfaces by electricity," dated **5 December 1848**, that sets out the 1843 copier again, down to "a method of making two pendulums at a distance regulate each other so as to keep the same time." The [official catalogue](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1851_Great_Exhibition:_Official_Catalogue:_Class_X.:_Alexander_Bain) of the Great Exhibition of **1851** listed his "Patent electro-chemical copying telegraph, said to be capable of copying any figure, such as profiles, autographs, stenography, etc."

At the exhibition, [Ruddock writes](https://www.slhf.org/sites/default/files/publications/slhf12_alexanderbain.pdf), Bain was advertising that his copier could now send handwriting and diagrams "and not just raised typeface as in the 1843 patent." The [Class X jury](https://archive.org/details/reportsbyjurieso00grea/page/290/mode/1up) gave him a Council Medal for a different exhibit, an electro-chemical telegraph that sent messages punched as holes and slits in a paper band.

## Bakewell put the message on a turning cylinder

Frederick Collier Bakewell's [patent No. 12,352](https://archive.org/details/patentsforinvent1766grea/page/169/mode/1up), dated **2 December 1848**, describes a "copying telegraph" whose cylinders turned in step while an insulated iron point, carried along by a screw, ran over each. The message was written in varnish on tin foil, so it could be **handwriting**.

His [1851 catalogue entry](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/1851_Great_Exhibition:_Official_Catalogue:_Class_X.:_Frederick_Collier_Bakewell) offered "facsimiles of the handwriting of correspondents, so that their signatures may be identified," the copy "appearing of a pale colour on a ground of closely-drawn blue lines." Pendulums only kept time: on a single wire each instrument had one, and both were "regulated at every beat of the pendulums." It was "not yet in operation," the entry admitted, though tested between stations of the Electric Telegraph Company. The same [jury](https://archive.org/details/reportsbyjurieso00grea/page/290/mode/1up) gave Bakewell a Council Medal for it.

From 1848 to 1850, Jonathan Coopersmith writes in [Faxed](https://archive.org/details/faxedrisefalloff0000coop) (2015), Bain and Bakewell traded public letters "accusing each other of theft of intellectual property, dishonesty, incompetence, and betrayals of trust."

## Paris and Lyon traded faxes 11 years before the telephone patent

The machine that reached paying customers was the pantelegraph of the Abbe Giovanni Caselli. In the engineer Robert Sabine's [history of the telegraph](https://archive.org/details/historyandprogr02sabigoog), in its 1869 edition, "a long and heavy pendulum" rocks two styluses, so one side of the machine can send while the other receives: the outgoing message is written in insulating ink on metallic paper, and the receiving stylus marks damp, chemically treated paper with lines of "Prussian blue."

Sabine dates the invention to 1856, credits Froment, of Paris, with "overcoming the mechanical difficulties" eight years later, and [reports](https://archive.org/details/historyandprogr02sabigoog) that "In 1865 two of these instruments were set up at work between Paris and Lyons." Coopersmith, who glosses pantelegraph as "all-telegraph," calls the Paris-Lyon line [the world's first fax service](https://archive.org/details/faxedrisefalloff0000coop) and records that in 1866, **4,853 of 4,860** Caselli transmissions between Lyon and Paris "involved finance or business."

It was slow: by [Sabine's arithmetic](https://archive.org/details/historyandprogr02sabigoog), a sheet 111 by 27 millimetres holding about twenty-five written words took **1 minute 48 seconds**.

> "Copying telegraphs have never reached a considerable status as an employable system," Robert Sabine wrote, partly because "occasions requiring the production of fac-similes present themselves very rarely."

Alexander Graham Bell's [US patent 174,465](https://patents.google.com/patent/US174465A/en) for the telephone, titled "Improvement in Telegraphy," was granted on **7 March 1876**, nearly **33 years** after Bain's British patent.

## The office fax took another century

Xerox's Magnafax Telecopier worked through an ordinary telephone: with a phone handset laid in its coupler, a letter-size page could be "transmitted in six minutes to any spot in the nation where there is an ordinary telephone and a counterpart Telecopier," [Computers and Automation](https://archive.org/details/bitsavers_computersA_8245117) reported in July 1966. In [Coopersmith's account](https://archive.org/details/faxedrisefalloff0000coop), Xerox and Magnavox announced the Telecopier on 11 November 1965, the 46-pound machine was delivered in **July 1966**, and the Telecopier "marked the start of successful mass production of fax machines."

The boom waited for the early **1980s**. [Coopersmith traces it](https://archive.org/details/faxedrisefalloff0000coop) to "the combination of increasing deregulation, true compatibility, quickly dropping costs, greater competition, and rapid technological change" from that point.

Which machine counts as the first fax depends on what it had to copy:

| Date | Milestone | What it copied |
|---|---|---|
| 27 May 1843 | Bain's British patent | Printer's type, as dots |
| 2 Dec. 1848 | Bakewell's British patent | Handwriting in varnish on tin foil |
| 1865 | Caselli's Paris-Lyon service | Writing in insulating ink |
| July 1966 | Xerox Magnafax Telecopier delivered | A letter-size page by telephone, in six minutes |

Paris was sending handwriting to Lyon by wire 11 years before Bell patented the telephone, and the idea began as the seventh of nine improvements claimed for clocks and telegraphs in a patent that opens with batteries buried in the earth. Strictly FYI.
