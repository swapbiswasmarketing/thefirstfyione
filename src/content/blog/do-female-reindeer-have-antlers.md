The sled reindeer of Scandinavia and Finland were traditionally castrated bulls, and unlike full males they kept their antlers through the winter. Before snowmobiles, transport reindeer "were used to pull the sleds," says a [2022 study of reindeer castration](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9734228/), which adds that "castrated reindeer and sometimes sterile does were used as working animals" and that in Fennoscandia they are "usually castrated between 3 and 4 years of age." A caption in [the same study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9734228/), under a 1962 photograph of castrated reindeer with a herder on skis, puts it plainly: "Castrated reindeer retain their antlers in the winter, unlike full males."

Those working bulls break a chain of Christmas logic that starts from a true fact: female reindeer have antlers, the males drop theirs before Christmas, so Santa's reindeer must all be female. The US Food and Drug Administration's page of [reindeer fun facts](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/animal-health-literacy/fun-facts-about-reindeer-and-caribou) applies it to Rudolph, who "is most likely a female to have those antlers on Christmas Eve!"

## Do female reindeer have antlers?

Yes: reindeer, or caribou, are **[the only member of the deer family](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/index.cfm?adfg=caribou.main) in which both sexes normally grow antlers**, though a cow's are much shorter than a bull's. A pregnant cow usually [keeps hers until she calves](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/index.cfm?adfg=caribou.main) in **late May or early June**, months after the largest bulls drop theirs, and some cows [have none at all](https://septentrio.uit.no/index.php/rangifer/article/view/662).

## A gene and a hormone let reindeer cows grow antlers

The rest of the deer family follows the usual pattern: "in most other deer species, only the males have antlers," the [FDA's fact page](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/animal-health-literacy/fun-facts-about-reindeer-and-caribou) notes. The [same page](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/animal-health-literacy/fun-facts-about-reindeer-and-caribou) sizes the gap within reindeer, up to **51 inches** long for a bull's antlers and up to **20 inches** for a cow's, and says that in both sexes antlers "fall off and grow back larger each year." Androgens, the male sex hormones, "play an essential role in the antler formation of cervids," [Wikipedia's reindeer article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reindeer) explains, much as testosterone [switches on a rooster's crow](/blog/why-do-roosters-crow/).

Two findings help explain the reindeer exception. A [2019 genome study in Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aav6312) found a mutation in the DNA ahead of a gene called CCND1 that gives the androgen receptor, the protein that responds to male hormones, an extra place to bind, a change that "may result in female antlers." In the cow herself, the ovarian hormone that shapes the antler cycle is estradiol: studying reindeer kept outdoors in northern Norway, [Lincoln and Tyler](https://doi.org/10.1530/jrf.0.1150167) concluded that it spurs the antler bone to grow and harden, strips the velvet and helps hold off the casting of the hard antlers.

## In some herds, most cows have no antlers

Across four caribou herds in west Greenland, **[the share of adult cows with no antlers ran from 21% to 79%](https://septentrio.uit.no/index.php/rangifer/article/view/662)**, Henning Thing, Carsten Riis Olesen and Peter Aastrup reported in the journal *Rangifer* in 1986, and the poorer a herd's winter range, the more cows went without. Going without was not all loss: in one herd, ["antlerless cows showed higher reproductive rate than antlered ones."](https://septentrio.uit.no/index.php/rangifer/article/view/662) Yet calves of antlerless mothers were more prone to disease, and [42% of them died over the summer, against 27% of other calves](https://septentrio.uit.no/index.php/rangifer/article/view/662).

In Newfoundland and Labrador, James Schaefer and Shane Mahoney compared 15 herds for [a 2001 paper in Ecology](https://doi.org/10.1890/0012-9658%282001%29082%5B3556:AOFCBO%5D2.0.CO;2) and found more antlered cows where more snow fell and where it lay deeper at the end of March, and fewer where herds were denser. Their results, [they wrote](https://doi.org/10.1890/0012-9658%282001%29082%5B3556:AOFCBO%5D2.0.CO;2), support the idea that a cow's antlers help her compete for winter food, though fewer cows may carry them where herds are dense and poorly fed.

## Cows keep their antlers through the hungry months

In winter, reindeer reach lichen and fungi by [scraping the snow away with their hooves](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/animal-health-literacy/fun-facts-about-reindeer-and-caribou). When the adult bulls lose their antlers in early to midwinter, they also lose their rank in the herd's feeding hierarchy, and **antlered cows move to the top**: they "acquire highest ranks, thereby getting access to the best forage patches," as [Thing and his colleagues](https://septentrio.uit.no/index.php/rangifer/article/download/662/629/2512) summed up the earlier studies.

Lincoln and Tyler tied the timing to pregnancy: the hormone keeps a cow's antlers on "throughout the autumn and winter when the females are normally pregnant and when competition between females over food in the snow is most intense," [they wrote](https://doi.org/10.1530/jrf.0.1150167). [Thing's paper](https://septentrio.uit.no/index.php/rangifer/article/download/662/629/2512), citing earlier work by Espmark, adds a proposed second use: cows that still have antlers at calving are "capable of defending their calves against predators." The castrated bulls have a winter job too, as the [castration study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9734228/) describes it: they are "strong enough to break through the ice cover and permit the smaller animals to graze."

## Antlers on Christmas Eve rule out the adult bulls

The Christmas argument uses half of the antler calendar. The [Alaska Department of Fish and Game](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/index.cfm?adfg=caribou.main) puts both halves in one sentence of its caribou profile:

> "The largest bulls shed their antlers in late October, but small bulls and non-pregnant cows do not shed their antlers until April." - Alaska Department of Fish and Game

The agency [adds](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/index.cfm?adfg=caribou.main) that "identifying those seasons varies between herds and caribou biologists," and its [Santa's reindeer page](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/index.cfm?adfg=santasreindeer.main) applies the same calendar to "most subspecies of reindeer/caribou." With the pregnant cows, which usually keep their antlers until calving, and the castrated sled bulls of Lapland, where Finnish tradition [gives Santa a home](/blog/where-does-santa-live/), the antler calendar on Christmas Eve looks like this:

| Reindeer | Antlers drop | Antlers on Christmas Eve? |
|---|---|---|
| Adult bulls | Late fall to midwinter; the largest in late October | Mostly no |
| Small bulls | April | Yes |
| Cows that are not pregnant | April | Yes |
| Pregnant cows | At calving, late May or early June | Yes |
| Castrated bulls | Not in winter | Yes |

The stories leave the question open. The poem that [named Santa's eight](/blog/what-are-the-names-of-santas-reindeer/) never calls any of them male or female.

Rudolph, the ninth, is a he: Robert L. May's [original story](https://archive.org/details/rudolphrednosedr00mayr) says of him, "What else could he do?" May's Rudolph is also "one little deer," and small bulls, [by Alaska's count](https://www.adfg.alaska.gov/index.cfm?adfg=caribou.main), keep their antlers until April.

So a team with antlers on Christmas Eve could be cows, small bulls, castrated bulls or any mix of them. What the antlers rule out is the adult bulls, which, the [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/articles/caribou-did-you-know.htm) says, "shed their antlers after the rut in late fall." The poem never asked for those: its [first printing](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Troy_Sentinel/1823/12/23/Account_of_a_Visit_from_St._Nicholas) gave St. Nicholas "a miniature sleigh, and eight tiny rein-deer."
