Say the names of Santa's reindeer out loud and the list ends on a pair that runs together like one phrase: Donner and Blitzen. In German, [Donner](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Donner) is thunder and a [Blitz](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Blitz) is a bolt of lightning, and "donner and blitzen!" was an oath in English-language fiction before Santa's reindeer had names. Walter Scott's smuggler Dirk Hatteraick swears it more than once in [*Guy Mannering*](https://archive.org/details/guymanneringoras02scot), published in **1815**.

The poem that named Santa's team called that pair Dunder and Blixem when it [first appeared, in **1823**](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Troy_Sentinel/1823/12/23/Account_of_a_Visit_from_St._Nicholas).

## What are the names of Santa's reindeer?

Santa's reindeer are Dasher, Dancer, Prancer, Vixen, Comet, Cupid, Donner and Blitzen, the **eight**, in today's spelling, that St. Nicholas calls by name in ["A Visit from St. Nicholas,"](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Troy_Sentinel/1823/12/23/Account_of_a_Visit_from_St._Nicholas) the poem that begins "'Twas the night before Christmas." Rudolph, created for [a department store's holiday promotion in **1939**](https://www.npr.org/2013/12/25/256579598/writing-rudolph-the-original-red-nosed-manuscript), later joined them as the ninth. In the order the poem calls them:

1. Dasher
2. Dancer
3. Prancer
4. Vixen
5. Comet
6. Cupid
7. Donner
8. Blitzen

## The first six names are plain English

Six of the names read the same in 1823 as they do now, and they need no translation. The poem itself echoes two of them in its [1823 text](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Troy_Sentinel/1823/12/23/Account_of_a_Visit_from_St._Nicholas): St. Nicholas shouts "dash away! dash away! dash away all!" and the narrator hears "The prancing and pawing of each little hoof." A [vixen](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/vixen) is a female fox, and [Cupid](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Cupid) is the Roman god of love. In the poem, though, Vixen is only a name: it never calls any of the eight male or female.

## The 1823 roll call ended on a Dutch oath

Two years before "A Visit from St. Nicholas," Santa's sleigh had appeared with a single reindeer, unnamed, in "Old Santeclaus with Much Delight," an [1821 New York children's poem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Claus%27s_reindeer). The 1823 poem gave him eight and named every one.

The poem appeared unsigned in the [*Troy Sentinel*](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Troy_Sentinel/1823/12/23/Account_of_a_Visit_from_St._Nicholas) of Troy, New York, on **December 23, 1823**. Its editor introduced it with "We know not to whom we are indebted" and called its hero "Sante Claus." The roll call ran:

> "Now! Dasher, now! Dancer, now! Prancer, and Vixen, / On! Comet, on! Cupid, on! Dunder and Blixem;" - *Troy Sentinel*, December 23, 1823

Dunder and Blixem stand for two Dutch words, [donder](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/donder) for thunder and [bliksem](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/bliksem) for lightning. Blixem was not an English respelling but an older Dutch one: a Leeuwarden newspaper of [March 6, 1804](https://archive.org/details/ddd_010172309_mpeg21) reported heavy weather "van Donder en Blixem," of thunder and lightning, at Antwerp. Together they made an oath that was already in print in New York: in Washington Irving's comic [*History of New York*](https://archive.org/details/ahistorynewyork00conggoog) of **1809**, the Dutch side in a battle with the Swedes swears "Dunder and blixum!"

## Reprints respelled the pair, and Moore printed Donder and Blitzen

The newspapers and anthologies that reprinted the poem did not all keep its spelling. The *New England Galaxy* of Boston had "Dunder and Blixen" by [January 9, 1829](https://archive.org/details/sim_boston-pearl-and-galaxy_1829-01-09_12_587), and *The New-York Book of Poetry*, an [1837 anthology](https://archive.org/details/newyorkbkpoet00fennrich) that credited the poem to Clement C. Moore, printed "Donder and Blixen."

Moore printed the poem in his own collection, [*Poems*](https://archive.org/details/poemsmoor00moorrich/page/125), in **1844**, and that printing says "Donder and Blitzen." Donder is the standard Dutch spelling of the word for thunder. [Blitzen](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Blitzen) is a German form of Blitz, lightning (its dative plural), so Moore's pair is half Dutch and half German.

Edgar Allan Poe had already put that mix into an oath: the little old gentlemen of a Dutch borough in "The Devil in the Belfry" swear "Donder und Blitzen!" in his [*Tales of the Grotesque and Arabesque*](https://archive.org/details/talesofgrotesque01poee), copyrighted in 1839. *Harper's Weekly* kept Moore's spelling when it printed the poem under his name on [December 29, 1866](https://archive.org/details/sim_harpers-weekly_1866-12-29_10_522).

## Baum gave his Santa ten reindeer

Not every writer used the poem's team. In [*The Life and Adventures of Santa Claus*](https://archive.org/details/lifeadventuresof0baum), a 1902 novel by L. Frank Baum, Claus drives ten reindeer: Glossie and Flossie, and then "Racer and Pacer, Reckless and Speckless, Fearless and Peerless, and Ready and Steady."

## Rudolph joined in 1939, from a department store

Rudolph is nowhere in the poem. In **1939**, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2013/12/25/256579598/writing-rudolph-the-original-red-nosed-manuscript), a manager at Montgomery Ward in Chicago decided the store should make its own children's book for its holiday promotion, and the job went to Robert L. May, one of its ad men.

The store printed and distributed [more than **2 million** copies](https://www.npr.org/2013/12/25/256579598/writing-rudolph-the-original-red-nosed-manuscript) that year. May considered other names before settling on Rudolph, among them Reginald, Rollo and Romeo, according to NPR's account of his manuscript at Dartmouth.

After World War II, NPR's story continues, the store's chief executive, Sewell Avery, gave May the rights to Rudolph. The store's annual holiday booklet giveaway was discontinued in **1947**, according to Autry's biographer Holly George-Warren, quoted on the [official Gene Autry site](https://www.geneautry.com/clubhouse/christmas/geneautry_rudolphsong.php), and May's brother-in-law, the songwriter Johnny Marks, wrote a song about Rudolph that Autry recorded on [June 27, 1949](https://www.geneautry.com/clubhouse/christmas/geneautry_rudolphsong.php). In the first issue of *Billboard* for 1950, dated January 7, the record was No. 1 on the [pop best-seller chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Billboard_number-one_singles_of_1950) and on the [country disk-jockey chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Billboard_number-one_country_songs_of_1950).

The song's [opening verse](https://www.geneautry.com/clubhouse/christmas/geneautry_rudolphsong.php) is where Rudolph joins the poem's eight. It runs "You know Dasher and Dancer and Prancer and Vixen / Comet and Cupid and Donner and Blitzen" and then asks "But do you recall the most famous reindeer of all?" The [1964 Rankin/Bass television special](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolph_the_Red-Nosed_Reindeer_(TV_special)) went further and made Rudolph the son of Donner.

## Donner reached print decades before Rudolph

One explanation credits Rudolph with fixing the modern spelling. The usage site [Grammarist](https://grammarist.com/spelling/donner-or-donder/) says the name "was put firmly in place as Donner when Rudolph was penned in a song in 1939."

May's Rudolph was a story in verse, and the song came ten years after it. The story does say Donner, though: Santa calls "Come Comet! Come Cupid! Come Donner and Blitzen!" in [May's text](https://archive.org/details/rudolphrednosedr00mayr).

The spelling is far older than May's story. When Susan Fenimore Cooper quoted "Mr. Moore" on Santa's sleigh in [*Rural Hours*](https://archive.org/details/ruralhours00coop/page/436), her **1850** book, she gave the pair as "Donner and Blixen."

Later the *Huntsville Gazette* of Alabama printed the poem with "Donner and Blitzen" on [December 26, 1891](https://archive.org/details/sim_huntsville-gazette_1891-12-26_13_4). The American Book Company's [*New McGuffey Third Reader*](https://archive.org/details/newmcguffeythir00mcgugoog), copyrighted in **1901**, printed the line as "On, Comet! on, Cupid! on, Donner and Blitzen!" Here is a sample of printings, in date order:

| Year | Text | The closing pair |
|---|---|---|
| 1823 | *Troy Sentinel*, unsigned | Dunder and Blixem |
| 1829 | *New England Galaxy* | Dunder and Blixen |
| 1837 | *The New-York Book of Poetry* | Donder and Blixen |
| 1844 | Moore's *Poems* | Donder and Blitzen |
| 1850 | Susan Fenimore Cooper's *Rural Hours* | Donner and Blixen |
| 1866 | *Harper's Weekly* | Donder and Blitzen |
| 1891 | *Huntsville Gazette*, Alabama | Donner and Blitzen |
| 1901 | *New McGuffey Third Reader* | Donner and Blitzen |
| 1939 | Robert L. May's Rudolph verse | Donner and Blitzen |
| 1949 | Johnny Marks's song, recorded by Gene Autry | Donner and Blitzen |

The pair at the end has been printed at least six ways. From Dunder and Blixem to Donner and Blitzen, the last two of Santa's reindeer have meant thunder and lightning. Strictly FYI.
