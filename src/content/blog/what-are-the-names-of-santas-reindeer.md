Say the names of Santa's reindeer out loud and the list ends on a pair that runs together like one phrase: Donner and Blitzen. In German, [Donner](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Donner) is thunder and a [Blitz](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Blitz) is a bolt of lightning, and "donner and blitzen!" was an oath in English-language fiction before Santa's reindeer had names. Walter Scott's smuggler Dirk Hatteraick swears it more than once in [*Guy Mannering*](https://archive.org/details/guymanneringoras02scot), published in **1815**.

The poem that named Santa's team called that pair Dunder and Blixem when it first appeared, in **1823**.

## What are the names of Santa's reindeer?

Santa's reindeer are Dasher, Dancer, Prancer, Vixen, Comet, Cupid, Donner and Blitzen, the **eight** that St. Nicholas calls by name in "A Visit from St. Nicholas," the poem that begins "'Twas the night before Christmas." Rudolph, created for a department store's Christmas giveaway in **1939**, later joined them as the ninth. In the order the poem calls them:

1. Dasher
2. Dancer
3. Prancer
4. Vixen
5. Comet
6. Cupid
7. Donner
8. Blitzen

## The first six names are plain English

Six of the names read the same in 1823 as they do now, and they need no translation. The poem itself echoes two of them in its [1823 text](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Troy_Sentinel/1823/12/23/Account_of_a_Visit_from_St._Nicholas): St. Nicholas shouts "dash away! dash away! dash away all!" and the narrator hears "The prancing and pawing of each little hoof." A [vixen](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/vixen) is a female fox, and [Cupid](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Cupid) is the Roman god of love. In the poem, though, Vixen is only a name: it never calls any of the eight male or female, and even antlers could not settle the question, since reindeer [cows grow them as well as bulls](/blog/do-female-reindeer-have-antlers/).

## The 1823 roll call ended on a Dutch oath

Two years before "A Visit from St. Nicholas," Santa's sleigh had appeared with a single reindeer, unnamed, in "Old Santeclaus with Much Delight," an [1821 New York children's poem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Claus%27s_reindeer). The 1823 poem gave him eight and named every one.

The poem appeared unsigned in the [*Troy Sentinel*](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Troy_Sentinel/1823/12/23/Account_of_a_Visit_from_St._Nicholas) of Troy, New York, on **December 23, 1823**. Its editor introduced it with "We know not to whom we are indebted" and called its hero "Sante Claus." The roll call ran:

> "Now! Dasher, now! Dancer, now! Prancer, and Vixen, / On! Comet, on! Cupid, on! Dunder and Blixem;" - *Troy Sentinel*, December 23, 1823

Dunder and Blixem are English spellings of two Dutch words, [donder](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/donder) for thunder and [bliksem](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/bliksem) for lightning. Together they made an oath that was already in print in New York: in Washington Irving's comic [*History of New York*](https://archive.org/details/ahistorynewyork00conggoog) of **1809**, the Dutch side in a battle with the Swedes swears "Dunder and blixum!"

New Yorkers respelled Dutch by ear elsewhere too, though not always with the meaning intact: a salt marsh recorded as Smee's Vlie became [Smith's Fly](/blog/why-is-it-called-a-flea-market/), keeping the sound and losing the sense.

## Reprints respelled the pair, and Moore printed Donder and Blitzen

The newspapers and anthologies that reprinted the poem did not all keep its spelling. The *New England Galaxy* of Boston had "Dunder and Blixen" by [January 9, 1829](https://archive.org/details/sim_boston-pearl-and-galaxy_1829-01-09_12_587), and *The New-York Book of Poetry*, an [1837 anthology](https://archive.org/details/newyorkbkpoet00fennrich) that credited the poem to Clement C. Moore, printed "Donder and Blixen."

Moore printed the poem in his own collection, [*Poems*](https://archive.org/details/poemsmoor00moorrich/page/125), in **1844**, and that printing says "Donder and Blitzen." Donder is the standard Dutch spelling of the word for thunder. [Blitzen](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Blitzen) is a German form of Blitz, lightning, so Moore's pair is half Dutch and half German.

Edgar Allan Poe had already put that mix into an oath: the little old gentlemen of a Dutch borough in "The Devil in the Belfry" swear "Donder und Blitzen!" in his [*Tales of the Grotesque and Arabesque*](https://archive.org/details/talesofgrotesque01poee), copyrighted in 1839. *Harper's Weekly* kept Moore's spelling when it printed the poem under his name on [December 29, 1866](https://archive.org/details/sim_harpers-weekly_1866-12-29_10_522), in the same issue in which Thomas Nast lettered [Santa's North Pole town](/blog/where-does-santa-live/).

## Baum gave his Santa ten reindeer

Not every writer used the poem's team. In [*The Life and Adventures of Santa Claus*](https://archive.org/details/lifeadventuresof0baum), a 1902 novel by L. Frank Baum, Claus drives ten reindeer: Glossie and Flossie, and then "Racer and Pacer, Reckless and Speckless, Fearless and Peerless, and Ready and Steady."

## Rudolph joined in 1939, from a department store

Rudolph is nowhere in the poem. In **1939**, [NPR reported](https://www.npr.org/2013/12/25/256579598/writing-rudolph-the-original-red-nosed-manuscript), a manager at Montgomery Ward in Chicago decided the store should make its own children's book for its holiday promotion, and the job went to Robert L. May, one of its ad men.

The store printed and gave away [more than **2 million** copies](https://www.npr.org/2013/12/25/256579598/writing-rudolph-the-original-red-nosed-manuscript) that year. May considered other names before settling on Rudolph, among them Reginald, Rollo and Romeo, according to [NPR's account](https://www.npr.org/2013/12/25/256579598/writing-rudolph-the-original-red-nosed-manuscript) of his manuscript at Dartmouth.

After World War II, [NPR's story](https://www.npr.org/2013/12/25/256579598/writing-rudolph-the-original-red-nosed-manuscript) continues, the store's chief executive, Sewell Avery, gave May the rights to Rudolph. Montgomery Ward stopped the giveaway in **1947**, says the [official Gene Autry site](https://www.geneautry.com/clubhouse/christmas/geneautry_rudolphsong.php), and May's brother-in-law, the songwriter Johnny Marks, wrote a song about Rudolph that Autry recorded on [June 27, 1949](https://www.geneautry.com/clubhouse/christmas/geneautry_rudolphsong.php). In the first issue of *Billboard* for 1950, dated January 7, the record was No. 1 on the [pop best-seller chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Billboard_number-one_singles_of_1950) and on the [country disk-jockey chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Billboard_number-one_country_songs_of_1950).

The song's [opening verse](https://www.geneautry.com/clubhouse/christmas/geneautry_rudolphsong.php) is where Rudolph joins the poem's eight: "You know Dasher and Dancer and Prancer and Vixen, / Comet and Cupid and Donner and Blitzen," it runs, before asking "But do you recall the most famous reindeer of all?" The [1964 Rankin/Bass television special](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolph_the_Red-Nosed_Reindeer_(TV_special)) went further and made Rudolph the son of Donner.

## Donner reached print decades before Rudolph

One explanation credits Rudolph with the modern spelling. The usage site [Grammarist](https://grammarist.com/spelling/donner-or-donder/) says the name "was put firmly in place as Donner when Rudolph was penned in a song in 1939."

May's Rudolph was a story in verse, and the song came ten years after it. The story does say Donner, though: Santa calls "Come Comet! Come Cupid! Come Donner and Blitzen!" in [May's text](https://archive.org/details/rudolphrednosedr00mayr).

The spelling is older than May's story: the *Huntsville Gazette* of Alabama printed the poem with "Donner and Blitzen" on [December 26, 1891](https://archive.org/details/sim_huntsville-gazette_1891-12-26_13_4). The American Book Company's [*New McGuffey Third Reader*](https://archive.org/details/newmcguffeythir00mcgugoog), copyrighted in **1901**, printed the line as "On, Comet! on, Cupid! on, Donner and Blitzen!" Here is a sample of printings, in date order:

| Year | Text | The closing pair |
|---|---|---|
| 1823 | *Troy Sentinel*, unsigned | Dunder and Blixem |
| 1829 | *New England Galaxy* | Dunder and Blixen |
| 1837 | *The New-York Book of Poetry* | Donder and Blixen |
| 1844 | Moore's *Poems* | Donder and Blitzen |
| 1866 | *Harper's Weekly* | Donder and Blitzen |
| 1891 | *Huntsville Gazette*, Alabama | Donner and Blitzen |
| 1901 | *New McGuffey Third Reader* | Donner and Blitzen |
| 1939 | Robert L. May's Rudolph verse | Donner and Blitzen |
| 1949 | Johnny Marks's song, recorded by Gene Autry | Donner and Blitzen |

The pair at the end has been printed at least five ways. From Dunder and Blixem to Donner and Blitzen, the last two of Santa's reindeer have meant thunder and lightning. Strictly FYI.
