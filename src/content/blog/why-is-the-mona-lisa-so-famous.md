A painter set up his easel in the Louvre's Salon Carre to paint the room. Where the Mona Lisa should have hung, the wall held only **four bare hooks**, and at first he was not alarmed. A project was under way to photograph the museum's works, each one carried up to the roof because the cameras of the day did not work well indoors.

So he persuaded a guard to go and ask how long the photographers would keep the picture. Tom Hoobler, who co-wrote a book on the theft, [told NPR in 2011](https://www.npr.org/2011/07/30/138800110/the-theft-that-made-the-mona-lisa-a-masterpiece) what the guard said when he came back: "You know what, the photographers say they don't have it!"

That was [at around noon on Tuesday](https://www.history.com/articles/the-heist-that-made-the-mona-lisa-famous), a day after the Mona Lisa had been lifted off the wall while the museum was closed. The museum called the police, and that evening it told the world.

## Critics made it a masterpiece, and a theft made it a household name

The Mona Lisa's fame came in two stages that arrived centuries apart. Leonardo da Vinci's contemporaries [regarded the portrait as a masterpiece](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/why-i-think-mona-lisa-became-an-icon/164983.article), and by the 19th century writers had turned its smile into a mystery. What made it known to people who never read art criticism was its theft from the Louvre on **21 August 1911**, a hunt that lasted more than two years, and its recovery in Italy.

As [the Louvre puts it](https://www.louvre.fr/en/explore/the-palace/from-the-mona-lisa-to-the-wedding-feast-at-cana), "So the Mona Lisa was recovered - and her fame was all the greater." The historian Donald Sassoon, who wrote a book on the painting's reputation, put the sequence this way:

> "To bring the painting to the attention of the new popular press, a startling event was required. As if on cue, it was stolen in August 1911, obtaining the required worldwide coverage." - [Donald Sassoon, Times Higher Education, 2001](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/why-i-think-mona-lisa-became-an-icon/164983.article)

## Centuries of admiration came first

The sitter, the Louvre says, is [Lisa Gherardini, wife of the Florentine silk merchant Francesco del Giocondo](https://www.louvre.fr/en/explore/the-palace/from-the-mona-lisa-to-the-wedding-feast-at-cana), hence her Italian name La Gioconda and her French name La Joconde. King Francis I invited Leonardo to France, and the museum's catalogue says the king very probably acquired the painting in **1518**; it dates the work to [1503 / 1519](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark:/53355/cl010062370).

Giorgio Vasari, the 16th-century biographer of Italian artists, [never saw the painting](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark:/53355/cl010062370), the Louvre's catalogue notes, but he wrote that Leonardo, ["after toiling over it for four years, he left it unfinished"](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/28420), and that he kept musicians and jesters on hand to keep the sitter merry while he worked. The result, Vasari wrote, was "a smile so pleasing, that it was a thing more divine than human to behold."

The 19th century added the mystery. Walter Pater's essay on Leonardo, dated 1869, says of her that ["She is older than the rocks among which she sits; like the vampire, she has been dead many times"](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2398), and in the same passage writes of "the unfathomable smile" that "plays over all Leonardo's work."

That reputation stayed among the educated. James Zug, writing in Smithsonian magazine, notes that it was [not until the 1860s that art critics claimed the Mona Lisa was one of the finest examples of Renaissance painting](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/stolen-how-the-mona-lisa-became-the-worlds-most-famous-painting-16406234/), and that Baedeker's guide to Paris gave it a paragraph in 1878 and only two sentences in 1907.

The London Spectator, reporting the theft that week in 1911, still ranked it ["one of the half-dozen most famous pictures in the world"](https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/26th-august-1911/3/on-monday-leonardos-mona-lisa-commonly-known-in-fr), and wrote that its smile "has bewitched and puzzled generations." It was famous already, as one celebrated picture among several.

## The theft's headlines turned high art into mass art

Once the Louvre announced the theft, newspapers around the world ran banner headlines. The New York Times of August 24, 1911, ran the headline ["60 Detectives Seek Stolen 'Mona Lisa,' French Public Indignant."](https://www.npr.org/2011/07/30/138800110/the-theft-that-made-the-mona-lisa-a-masterpiece) The Spectator [worried that a thief](https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/26th-august-1911/3/on-monday-leonardos-mona-lisa-commonly-known-in-fr) who could never sell so well-known a picture might panic and destroy it.

When the museum reopened a week later, [thousands of people came to gawk at the empty wall](https://www.history.com/articles/the-heist-that-made-the-mona-lisa-famous) where the painting had hung. The poet Guillaume Apollinaire was [arrested in September 1911](https://www.history.com/articles/the-heist-that-made-the-mona-lisa-famous) over an earlier theft of statuettes from the Louvre, and his friend Pablo Picasso was questioned; both were later cleared of any part in the Mona Lisa theft.

Zug counts Franz Kafka among the crowds, with his travelling companion Max Brod, and lists what followed: [satirical postcards, a short film and cabaret songs](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/stolen-how-the-mona-lisa-became-the-worlds-most-famous-painting-16406234/). In his words, "popular culture seized upon the theft and turned high art into mass art."

## The thief had helped put it under glass

The man jailed for stealing the Mona Lisa was one of the workmen the Louvre had hired to protect it. After a letter from Vienna threatened the painting in 1910, Zug writes, the museum hired the glazier firm Cobier to put a dozen of its more prized paintings under glass, and [one of the Cobier men assigned to the project was Vincenzo Perugia](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/stolen-how-the-mona-lisa-became-the-worlds-most-famous-painting-16406234/) (the name is also spelled Peruggia).

He hid the painting in the false bottom of a trunk in his Paris lodgings, and the police who questioned him never treated him as a serious suspect. [In December 1913](https://www.history.com/articles/the-heist-that-made-the-mona-lisa-famous) he took it to Florence and offered it to the art dealer Alfredo Geri, who brought in Giovanni Poggi, director of the Uffizi Gallery.

A stamp on the back confirmed it was the original, and the police were at Peruggia's door [half an hour later](https://www.npr.org/2011/07/30/138800110/the-theft-that-made-the-mona-lisa-a-masterpiece), Hoobler told NPR. He [said later that he was a patriot](https://www.npr.org/2011/07/30/138800110/the-theft-that-made-the-mona-lisa-a-masterpiece) returning a painting Napoleon had stolen from Italy, but it had been in the French royal collection since Francis I, long before Napoleon was born.

## Her eyes do not follow you, and the smile was already old news

The usual answer to the question is in the painting itself. Sassoon recalls that his mother told him, before his first visit, that ["her eyes follow you everywhere and her smile is enigmatic"](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/why-i-think-mona-lisa-became-an-icon/164983.article).

The eyes claim has been measured. In a 2019 study at Bielefeld University, Gernot Horstmann and Sebastian Loth had twenty-four participants judge where she looks, using a ruler set between them and a screen. They found [a line of gaze with an angle of **15.4 degrees** to the viewer's right](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6327345/) and concluded: "Mona Lisa does not gaze at the viewer." The effect named after her is real in portraits that do look straight out, the authors add: "it just does not occur with Mona Lisa herself."

The smile is real, but it cannot explain the timing: Vasari praised it in the 16th century, and the Spectator was still praising it in the week of the theft, before anyone knew where the picture had gone. Sassoon's own conclusion is that ["a complex historical process, not a single cause"](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/why-i-think-mona-lisa-became-an-icon/164983.article) built its status, with the theft as the event that carried it out of the art world.

## The crowds kept coming after it came home

The painting went on show in Florence, Milan and Rome before it went back to Paris, and in its first two days back in the Salon Carre, Zug writes, [**more than 100,000 people** viewed it](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/stolen-how-the-mona-lisa-became-the-worlds-most-famous-painting-16406234/).

In 1963 it crossed the Atlantic on loan. It drew [more than a half million visitors](https://www.nga.gov/research/national-gallery-art-archives/highlights-history) in a 26-day display at the National Gallery of Art in Washington, and [**over one million**](https://www.metmuseum.org/perspectives/today-in-met-history-february-4) in about three and a half weeks at the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York.

The Met notes that before that loan the painting had [left French soil only once](https://www.metmuseum.org/perspectives/today-in-met-history-february-4), when Peruggia took it to Italy. On the first morning it was on show in New York, the Met records, the first person in the queue was [a taxi driver from Brooklyn who got up at 4:30 a.m.](https://www.metmuseum.org/perspectives/today-in-met-history-february-4)

Back in Paris, the Louvre moved it in 1966 to its largest room, the Salle des Etats, a space [big enough to welcome its many admirers](https://www.louvre.fr/en/explore/the-palace/from-the-mona-lisa-to-the-wedding-feast-at-cana), and since 2005 it has sat there in a protective glass case.

Leonardo's portrait had centuries of admirers behind it, and it took a glazier with a false-bottomed trunk to make it the painting a Brooklyn taxi driver would get up at 4:30 a.m. to see. Strictly FYI.
