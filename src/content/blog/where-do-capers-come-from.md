A caper flower opens at dusk and is spent by the next day, a [2023 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10108209/) in the *American Journal of Botany* reports: one large white bloom, **one night**. Every caper in a jar is a bud picked by hand before that night came.

## Where do capers come from?

Capers come from the caper bush, *Capparis spinosa*, a shrub of dry climates whose range runs from the Mediterranean south into Africa and east across Asia. Each caper is an unopened flower bud, picked by hand and cured in salt, brine or vinegar. A bud left to flower and set fruit becomes a caperberry.

Whether the bush is native to the Mediterranean is unsettled. The [University of Wisconsin's extension page](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/capers-capparis-spinosa/) says it is not clear whether it is native "or just naturalized," and the most popular hypotheses, the 2023 paper notes, make it a relic of an ancient dry-tropical flora, probably from Africa or western Asia.

Purdue University's 1998 [NewCROP factsheet](https://hort.purdue.edu/newcrop/cropfactsheets/caper.html) lists Europe's sources as Spain, Provence in France, and Italy, "especially Sicily and the Aeolian island of Salina and the Mediterranean island of Pantelleria." Pantelleria's capers, preserved in sea salt, were registered as a geographical indication in Italy in **1993** and EU-wide in June **1996**, [WIPO Magazine](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/wipo-magazine/articles/gi-protection-revives-caper-production-on-pantelleria-42042) reports.

## The flower opens at dusk and lasts one night

An open caper flower has four white to pinkish-white petals and a spray of long violet stamens. Its scent, white petals and nighttime opening fit night-flying hawkmoths, the pollinators the 2023 authors predicted.

A motion-activated camera then recorded three bushes on the Greek island of Lesvos over six nights in 2014. Most visits came from honeybees, which took pollen without touching the stigma, so the authors class them as pollen thieves. Carpenter bees, which could brush the stigma as they fed, visited **eight times** more often than hawkmoths, at dusk and the following morning.

## Pickers take each bud by hand, and the smallest pay best

Because the buds are small and delicate, "they have to be harvested individually by hand, instead of by machine, each morning just as they reach the proper size," the Wisconsin page says. In southern Italy, buds are collected by hand "about every 8 to 12 days," Purdue's factsheet says, for **9 to 12** harvests a season.

> "Harvesting labor is what makes capers expensive," the University of Wisconsin's extension page says.

Size sets the value. In Italy capers are graded from **7 to 16**, their size in millimeters, and Purdue's factsheet says buds under a centimeter across are worth more than the larger grades. Other kitchen crops share the bottleneck: Ceylon cinnamon is [peeled from the stem by hand](/blog/how-is-cinnamon-made/), and US [sesame](/blog/where-do-sesame-seeds-come-from/) could not be harvested without hand labor until 1982.

## The bite is the bud's own mustard oil

The caper family sits in the plant order Brassicales with cabbage and mustard, and the caper and mustard families "have long been known to be closely related families," as a [2002 study](https://doi.org/10.3732/ajb.89.11.1826) of their chloroplast DNA put it.

Like those relatives, the caper bush makes compounds called glucosinolates. In intact plant cells an enzyme, myrosinase, is kept apart from them, the [Linus Pauling Institute](https://lpi.oregonstate.edu/mic/dietary-factors/phytochemicals/isothiocyanates) at Oregon State University explains. When the cells are damaged, the two meet and form isothiocyanates, the mustard oils behind a pungent aroma and spicy taste.

In raw caper buds the main glucosinolate is glucocapparin, about **90%** of the total in a [2002 analysis](https://doi.org/10.1021/jf020530+) of wild capers from Turkey. Its mustard oil is methyl isothiocyanate, which Purdue's factsheet credits with the caper's strong flavor.

When food scientists in Turkey analyzed the aroma of fresh buds for a [2019 study](https://doi.org/10.1111/1750-3841.14777) in the *Journal of Food Science*, methyl isothiocyanate was the most potent of the **12** aroma-active compounds they found, with a flavor dilution factor of 512.

## Curing cut aroma compounds by two-thirds in one study

In the traditional method, as a [2022 paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9740950/) from the University of Messina describes it, fresh buds are mixed with sea salt, **30 to 40%** of their weight, for about 8 to 10 days while a brine forms and fermentation occurs. The brine is then poured off and the buds are salted again for 20 to 30 days; other capers are pickled in vinegar instead.

In the 2019 study, fermentation cut the total amount of aroma compounds in the buds by **66%**, from 62,616 to 21,471 micrograms per kilogram, and total phenolic compounds by 78%. In the fermented buds the most potent aroma was acetic acid, the acid in vinegar. That is a single study of one fermentation.

## The usual explanation mixes up two molecules

Popular accounts credit the cure with the bite. "Intense flavor is developed as mustard oil (glucocapparin) is released from each bud," the Wisconsin page says, and Wikipedia's caper article likewise credits the cure. Glucocapparin is the stored precursor, though, and the mustard oil is methyl isothiocyanate, which forms when the bud's tissue is damaged.

The cure does matter: the Messina paper says fermentation develops the volatile compounds behind the caper's "unique sensory features." It builds much of a cured caper's aroma, while the precursor of the bite is already in the fresh bud.

Sources also split over how the raw bud tastes. Purdue's factsheet says "Raw capers are bland flavored and need to be cured to develop their piquant flavor." The Messina team brined fresh buds "until the capers' bitter taste was neutralized," and in the 2019 measurements the mustard oil was the most potent aroma in the fresh buds.

## Rabbi Akiba held that only the caper berries are fruit

A [Gotham Bagels blog post](https://www.gothambagels.com/blog/what-are-capers) calls capers, "technically speaking," a fruit. Botanically a caper is a flower bud. The fruit is the caperberry, and [Tablet](https://www.tabletmag.com/sections/food/articles/long-history-caper-mishna-bagels-western-wall) notes that mature fruit "are less desirable, as they contain hard seeds."

| Part of the bush | What it is | How it is eaten |
|---|---|---|
| Caper | Unopened flower bud | Cured in salt, brine or vinegar |
| Caperberry | Immature fruit | Pickled, eaten like pickles or olives |
| Ripe fruit | Mature fruit with hard seeds | Less prized because of the seeds |
| Young shoots | Stems with small leaves | Eaten as a vegetable or pickled |

The question is old. In the Mishnah, the first major work of rabbinic literature, compiled around **200 CE**, Rabbi Eliezer said tithes were due on the caper bush's shoots, berries and blossoms. Rabbi Akiba disagreed: "only the berries are tithed since they [alone] count as fruit," in Joshua Kulp's [translation](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishnah_Maasrot.4.6).

The Mishnah's Hebrew word for the berries, aviyonot, appears in the singular in [Ecclesiastes 12:5](https://biblehub.com/ecclesiastes/12-5.htm), where the King James Bible reads "desire shall fail" and the New American Standard Bible "the caper berry is ineffective."

By Rabbi Akiba's rule, a jar of capers holds no fruit and owes no tithe. Every bud in it was picked in the morning, before its flower could open at dusk and set a berry. Strictly FYI.
