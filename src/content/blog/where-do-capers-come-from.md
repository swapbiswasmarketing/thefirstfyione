A caper flower opens at dusk and is spent by the next day, a [2023 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10108209/) in the *American Journal of Botany* reports: one large white bloom, **one night**. Every caper in a jar is a bud picked by hand before that night came.

## Where do capers come from?

Capers come from the caper bush, *Capparis spinosa*: each one is an unopened flower bud, picked by hand and then brined, packed in salt or pickled, as the [University of Wisconsin's extension page](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/capers-capparis-spinosa/) describes. A bud left on the bush opens into a flower and can then set fruit, the caperberry.

The species ranges from the Mediterranean south into sub-Saharan Africa and east across Asia to Oceania, and the most popular hypotheses, [the 2023 paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10108209/) says, make it a relic of an ancient dry-tropical flora, probably from Africa or western Asia. Whether it is native to the Mediterranean itself is unsettled: [the Wisconsin page](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/capers-capparis-spinosa/) says it is not clear whether the bush is native there "or just naturalized."

Purdue University's 1998 [NewCROP factsheet](https://hort.purdue.edu/newcrop/CropFactSheets/caper.html) lists Europe's sources as Spain, Provence in France, and Italy, "especially Sicily and the Aeolian island of Salina and the Mediterranean island of Pantelleria." Pantelleria's capers, preserved in sea salt, were registered as a geographical indication in Italy in **1993** and EU-wide in June **1996**, the head of a caper firm on the island told [WIPO Magazine](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/wipo-magazine/articles/gi-protection-revives-caper-production-on-pantelleria-42042).

## The flower opens at dusk and lasts one night

An open caper flower has four white petals and a spray of long stamens, the outer half of each one pink-magenta. Night-flying hawkmoths were "a rather straightforward prediction," [the study's authors write](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10108209/), given the brush-like flowers, nighttime opening, strong scent and abundant nectar. The white petals, they add, cannot be tied to hawkmoths alone, because white flowers of this kind also suit the vision of bees.

A motion-activated camera filmed three bushes in turn on the Greek island of Lesvos over [six nights in 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10108209/), and carpenter bees, which could brush the stigma as they fed, visited **eight times** more often than hawkmoths, at dusk and the following morning. Most visits came from honeybees, which took pollen without touching the stigma, so the authors class them as pollen thieves.

## Pickers take each bud by hand, and the smallest pay best

Because the buds are small and delicate, the Wisconsin page explains, they are picked one at a time by hand rather than by machine, just as they reach the proper size. In southern Italy, pickers go back over the bushes "about every 8 to 12 days," [Purdue's factsheet](https://hort.purdue.edu/newcrop/CropFactSheets/caper.html) says, for **9 to 12** harvest rounds a season.

> "Harvesting labor is what makes capers expensive," [the University of Wisconsin's extension page](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/capers-capparis-spinosa/) says.

Size sets the value. In Italy capers are graded from **7 to 16**, their size in millimeters, and [Purdue's factsheet](https://hort.purdue.edu/newcrop/CropFactSheets/caper.html) says buds under a centimeter across are worth more than the larger grades.

## The bite is the bud's own mustard oil

The caper family sits in the plant order [Brassicales](https://www.mobot.org/MOBOT/research/APweb/orders/brassicalesweb.htm) with cabbage and mustard, and the caper and mustard families "have long been known to be closely related families," as a [2002 study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21665611/) of their chloroplast DNA put it. The same study found the caper family, as then drawn, was not one natural group: its spider-flower branch sat closer to the mustards than to the capers.

Like those relatives, the caper bush makes compounds called glucosinolates. In intact plant cells an enzyme, myrosinase, is kept apart from them, the [Linus Pauling Institute](https://lpi.oregonstate.edu/mic/dietary-factors/phytochemicals/isothiocyanates) at Oregon State University explains. When the cells are damaged, the two meet, and the breakdown products, isothiocyanates among them, "impart a pungent aroma and spicy (some say bitter) taste."

In a [2002 analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12452652/) of young shoots and raw buds from two caper species growing wild in Turkey, the main glucosinolate was glucocapparin, about **90%** of the total. Its mustard oil is methyl isothiocyanate, which Purdue's factsheet credits with the caper's strong flavor, a taste it likens to mustard and black pepper.

When food scientists in Turkey analyzed the aroma of fresh buds for a [2019 study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31476250/) in the *Journal of Food Science*, methyl isothiocyanate was the most potent of the **twelve** aroma-active compounds they found, with a flavor dilution factor of 512.

## Curing cut aroma compounds by two-thirds in one study

In the traditional method, as a [2022 paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9740950/) from the University of Messina describes it, fresh buds are mixed with sea salt, **30 to 40%** of their weight, for about 8 to 10 days while a brine forms and fermentation occurs. The brine is then poured off and the buds are salted again for 20 to 30 days. Other capers are preserved in vinegar instead, Purdue's factsheet notes.

In the [2019 study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31476250/), fermentation cut the total amount of aroma compounds in the buds by **66%**, from 62,616 to 21,471 micrograms per kilogram, and total phenolic compounds by 78%. In the fermented buds the most potent aroma was acetic acid, the acid in vinegar. That is a single study.

## Crediting the cure mixes up two molecules

The Wisconsin page credits the cure with the bite. "Intense flavor is developed as mustard oil (glucocapparin) is released from each bud," [the page says](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/capers-capparis-spinosa/). Glucocapparin is the stored precursor, though, and the mustard oil is methyl isothiocyanate, which forms when the bud's tissue is damaged.

The cure does matter: the [Messina paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9740950/) says the dry-salting fermentation develops the volatile compounds behind the caper's "unique sensory features." It shapes much of a cured caper's aroma, while the precursor of the bite is already in the fresh bud.

Sources also split over how the raw bud tastes. "Raw capers are bland flavored and need to be cured to develop their piquant flavor," [Purdue's factsheet](https://hort.purdue.edu/newcrop/CropFactSheets/caper.html) says. The Messina team brined fresh buds "until the capers' bitter taste was neutralized," [their paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9740950/) reports, and in the [2019 measurements](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31476250/) the mustard oil was the most potent aroma in the fresh buds.

## Rabbi Akiba held that only the caper berries are fruit

A [Gotham Bagels blog post](https://www.gothambagels.com/blog/what-are-capers) calls capers, "technically speaking," a fruit. Botanically a caper is a flower bud. The fruit is the caperberry, and [Tablet](https://www.tabletmag.com/sections/food/articles/long-history-caper-mishna-bagels-western-wall) notes that mature fruit "are less desirable, as they contain hard seeds."

| Part of the bush | What it is | How it is eaten |
|---|---|---|
| Caper | Unopened flower bud | Cured in salt, brine or vinegar |
| Caperberry | Immature fruit | Pickled, eaten like pickles or olives |
| Ripe fruit | Mature fruit with hard seeds | Less prized because of the seeds |
| Young shoots | Stems with small leaves | Eaten as a vegetable or pickled |

The question is old. The Mishnah, whose redaction the [Encyclopaedia Judaica](https://www.jewishvirtuallibrary.org/mishnah) places "in the early third century," records a debate over it. Rabbi Eliezer said tithes were due on the caper bush's shoots, berries and blossoms. Rabbi Akiba disagreed: "only the berries are tithed since they [alone] count as fruit," in Joshua Kulp's [translation](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishnah_Maasrot.4.6).

The Mishnah's Hebrew word for the berries, aviyonot, appears in the singular in [Ecclesiastes 12:5](https://biblehub.com/ecclesiastes/12-5.htm), where the King James Bible reads "desire shall fail" and the New American Standard Bible "the caper berry is ineffective." Bible Hub's lexicon notes that the word "appears only once in Scripture," and [Strong's concordance](https://biblehub.com/hebrew/35.htm) glosses it "provocative of desire; the caper berry (from its stimulative taste)," a definition that holds both English readings.

By Rabbi Akiba's rule, a jar of capers holds no fruit. Every bud in it was picked before its flower could open at dusk and set a berry. Strictly FYI.
