Homer Smith found out whether sea fish drink by dyeing their water. In a paper published in **1930**, the New York University physiologist used the marker phenol red on American eels kept in his own laboratory and on sculpins at the Mount Desert Island Biological Laboratory in Maine, and showed that [both the sculpin and the eel ingested seawater](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3059943/), as the biologist David H. Evans tells it in a 2010 history of the field.

The dye was concentrated in the gut, a sign that the intestine was absorbing much of the water. When Smith tied off the outlet of the stomach, no dye reached the intestine, which settled, Evans writes, that it had entered the fish ["via the mouth, not by diffusion across the gills."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3059943/) Sea fish drink the sea.

## Do fish get thirsty?

**Not the way people do, as far as physiologists can tell.** A sea fish drinks, swallowing seawater and pumping the extra salt out through its gills, but underwater that drinking is a swallowing reflex, thought to start in the hindbrain, not a thirst that sends it looking for water. A freshwater fish barely drinks, because water soaks in through its gills.

## A sea fish loses water to the sea around it

A bony fish, or teleost, keeps its blood much less salty than the ocean. In a table Yoshio Takei compiled for the eel, seawater measures [**1029 mOsm/l** and eel plasma **377**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8215749/), little more than a third as concentrated. Water is drawn out of the fish through its gills and the lining of its mouth, and Smith, describing the first fishes to move from fresh water into the sea, wrote in his book From Fish to Philosopher that without a way to offset that loss the animal ["would die of exsiccation just as certainly as though it were being slowly dried in the air and sun."](https://archive.org/details/B-001-014-326)

So it drinks. The seawater is desalted and diluted along the gut, and marine bony fish [can absorb **70-85%**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8215749/) of the seawater they swallow across the intestine, Takei writes. In mammals the magnesium and sulfate in seawater are scarcely absorbed and severe diarrhea follows, and because [human kidneys can only make urine that is less salty than salt water](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/drinksw.html), still more water is lost getting rid of the salt.

The leftover sodium and chloride go out through the gills. In 1931 Ancel Keys, working in August Krogh's laboratory in Copenhagen, showed that a perfused eel gill could [secrete chloride against the chemical gradient](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3059943/), and in 1932, with his co-author Willmer, he described what they termed "chloride-secreting cells" in the gills of the eel. Most of the swallowed magnesium and sulfate is never absorbed and stays in the gut; the share that does reach the blood leaves by the kidneys.

## A freshwater fish has the opposite problem

In a river or lake the gradient runs the other way. The fish's blood is saltier than the water, so water seeps in through the gills and mouth, and the job is to get rid of it.

Freshwater fish do it by making urine and by hardly drinking. Smith noted that in fresh water ["the only water to enter the gastrointestinal tract is apparently accidental"](https://archive.org/details/B-001-014-326) for the eel, whose urine flow there ranges **up to 150 cc per kg per day**, very dilute, against **generally less than 5 cc** in salt water.

He called those eel figures roughly typical of all the fresh- and salt-water fishes that had been studied, and added that goldfish may swallow some water while feeding on microscopic food. The salt a freshwater fish loses has to be won back, and Krogh, Keys's postdoctoral adviser, proposed that freshwater fishes [extract needed NaCl from the environment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3059943/) through ion exchange at the gills.

|---|---|---|
| Water moves | Out, across the gills | In, across the gills |
| Drinking | Swallows seawater | Hardly at all |
| Eel urine, per kg a day | Usually under 5 cc | Up to 150 cc, very dilute |
| Salt at the gills | Pumped out | Taken up |

## An eel with no forebrain still drinks

Thirst, as physiologists define it, is ["a conscious sensation of a need for water and a desire to drink"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6102947/), and in mammals it is generated in the forebrain and sends the animal looking for water. A fish in the sea has nothing to look for. Its water is already in its mouth.

For drinking set off by seawater, the eel's forebrain does not even seem to take part. Eels with the whole forebrain removed still drank when they were moved into seawater, a 1972 study found, according to [a 2018 review by Yukitoshi Katayama, Takei and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6102947/). The review proposes that such stimuli act on the hindbrain to start a swallowing reflex, and it placed the main action of angiotensin II, the hormone that makes mammals thirsty, at a hindbrain site called the area postrema.

A 2022 study by Nobata and Takei revised the hormone's part. Of three hormones injected into the blood of conscious eels, [only isotocin induced copious drinking](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35502793/), while angiotensin II was followed by a sustained inhibition of drinking, and destroying the area postrema abolished the drinking that isotocin set off. Angiotensin II still raised drinking when injected into the brain, and the authors suggest it acts there at a different site, possibly a structure without a blood-brain barrier at the front of the third ventricle, as in birds and mammals.

> "Aquatic teleosts can drink water that is constantly present in their mouth only by reflex swallowing." - [Katayama and colleagues, Frontiers in Physiology, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6102947/)

Even the reflex has a rhythm. Takei reports that eels in seawater [do not drink constantly but rather drink in a rhythmic pattern](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8215749/), rising and falling at intervals of **15 to 30 minutes**, which he reads as a full stomach holding back further drinking until the sphincter at its exit relaxes.

## The mudskipper climbs out, and goes back to drink

Mudskippers are gobies that spend much of their lives out of water, and they are the exception. On land, swallowing empties the mouth and appears to dry it, and angiotensin II then [motivates these fishes to move to water for drinking](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6102947/), which Katayama and colleagues call a unique thirst regulation by sensory detection in the mouth.

## Eels and salmon switch strategies

Some fish run both systems in one lifetime. Smith counted the killifish, the stickleback and the common eel among fish that [can tolerate rapid transfer from fresh to salt water](https://archive.org/details/B-001-014-326), and noted that the Atlantic salmon spawns in fresh water but grows to maturity in the sea. Eels grow up in rivers and estuaries and then [return to the open ocean to spawn](/blog/how-do-eels-reproduce/), and the eel that drank almost nothing in fresh water starts swallowing seawater once it reaches salt water.

## Sharks hold enough urea to draw water in

Sharks and rays dodge most of the problem. Smith described their blood as holding [upwards of **2.0 per cent** urea](https://archive.org/details/B-001-014-326) against none in seawater, kept by the kidneys at a level that will "draw just sufficient water from sea water to meet the animal's needs."

## Sea fish turn the calcium and magnesium they swallow into carbonate

Drinking the sea leaves a residue. The bicarbonate a marine fish secretes into its gut [helps precipitate carbonates of divalent ions](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8215749/), the magnesium and calcium in the swallowed seawater, and that lowers the gut fluid's concentration so more water can be absorbed. A 2009 study in Science estimated that marine fish [contribute **3 to 15%** of total oceanic carbonate production](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19150840/) this way, excreting the precipitates at high rates.

A fish in the sea drinks all its life without ever going looking for water. The fish in this story that does go looking is the mudskipper, and it has to climb out of the water first. Strictly FYI.
