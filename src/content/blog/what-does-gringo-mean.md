American soldiers in the Mexican-American War are supposed to have sung "Green Grow the Lilacs", or "Green Grow the Rushes, O", until the Mexicans who heard them turned the opening words of the title into a name for the singers, as [the popular story](https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/gringo/) goes. In the other common version, a regiment wore green coats and the Mexicans answered them with "green go home", a tale [Vocabulary.com](https://www.vocabulary.com/articles/wordroutes/the-colorful-origin-stories-of-gringo/) files with the folk etymologies.

As usually told, both versions need the word to be born during that war. A [dictionary published in Madrid](https://archive.org/details/diccionario-castellano-con-las-voces-de-ciencias-y-artes-vol-3/Diccionario%20castellano%20%20con%20las%20voces%20de%20ciencias%20y%20artes%20vol2/page/n0/mode/1up) already defined it **59 years before the war began**, and in Madrid the name went particularly to the Irish.

## What does gringo mean?

**Gringo** is a colloquial Spanish word for a foreigner, especially an English speaker. In Mexico it means first of all someone from the **United States**, according to the [Diccionario del espanol de Mexico](https://dem.colmex.mx/Ver/gringo). The [Real Academia Espanola](https://dle.rae.es/gringo) records that sense in a dozen other Latin American countries and notes that the word is **sometimes used disparagingly**. In Brazilian Portuguese, the [Michaelis](https://michaelis.uol.com.br/moderno-portugues/busca/portugues-brasileiro/gringo/) dictionary defines a gringo as someone who is not native to the country where they live. The word predates the Mexican-American War: a [Madrid dictionary](https://archive.org/details/diccionario-castellano-con-las-voces-de-ciencias-y-artes-vol-3/Diccionario%20castellano%20%20con%20las%20voces%20de%20ciencias%20y%20artes%20vol2/page/n0/mode/1up) had it by **1787**.

## The word was in a Madrid dictionary by 1787

The second volume of Esteban de Terreros y Pando's *Diccionario castellano* has MDCCLXXXVII, **1787**, on its [title page](https://archive.org/details/diccionario-castellano-con-las-voces-de-ciencias-y-artes-vol-3/Diccionario%20castellano%20%20con%20las%20voces%20de%20ciencias%20y%20artes%20vol2/page/n0/mode/1up). The entry for GRINGOS is on [page 240](https://archive.org/details/diccionario-castellano-con-las-voces-de-ciencias-y-artes-vol-3/Diccionario%20castellano%20%20con%20las%20voces%20de%20ciencias%20y%20artes%20vol2/page/n241/mode/1up). Translated, it reads:

> "Gringos is what they call foreigners in Malaga who have a certain kind of accent, which keeps them from speaking easy, natural Castilian; and in Madrid they give the same name, for the same reason, particularly to the Irish." - Terreros y Pando, *Diccionario castellano*, 1787

The Spanish for that last clause is *con particularidad a los Irlandeses*. The definition turns on an accent, and the word was already current in two cities when Terreros wrote it.

Terreros never saw the dictionary published, and he wrote the entry years before its title page was set. Printing began in 1765. When the Crown expelled his order, the Jesuits, in 1767, volume one was finished except for its prologue and about half of volume two had been printed, according to the [Biblioteca Virtual de la Filologia Espanola](https://www.bvfe.es/es/autor/10757-terreros-y-pando-esteban-de-s-i.html). He died in exile in Forli in 1782. Two librarians of the Reales Estudios in Madrid later found all his slips, numbered and in order, and the Count of Floridablanca ordered the work printed: volume one appeared in 1786 and volume two in 1787.

## Green Grow the Lilacs cannot explain a word in print by 1787

The war both stories depend on ran from **1846 to 1848**, according to the [National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/treaty-of-guadalupe-hidalgo). A word already sitting in a Madrid dictionary cannot have been coined by soldiers in Mexico during that war.

The [Online Etymology Dictionary](https://www.etymonline.com/word/gringo) finds it in English before the war too, **by 1841**, in a Chilean context, in reference to an Englishman. It also notes an **1831** English-language novel by Telesforo de Trueba y Cosio with a character called "Mr. O'Gringo, an Irishman," a name that echoes Madrid's usage.

The song story is much younger than the word. A version printed in 1883 by the Los Angeles Times and the Arizona Weekly Citizen had nothing to do with the war: drunken sailors from an English man-of-war paraded the streets of Mazatlan, one of them singing "Green Grow the rushes," and the Mexicans "only caught the first two words," according to [Vocabulary.com's Word Routes column](https://www.vocabulary.com/articles/wordroutes/the-colorful-origin-stories-of-gringo/). The war version that "really stuck" was the one the journalist Nellie Bly sent home from Mexico, in a report American newspapers ran widely in 1886, [the same column](https://www.vocabulary.com/articles/wordroutes/the-colorful-origin-stories-of-gringo/) says. In her 1888 book [Six Months in Mexico](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/49314) she passed it on as hearsay, "They say the way the title originated was this": the ballad was popular then, and "all the American soldiers were singing it."

Mexican writers knew the sailor version as well. Joaquin Garcia Icazbalceta's 1899 [Vocabulario de mexicanismos](https://archive.org/details/vocabulariodeme01icazgoog) reprints it from the Mexico City paper El Tiempo of August 3, 1893, allows that the anecdote might be true, and insists that gringo was not born from it.

Etymonline's [verdict on the song](https://www.etymonline.com/word/gringo) is just as flat: the claim of "a Mexican mishearing of American or Irish soldiers singing Green Grow the Rushes (or some variant on the title) has no factual basis. The word first appears in Spain." An Argentine telling, which [Adriana Crolla](https://www.fhuc.unl.edu.ar/portalgringo/crear/gringa/elportal/pdf/editoriales/gringo1.pdf) quotes from Mario E. Teruggi, moves the singers to Beresford's British soldiers in Buenos Aires in 1806, which is still too late.

The green-uniform version fails on the same dates, and so does [a companion story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gringo) that derives gringo from Erin go bragh, the motto of the Saint Patrick's Battalion, which fought alongside the Mexican army. Icazbalceta's own guess, offered explicitly as a conjecture, has no battalion and no war in it, so the dates cannot rule it out: the Irish, as Catholics, went to Spain in numbers, and the opening words of Erin go bragh, which he knew as the motto on the arms of Ireland, run together as eringo, close to gringo.

## Greek is the likeliest source, and still unproven

The [American Heritage Dictionary](https://ahdictionary.com/word/search.html?q=gringo) and Etymonline both point to **griego**, Spanish for Greek, and [Snopes](https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/gringo/) calls it the most likely origin, but the dictionaries hedge. The AHD says gringo is possibly an alteration of griego; [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/gringo) says "perhaps" and warns that "this may be folk etymology". The Real Academia labels the etymology *disc.*, its abbreviation for disputed.

The case for Greek survives in the current dictionary: the [Real Academia](https://dle.rae.es/gringo) defines gringo as "lenguaje ininteligible", unintelligible language, with griego as a synonym. Its [entry for griego](https://dle.rae.es/griego) defines that word as language "considerado incomprensible" and lists gringo as a synonym. English has the same idiom, familiar from Casca in Shakespeare's *Julius Caesar*, who reports that Cicero spoke Greek and says, "[it was Greek to me](https://shakespeare.mit.edu/julius_caesar/julius_caesar.1.2.html)."

In 1805 Antonio de Capmany's [French-Spanish dictionary](https://archive.org/details/nuevodiccionario00capm), printed in Madrid, needed Spanish for the French idioms for speech nobody can follow: *parler hebreu, bas-breton, haut-allemand* (to speak Hebrew, Low Breton or High German). It gave *hablar en griego, en guirigay, en gringo*: **to speak Greek, gibberish or gringo**.

## Who counts as a gringo depends on the country

By 1849 the word was being shouted at Americans. John Woodhouse Audubon, son of the ornithologist John James Audubon, was crossing northern Mexico toward the California gold fields that June, and at a garrison town called Cerro Gordo his party was "hooted and shouted at" and "called 'Gringoes,'" [his journal records](https://archive.org/details/cu31924055366052). In Mexico, someone from the United States is still the word's first sense.

| Where | What gringo means there |
|---|---|
| Mexico | Someone from the United States; also a blond speaker of a foreign language |
| Chile, Colombia, Cuba, Peru, Venezuela and seven more | Someone from the United States |
| Uruguay | Also someone from England, or from Russia |
| Bolivia, Honduras, Nicaragua, Peru | Also a blond, fair-skinned person |
| Argentina | A foreigner, first the English and later particularly Italians |
| Brazil | Someone not native to the country where they live, especially if blond or red-haired and speaking another language |

Senses from the Real Academia Espanola, El Colegio de Mexico's [Diccionario del espanol de Mexico](https://dem.colmex.mx/Ver/gringo), the Academia Argentina de Letras as quoted by [Adriana Crolla](https://www.fhuc.unl.edu.ar/portalgringo/crear/gringa/elportal/pdf/editoriales/gringo1.pdf), and the Brazilian [Michaelis](https://michaelis.uol.com.br/moderno-portugues/busca/portugues-brasileiro/gringo/) dictionary.

Mexican Spanish has built idioms on it as well. To eat **a la gringa** is to split the bill so that each person pays for what they had, and **ojo de gringa**, a gringa's eye, was slang for the fifty-peso note back when it was printed in blue ink, according to the [Diccionario del espanol de Mexico](https://dem.colmex.mx/Ver/gringo). The [Diccionario de americanismos](https://www.asale.org/damer/gringo) records a Chilean phrase too: **a lo gringo**, going without underwear.

## The dictionaries disagree on how much it stings

Gringo can be an insult, but not every dictionary says so. [Capmany's 1805 dictionary](https://archive.org/details/nuevodiccionario00capm), which knew gringo only as a word for gibberish, marked it *joc.*, short for *voz jocosa*, a jocular word. El Colegio de Mexico's dictionary lists it as popular speech with no derogatory label.

The others are harsher. The [Real Academia's entry](https://dle.rae.es/gringo) carries "U. a veces en sent. despect.", its shorthand for sometimes used disparagingly, on three senses: a foreigner, a foreign language and someone from the United States. Michaelis marks it pejorative, the Portuguese [Priberam](https://dicionario.priberam.org/gringo) tags it *depreciativo*, and the American Heritage Dictionary labels it *Often Offensive*.

More than two centuries on, the target has moved from Irishmen in Madrid to Americans in Mexico, but the Real Academia still defines a gringo first as a foreigner, especially an English speaker. Strictly FYI.
