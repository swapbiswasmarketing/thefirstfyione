An advertisement for baseball goods in the Daily Princetonian of **4 March 1912**, as the [Oxford English Dictionary](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/t-shirt_n) quotes it, lists 400 baseball bats and then offers "T shirts", pitched as "just the thing" for "early Spring work".

The [dictionary](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/t-shirt_n) puts this advertisement at the head of its entry for the word, **eight years ahead of the F. Scott Fitzgerald novel** that usually gets the credit and supplies its second quotation.

## What does the T in T-shirt stand for?

**The T stands for no word at all.** It is a picture of the garment: laid flat, the body and sleeves make a capital T. The [Oxford English Dictionary](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/t-shirt_n) defines the T-shirt as a garment "forming the shape of a letter T when spread out flat", [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/T-shirt) says the name refers to "the shape it makes when laid flat", and neither treats the letter as short for training or any other word.

The [American Heritage Dictionary](https://www.ahdictionary.com/word/search.html?q=T-shirt) gives the same explanation with a hedge: "Perhaps from its being shaped like the letter T, when spread out."

## Tee is the letter's name, spelled out

The other spelling works the same way. [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/tee) records tee as "the name of the letter T" from the **1610s**, so a tee shirt is a T-shirt with its letter written out. The golf tee comes from a different word: [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/tee) has it back-formed by 1721 from teaz, a Scottish word of 1673 whose origin is uncertain, and in its original form it "was a little heap of sand."

## The word's earliest known uses sit beside bats and sweaters

Fitzgerald's line is that second quotation. In [This Side of Paradise](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/805), which Scribner's [published on 26 March 1920](https://manuscripts.blogs.princeton.edu/2013/09/11/fitzgeralds-princeton-novel-goes-online/), Amory Blaine sets out for school in New England with "six suits summer underwear, six suits winter underwear, one sweater or T shirt, one jersey, one overcoat, winter, etc." The novel spells it the way the advertisement did, as two words.

In 2021, [Literary Hub](https://lithub.com/did-you-know-that-f-scott-fitzgerald-was-the-first-writer-to-use-the-term-t-shirt/), citing the OED, called Fitzgerald the first writer to use the term in print. The dictionary entry that [Snopes reproduced in March 2025](https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/sewing-t-shirts-bachelors-history/) opens with the 1912 advertisement instead. [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/T-shirt) still gives **1920**, the year of the novel, as the first known use.

Two years after the novel, a 29 June 1922 piece in the Rutland Daily Herald of Vermont titled "Comfortable Togs for Your Vacation", quoted by [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/T-shirt), singled out "the sweat shirt, or 'T' shirt, which has been used for a good many years among athletes". The [Herald piece](https://www.etymonline.com/word/T-shirt) goes on to compare the shirt with the garment Fitzgerald had listed beside it: "A 'T' shirt will do everything a sweater does and more, while it costs fully a quarter less."

## The Navy's paperwork says undershirt

The usual history hands the garment to sailors: a [Victoria and Albert Museum](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/a-brief-history-of-mens-underwear) history says the knitted or flannel undershirts of laboring men "were also worn by sailors, and their transformation to the T-shirt has been attributed to naval adoption and adaption." [Wikipedia's article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-shirt) names the US Navy, stating that "In 1913, the U.S. Navy first issued them as undergarments," and its footnote for that sentence points to a history page on Tee Fetch, a T-shirt review site. The [archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20190107014747/http://www.teefetch.com/history-of-the-t-Shirt/) it links to never mentions 1913, and dates the Navy issue to "sometime around the Spanish American War."

The Navy's regulations describe the garment by 1905, and **neither the [1905 book](https://archive.org/details/regulationsgover00unit/page/46/mode/1up) nor the [1913 edition](https://archive.org/details/cu31924030896363), as reprinted in 1917, uses the word T-shirt**. The book dated [21 January 1905](https://archive.org/details/regulationsgover00unit) specifies enlisted undershirts "bleached white", with an "elastic collarette on neck opening, with no buttons". Its [light version](https://archive.org/details/regulationsgover00unit/page/46/mode/1up) had "Sleeves not to come below the elbow", and a further rule allowed for the heat: "Cotton undershirts of identical pattern may be worn in warm climates, at the discretion of the commanding officer."

That reprint of the [1913 regulations](https://archive.org/details/cu31924030896363) folds in changes made up to 1917, and it sets out three weights of undershirt. The elbow-sleeved shirt is now the medium, and a new light undershirt is "of the lightest weight cotton consistent with durability", with "sleeves only long enough to cover the armpit."

> The Navy's rule books called the garment an undershirt, while a college newspaper was already printing the name T shirt, sold beside the baseball bats.

The Navy's own magazine, [All Hands](https://archive.org/details/allhands3593unit), was still using other names in 1947. That March [the magazine](https://archive.org/details/allhands3593unit) reported that "skivvy shirt shortages" were "about over", and in May it relayed a notice keeping "undershirts, cotton" on the critical list; the [Naval History and Heritage Command](https://www.history.navy.mil/research/library/online-reading-room/title-list-alphabetically/u/uniforms-usnavy/mens-uniforms.html) reprints both items on its Men's Uniforms page.

## Sold as underwear first, worn on its own after the war

The [Oxford English Dictionary](https://www.oed.com/dictionary/t-shirt_n) defines the garment as originally a man's undershirt. Civilian makers were selling versions of it as underwear before the name turned up in print: an advertisement in the archives of Jockey International, once the Cooper Underwear Company, offers a cotton "bachelor shirt" with "no button-holes to get too big for the buttons, no buttons to come off or to need sewing on, no safety-pins needed to keep it closed", and a spokesman told [Snopes](https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/sewing-t-shirts-bachelors-history/) that Jockey estimates it dates from **1902 to 1911**.

That shirt had long sleeves and a crew neck, and Emma McClendon, an assistant professor of fashion studies at St. John's University, told [Snopes](https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/sewing-t-shirts-bachelors-history/) that shirts like it "would have been underwear" in the early 1900s, worn under a buttoned shirt.

Wearing it alone as everyday dress came later. "In the years after the Second World War, most men still wore their T-shirts underneath their shirts", according to a [Victoria and Albert Museum](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/a-brief-history-of-mens-underwear) history of men's underwear, which says screen portrayals by Marlon Brando in A Streetcar Named Desire (**1951**) and The Wild One (1953), and James Dean in Rebel Without a Cause (1955), helped make the shirt outerwear and "part of the uniform of a rebellious teenager".

The museum's history also quotes the fashion historian [Valerie Steele](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/a-brief-history-of-mens-underwear), who called the T-shirt "the most significant and pervasive example of underwear as outerwear".

## The soldiers in the training-shirt story arrive too late

The folk explanation says the T stands for training. A [garment-industry blog](https://www.onlineclothingstudy.com/2025/09/what-does-t-in-t-shirt-stand-for-origin.html) tells it two ways on one page: its article dates the theory to the Second World War, when "American soldiers were issued lightweight, short-sleeved undershirts for training", called training shirts, and its own FAQ moves those shirts to World War I. Its only source is another company's blog.

Neither war fits. The word was in Princeton's student paper **five years before** [Congress voted for war in April 1917](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1914-1920/wwi), and none of the three dictionaries quoted above mentions training.

The shirt's link to sport is real. The Vermont piece says athletes had worn it for a good many years, and the Princeton advertisement sold it beside the bats for "early Spring work".

Fitzgerald [started classes at Princeton](https://manuscripts.blogs.princeton.edu/2013/09/11/fitzgeralds-princeton-novel-goes-online/) on 24 September 1913, a year and a half after that advertisement ran, and Book One of [his novel](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/805) puts Amory Blaine "on the Princetonian board". The book usually credited with the word gives its hero a seat at the paper that had printed it eight years earlier. Strictly FYI.
