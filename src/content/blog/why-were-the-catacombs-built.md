In 1774, three hundred metres of a public road in Paris [stopped being a road](https://www.catacombes.paris.fr/en/history/site-history). The street now called Denfert-Rochereau dropped into the ground, into tunnels the city had spent centuries cutting beneath itself to quarry out the stone its own buildings were made of.

Twelve years later a disused quarry under the plain of Montrouge was blessed and consecrated, and carts arrived after dark, loaded with the dead.

That is one half of the answer. The other half happened in Rome, sixteen centuries earlier, and has almost nothing in common with it.

## Why were the catacombs built?

Rome's catacombs were community cemeteries: [Roman law banned burial inside the city](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/ancient/twelve_tables.asp), so the Church dug outside the walls, into soft volcanic tuff, making underground burial grounds large enough [to welcome "the whole community in only one necropolis"](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/pontifical_commissions/archeo/inglese/documents/rc_com_archeo_doc_20011010_cataccrist_en.html). Paris's catacombs were worked-out limestone quarries, [transformed into an ossuary in the eighteenth century](https://www.catacombes.paris.fr/en/history/ossuary) after the old workings [began collapsing under the city](https://www.catacombes.paris.fr/en/history/geology-and-quarries), and filled with [bones from the city's former cemeteries](https://www.catacombes.paris.fr/en).

## Rome went down outside the walls, where the law kept the dead

The Roman law code known as the Twelve Tables opens [its section on sacred law](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/ancient/twelve_tables.asp) with a flat instruction: **"A dead person shall not be buried or burned in the city."** Rome's burial grounds therefore lined the roads leading out of town, and on the Via Appia the tombs [formed "an almost uninterrupted street for many miles from the gates of the city"](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/secondary/SMIGRA%2A/Funus.html).

Burial also takes room. A body needs far more space than an urn, and [the Catholic Encyclopedia's article on the catacombs](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/03417b.htm) sets the two customs side by side: Romans "cremated their dead and deposited the ashes in a family tomb", while "the Jews living in Rome retained their native method of burial". That contrast describes an earlier Rome: the [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/roman-sarcophagi) places Roman cremation in "the Republican and early imperial periods", with burial in sarcophagi spreading through the empire from the second century. Christians buried too.

Rome's answer was geology. The [Pontifical Commission for Sacred Archaeology](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/pontifical_commissions/archeo/inglese/documents/rc_com_archeo_doc_20011010_cataccrist_en.html), which dates the origin of the catacombs to "between the end of the second and the beginning of the third centuries", records that they were "for the most part, excavated in tuff", a ground it calls "easily removable but solid". The land itself came from believers: [San Callisto's custodians write](http://www.catacombesancallisto.it/en/le-origini-delle-catacombe.php) that "as a result of various grants and donations, the Christians started burying their dead underground", often around family tombs opened to "their brethren in the faith".

Digging down let one plot hold storey after storey of graves. The Catacombs of San Callisto run [**"about 12 miles long, in four levels, more than twenty meters deep"**](http://www.catacombesancallisto.it/en/le-origini-delle-catacombe.php), and [their custodians count](http://www.catacombesancallisto.it/en/index.php) roughly **half a million** Christian burials there, including **16 popes**.

Rome's Christians did not call any of this a catacomb. The [ancient term was *coemeterium*](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/pontifical_commissions/archeo/inglese/documents/rc_com_archeo_doc_20011010_cataccrist_en.html), from a Greek word meaning "dormitory", and the Commission reads the term as stressing that "for Christians, burial is just a temporary moment while they wait for the final resurrection".

## The hiding-place story has no Roman evidence

The familiar picture has persecuted Christians tunnelling out a refuge and holding secret Mass underground while soldiers search the streets above. The [International Catacomb Society](https://www.catacombsociety.org/vom/catacombs_of_rome.html) finds "no evidence to support the common, romantic notion that Christians habitually worshiped in the catacombs; nor is there any proof that they used them as hiding places during the periods of persecution". One sentence from a Roman official shows why.

[In 257](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05011a.htm), when the emperor Valerian began persecuting the Church, Aemilianus, the prefect of Egypt, sentenced Bishop Dionysius of Alexandria to exile and attached a condition, [preserved by Eusebius](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250107.htm): it "shall by no means be permitted you or any others, either to hold assemblies, or to enter into the so called cemeteries."

> You cannot ban people from a hiding place whose address you do not have. The ban Aemilianus read out is evidence that the Roman state knew where Christian cemeteries were.

The prefect even uses the Christians' own word for the places, with a "so called" in front of it. The same campaign reached Rome, where, as Cyprian of Carthage [passed on the news](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/050681.htm), "Xistus was martyred in the cemetery on the eighth day of the Ides of August, and with him four deacons". The authorities knew where to find Rome's bishop.

When Valerian's son Gallienus called off the persecution, one of his decrees, as [Eusebius also records](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250107.htm), allowed bishops "to take possession again of the so-called cemeteries".

A century later, with the persecutions ended by the [edict of Milan in 313](http://www.catacombesancallisto.it/en/le-origini-delle-catacombe.php), Jerome, a student in Rome, remembered the catacombs as [an outing rather than a bolthole](https://www.catacombsociety.org/vom/catacombs_of_rome.html): "When I was a youth at Rome, studying liberal arts, it was my custom on Sundays" to "visit the sepulchres of the apostles and martyrs".

The romantic version had help in the nineteenth century. Nicholas Wiseman gave his 1854 novel the title [*Fabiola; or, The Church of the Catacombs*](https://archive.org/details/fabiolaorchurcho00wise), which tells you what readers were being sold.

## Paris inherited its catacombs from quarrymen

Everything under Paris was a hole first. The Lutetian limestone known as [Paris stone](https://www.catacombes.paris.fr/en/history/geology-and-quarries) was cut in open pits in antiquity, then dug again from the Middle Ages for the churches, the Louvre castle and the city walls, until the quarries turned into underground galleries. The digging was poorly regulated, and after a number of collapses in the city centre a decree of **15 September 1776** [closed the Paris quarries for good](https://www.catacombes.paris.fr/en/history/geology-and-quarries).

The second problem was on the surface. The [city's history of the site](https://www.catacombes.paris.fr/en/history/site-history) begins with "major public health problems tied to the city's cemeteries", and the largest cemetery in Paris, Saints-Innocents, closed in **1780** after "consecutive use for nearly ten centuries". Paris now had too many dead and, in its abandoned quarries, a great deal of empty space.

On **7 April 1786** the Tombe-Issoire quarries under the plain of Montrouge, a site then "located outside the capital", were [blessed and consecrated](https://www.catacombes.paris.fr/en/history/site-history) as the municipal ossuary and took the name "Catacombs", after Rome's. Bones came out of Saints-Innocents between **1785 and 1787**, travelling at night "to avoid hostile reactions from the Parisian population and the Church", and transfers from other parish grounds ran until **1814**, resumed in 1840 and ended in 1860.

The ossuary [opened to the public in **1809**](https://www.catacombes.paris.fr/en/history/ossuary), and from **1810 to 1814** Inspector Hericart de Thury [rearranged it](https://www.catacombes.paris.fr/en/history/site-history), stacking the loosely piled bones into walls of femurs and skulls. Visitors now walk a [1.5 km circuit](https://www.catacombes.paris.fr/en/visit/please-read-your-visit), [twenty metres underground](https://www.catacombes.paris.fr/en), past the remains of several million Parisians.

## The word first belonged to one cemetery

"Catacomb" started as an address. [In antiquity](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/pontifical_commissions/archeo/inglese/documents/rc_com_archeo_doc_20011010_cataccrist_en.html), the Pontifical Commission notes, the word "only defined the complex of St. Sebastian on the Appian Way". The [International Catacomb Society](https://www.catacombsociety.org/vom/catacombs_of_rome.html) says it "may derive from the term ad catacumbas, the Latinized Greek kata kumbas meaning 'down near the hollows'", perhaps "the resting places or graves", or may be "a topographical reference" to the cemetery "near the hollow in the Via Appia".

The origin is disputed. The [Online Etymology Dictionary](https://www.etymonline.com/word/catacomb) calls it obscure and offers Latin *cata tumbas*, "at the graves", as one candidate, while allowing that the word may have been a proper name to begin with. It spread because St Sebastian's stayed open: [monks there kept it accessible for pilgrims](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/03417b.htm) after most of the others were forgotten, and once those were rediscovered, "the name belonging to this one coemeterium was applied to all". English stretched it to "any subterranean receptacle of the dead" [by 1836](https://www.etymonline.com/word/catacomb).

Rome's dead went underground outside the walls, where the law kept them, because soft tuff let donated plots hold whole communities. Paris's dead went underground because the holes were already there and the graveyards had shut. A French limestone mine ended up wearing the name of a single burial ground beside an Italian road, and only one of the two cities ever set out to dig a cemetery. Strictly FYI.
