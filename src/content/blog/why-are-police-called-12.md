You have heard someone say it out of a car window, or in a chorus, or across a parking lot. Twelve. Everybody within earshot knows exactly who is coming, nobody breaks stride, and nobody has ever stopped to ask why a number is doing the work of a word.

Ask now and the answer comes back fast and confident, always off the same short menu: a police radio code, a television show from 1968, a letter of the alphabet, a story about Atlanta's police districts. Open any of them and the receipt is missing.

## Why are police called 12?

No documented origin exists. [Urban Dictionary's contributors](https://www.urbandictionary.com/define.php?term=12) offer at least six explanations for calling police 12 - the radio code 10-12, the NBC series Adam-12, a count of Atlanta's police districts, the letter L for law, the digits of 911 and the twelve members of a jury - and none of them comes with a dated first use. Green's Dictionary of Slang records the word and supplies no etymology.

Green's describes itself as [the largest historical dictionary of English slang](https://greensdictofslang.com/about), and it is built out of dated quotations, over 400,000 citations in the print edition alone. Its [entry for **twelve, n.**](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/zg3ofyq) gives sense three as US Black gang slang for the police, resting on a **single citation from 2016**, a magazine piece called "Dispatches from the Rap Wars". Where the etymology would go, there is nothing.

That citation is a glossary line, not a sentence. ["Dispatches from the Rap Wars"](https://www.chicagotribune.com/2016/09/19/dispatches-from-the-rap-wars/), published by Chicago magazine on **September 19, 2016**, is sociologist Forrest Stuart's account of a year and a half embedded with one of the city's drill-rap gangs. A Drill Rap Glossary set into the piece defines twelve simply as "The police," with the jakes offered as another name for them. It explains nothing about the number either.

## The code book runs to 10-99 and 10-12 means hold your position

[APCO's national training manual for police and public safety radio personnel](https://archive.org/details/nationaltraining025505mbp), first issued in **February 1967** and reissued by the federal Office of Law Enforcement Assistance in 1968, reproduces the revised official APCO ten-signal list. **10-12 reads "Stand by (stop)."** The manual's interpretation section leaves no room to improvise: **"Physical stand by, remain alert. Not a stand by (10-6) on the radio."**

Hold position and stay sharp. It is an instruction about where an officer should be standing.

| Signal | Official meaning, 1967 APCO manual |
|---|---|
| 10-6 | Busy - stand by unless urgent |
| 10-11 | Dog Case |
| 10-12 | Stand by (stop) |
| 10-33 | EMERGENCY |
| 10-39 | Urgent - use light and siren |

[The manual's list](https://archive.org/details/nationaltraining025505mbp) runs from **10-1 to 10-99** and covers a fight in progress, a dog case, a riot, and a request for the correct time. It is a full working vocabulary for a shift, and the entry at 10-12 is an instruction, not a name. A radio carried only by police had little use for a code meaning police.

Earlier printings are where this gets good. [APCO's Bulletin recalled in 1976](https://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.ssl.cf1.rackcdn.com/1976-01-pp014-200.pdf) that the first ten signals it published, **17 of them**, appeared in 1939. In [that first list, the Illinois operators' signals](https://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.ssl.cf1.rackcdn.com/1939-05-p006-200.pdf), 10-12 was "We have CW traffic." and "Officials or visitors present." sat at 10-21.

In the [1940 APCO Standards Committee list](https://web.archive.org/web/20170810212357/http://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.r13.cf1.rackcdn.com/1940-01-p008-200.pdf), printed in the APCO Bulletin that January, the meaning moved up: 10-12 read "Officials or visitors present." [The same list](https://web.archive.org/web/20170810212357/http://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.r13.cf1.rackcdn.com/1940-01-p008-200.pdf) saved 10-24 for "unwelcome visitors" at the station, an all-units call; 10-12 was the quiet one. [The list prepared by the National Operating Procedure Committee in 1955](https://web.archive.org/web/20250617093213/http://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.r13.cf1.rackcdn.com/1957-04-pp007-200.pdf) kept it as "Visitors or officials present." It was a warning about who could hear: a signal police sent each other about whoever was in the room.

> If the slang really did descend from 10-12, it took a code police used to warn each other that outsiders were listening and handed it to outsiders to warn each other that police are. The signal reversed direction on its way out of the radio.

## The 10-12 story is folklore, and it shows

One [Urban Dictionary entry, posted on April 22, 2007](https://www.urbandictionary.com/define.php?term=12), calls 10-12 a CB radio code meaning "visitors present", a way to tell someone on the other end to be careful what they said. That is close to the old APCO wording, but nothing in it says police, and no printing of the APCO list has ever made 10-12 a word for police. The list itself kept moving. The [ten-signals](/blog/what-does-10-4-mean/) were [rewritten in 1955](https://web.archive.org/web/20250617093213/http://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.r13.cf1.rackcdn.com/1957-04-pp007-200.pdf), again for [APCO Project 2 in 1967 and APCO Project 14 in 1974](https://www.apcointl.org/community/projects/), where [10-12 still read "Stand By (Stop)"](https://web.archive.org/web/20260906192505/http://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.r13.cf1.rackcdn.com/1974-proj14-apr-060.200.pdf).

[The manual](https://archive.org/details/nationaltraining025505mbp) dates the signals to Illinois APCO members in **1935**, registered and adopted nationally in **1940**, though [APCO's own project history](https://www.apcointl.org/community/projects/) puts the 10 Codes at 1937. An origin story that needs one code to have meant one thing, everywhere, for decades is asking a great deal of a system that kept revising itself.

## The 12 in 1-Adam-12 is a beat number

[Adam-12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam-12) ran on NBC from **September 21, 1968 to May 20, 1975**, created by Robert A. Cinader and Jack Webb. The call sign in the title is an LAPD unit designation in three parts: the **1** is Central Division, downtown Los Angeles; **Adam** is the LAPD phonetic letter A, which marks a two-officer patrol unit; and the **12** is the daily assigned reporting district, a beat.

The theory therefore needs viewers to have heard an arbitrary beat number, mistaken it for a word meaning police, and kept it for half a century after the show left the air. None of that is impossible, and none of it is written down anywhere.

## 1312 has a paper trail and 12 has none

Set it beside the other number people attach to police. [1312 spells ACAB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACAB) by alphabet position, and the phrase behind it has dates on it: Eric Partridge's *A Dictionary of Catch Phrases* puts the full phrase in use by the **1920s**, and Thompson and Black's forensic identification textbook records the acronym as a prison tattoo by the **1970s**, one letter to a finger.

Twelve offers nothing comparable. What it has is an [Urban Dictionary entry](https://www.urbandictionary.com/define.php?term=12) posted on **June 11, 2006**, which already describes the finished usage: shouting the word when police are spotted approaching. A decade before the dictionary of record found a printable citation, the term was fully grown online with no explanation attached.

Slang files no paperwork. It moves through speech for years before anyone writes it down, and by the time a dictionary catches up the origin has been painted over by whichever explanation sounded best. Half a dozen stories, no dates, and the best fact of the lot is buried inside the weakest one: the code everybody credits once told officers that officials or visitors could hear them. Strictly FYI.
