By the time the barbarian commander Odoacer entered Ravenna in the late summer of 476, he had [killed the emperor's father near Piacenza](https://web.archive.org/web/20260929135755/https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/auggiero.htm) and the emperor's uncle in a pine grove outside the city. The emperor himself, a boy called Romulus, he let live.

The second part of the [Anonymus Valesianus](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Excerpta_Valesiana/2*.html), a Latin history of King Theoderic that opens with these events, says Odoacer spared him "in pity for his tender years". Because the boy was handsome, Odoacer also gave him ["an income of six thousand gold-pieces"](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Excerpta_Valesiana/2*.html) and sent him to Campania, "to live there a free man with his relatives".

That retirement is the conventional end of the Western Roman Empire. The first known history to call it the fall of Rome was written in Constantinople, more than forty years later.

## When did Rome fall?

The Western Roman Empire is conventionally said to have fallen in **September 476**, when Odoacer [deposed the boy emperor](https://time.com/6101964/fabricated-fall-rome-lessons-history/) Romulus Augustulus. The Roman Empire itself carried on in the east, ruled from Constantinople, until Ottoman forces under Sultan Mehmed II [took the city](https://www.worldhistory.org/Byzantine_Empire/) on **29 May 1453**. The city of Rome had been sacked twice earlier in the fifth century, and the eastern half of the empire outlived the western by **977 years**.

The right date depends on which Rome you mean:

| Which Rome | Date | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| The city | 24 August 410 | Sacked by Alaric's Goths |
| The city | 455 | Sacked by Geiseric's Vandals |
| The western empire, by convention | 4 September 476 or soon after | Romulus Augustulus deposed by Odoacer |
| The western imperial title | 480 | Julius Nepos, still claiming the title, killed in Dalmatia |
| The Roman Empire | 29 May 1453 | Constantinople taken by Mehmed II |

## The sacked city belongs to 410 and 455

If 476 conjures barbarians sacking Rome, that scene played out in two earlier years. The sack that shocked contemporaries came on **24 August 410**, when Alaric's Goths took Rome, [the first time in about 800 years](https://antigonejournal.com/2024/09/when-did-the-roman-empire-fall/) that the city had fallen to a foreign enemy. Augustine later explained that pagans blamed the sack on Christianity, and that this ["kindled my zeal for the house of God"](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/45304) and set him writing *The City of God*.

Jerome, in a [letter memorializing his friend Marcella](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3001127.htm), wrote that "sobs choke my utterance" and that "The City which had taken the whole world was itself taken."

In **455** the Vandals of King Geiseric sacked Rome again, so thoroughly, [writes Anatoly Grablevsky in Antigone](https://antigonejournal.com/2024/09/when-did-the-roman-empire-fall/), that "the sack of 410 seemed mild by comparison". Italian sources of the period, he adds, barely register 476, treating Odoacer as "merely the latest in a long line of barbarian rulers of the West".

The historian Edward J. Watts counts [nine western emperors](https://time.com/6101964/fabricated-fall-rome-lessons-history/) who had risen and fallen since 455, most of them overthrown by barbarian commanders like Odoacer.

Why the West gave way is a separate and crowded argument. Grablevsky's candidate for the turning point is 468, when Vandal fireboats set a Roman armada alight off Cap Bon, near Carthage: ["the last point at which the tide could have been turned"](https://antigonejournal.com/2024/09/when-did-the-roman-empire-fall/).

## Odoacer sent the regalia to Constantinople

Odoacer did not make himself emperor. He took the title of king, and the chronicler Cassiodorus [recorded](https://web.archive.org/web/20260929135755/https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/auggiero.htm) that he "made use of neither the purple nor the imperial regalia". The regalia went to the eastern emperor, Zeno.

The historian Malchus says Romulus himself, in a final official act, had the Roman Senate send envoys, who [told Zeno](https://web.archive.org/web/20260929135755/https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/nepos.htm) that the West had "no need of a separate empire" and that "a single common emperor would be sufficient for both territories". They asked him to make Odoacer a patrician and hand him the government of Italy.

## The last recognized western emperor lived until 480

Zeno's reply, [as Malchus records it](https://web.archive.org/web/20260929135755/https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/nepos.htm), was pointed. The western Romans had received two men from the eastern empire and had "driven one out, Nepos, and killed the other, Anthemius". While their emperor was still alive, they should take him back.

Julius Nepos had fled to Dalmatia in 475, when Orestes, Romulus's father, marched on Ravenna, and he kept the imperial title. From **477 to 480**, Ralph Mathisen writes in the [Online Encyclopedia of Roman Emperors](https://web.archive.org/web/20260929135755/https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/nepos.htm), the mints of Milan and Ravenna, in Odoacer's Italy, struck gold coins in the names of Nepos and Zeno.

His own supporters killed him in **480**, and Mathisen concludes that Nepos ["rightly deserves the title 'last western Roman emperor.'"](https://web.archive.org/web/20260929135755/https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/nepos.htm) The Anonymus says that after five years in Dalmatia he was ["slain by his own men"](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Excerpta_Valesiana/2*.html).

## The empire that fell in 1453 still called itself Roman

In the east, the empire carried on. Apart from [1204 to 1261](https://www.worldhistory.org/Byzantine_Empire/), when crusaders held the city, Constantinople stayed the seat of a Roman emperor until Sultan Mehmed II began [a 53-day siege on 6 April 1453](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Constantinople) and took the city on **29 May**. The last emperor, Constantine XI, led the defence and died as the city fell, though [the accounts of how he died disagree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fall_of_Constantinople).

The city's people called themselves Romans [until the very end](https://antigonejournal.com/2024/09/when-did-the-roman-empire-fall/), and called their Greek language "Romaic". The label "Byzantine" for the whole empire was [coined by 16th-century historians](https://www.worldhistory.org/Byzantine_Empire/), after Byzantium, the capital's first name. Anthony Kaldellis built his 2019 book *Romanland* on the case that Roman was [both their identity and their ethnicity](https://www.worldhistory.org/review/222/romanland-ethnicity-and-empire-in-byzantium/).

## A chronicler in Constantinople picked 476

The boy's names made him easy to cast as the end of the line. Romulus was the name of [the city's legendary founder](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/19725), and Augustulus shrinks the name of Augustus, who founded the empire. The Anonymus's translator notes that the title was shrunk ["in mockery of, or perhaps because of, his youth"](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Excerpta_Valesiana/2*.html).

With names like those, Mathisen writes, "it would have been difficult indeed to resist" making him the last. Marcellinus Comes, an Illyrian writing a [Latin chronicle](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2002/2002.09.39/) in Constantinople, reached the year 476 and wrote: ["The western Empire of the Roman people... perished with this Augustulus."](https://web.archive.org/web/20260929135755/https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/auggiero.htm)

Watts, who dates the chronicle to the [late 510s](https://time.com/6101964/fabricated-fall-rome-lessons-history/), calls it "the first historical work known to claim that Rome fell in 476". In Brian Croke's study of Marcellinus, the chronicle originally ran to the year 518, with a [later update to 534](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2002/2002.09.39/).

Marcellinus called Odoacer "king of the Goths". Watts calls that a fabrication, because Odoacer was not a Goth, while Theoderic, who ruled Italy when Marcellinus wrote, was. Marcellinus was an aide to the future emperor Justinian, and in Watts's summary of Croke's argument, 476 is ["a manufactured historical turning point that has become an accepted historical fact."](https://time.com/6101964/fabricated-fall-rome-lessons-history/) Watts argues it became a pretext for Justinian's invasion of Italy in 535.

Justinian's own justification, in a letter to the leaders of the Franks that [Procopius preserves](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20298/20298-h/20298-h.htm), was that the Goths had seized "Italy, which was ours".

> In Watts's reading, the count ran from 476. When Justinian's army entered Rome in December 536, Procopius wrote that "Rome became subject to the Romans again after a space of sixty years", and Odoacer's coup lay sixty years and three months in the past.

Romulus himself may have lived three decades past the date now given for the fall of Rome. Mathisen records [a suggestion](https://web.archive.org/web/20260929135755/https://roman-emperors.sites.luc.edu/auggiero.htm) that he is the Romulus to whom the Ostrogothic king Theoderic wrote around 507 to 511, about a grant to "a certain Romulus and his mother".

That letter, in [Thomas Hodgkin's 1886 translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/18590), confirms any gift that the Patrician Liberius made on the king's orders "to you or to your mother", and Hodgkin thought it "surely possible that this is the dethroned Emperor."

If so, the man remembered as the last western emperor was still alive, with a king guaranteeing his gifts, not long before Marcellinus wrote him down as the end of Rome. Strictly FYI.
