From her mother's house in Cumberland Street, Parsonstown, Mary Magrath saw the engine coming and called a friend who had never seen it before. Then, as she told a coroner's jury the next morning, she [saw the engine "rise" at one side](https://web.archive.org/web/20140116122125/http://www.offalyhistory.com/reading-resources/history/famous-offaly-people/mary-ward-1827-1869) and saw a lady fall off. The wheel, she said, "hit the lady and pushed her on one side."

The lady was Mary Ward, a microscopist and artist. Parsonstown is the Irish town now called Birr.

## When was the first car accident?

The first fatal car accident usually counted is Mary Ward's death on **31 August 1869**, when she was thrown from an experimental steam carriage at Parsonstown, Ireland, and crushed by its wheel. A 2005 paper calls it [the first recorded automobile fatality](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15949449/).

A Scottish steam coach had [killed passengers in 1834](https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/9th-august-1834/8/scotland). Cugnot's steam wagon, said to have hit a wall in Paris [between 1769 and 1771](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas-Joseph_Cugnot), is the story with the thinnest paperwork.

## Mary Ward's death has a newspaper report and an inquest

The case does not rest on legend. The King's County Chronicle printed its report on 1 September 1869, the day after, with the inquest testimony, and the text is reproduced by the [Offaly Historical and Archaeological Society](https://web.archive.org/web/20140116122125/http://www.offalyhistory.com/reading-resources/history/famous-offaly-people/mary-ward-1827-1869). Five people were on a steam carriage that had set out from Birr Castle: Ward, her husband Captain Ward, two sons of her cousin William, the 3rd Earl of Rosse, and their tutor, Richard Biggs, who was steering.

Biggs said he felt a slight jolt as they turned into Cumberland Street and could give no reason for it. James Rolleston, a magistrate who had walked along behind the carriage, put its speed at between three and a half and [4 miles an hour](https://web.archive.org/web/20140116122125/http://www.offalyhistory.com/reading-resources/history/famous-offaly-people/mary-ward-1827-1869). The local doctor, whose house stood almost opposite, found her neck broken and the base of her skull fractured, and she died about a minute after he reached her.

> The jury found "that the deceased came to her death by an accidental fall from a steam engine on which she had been riding in the town of Parsonstown on the preceding day" and "that there was no blame attaching to any person in connection with the occurrence." - [King's County Chronicle, 1869](https://web.archive.org/web/20140116122125/http://www.offalyhistory.com/reading-resources/history/famous-offaly-people/mary-ward-1827-1869)

Isabelle Fallon, of Trinity College Dublin, and Desmond O'Neill titled their 2005 paper in Accident Analysis and Prevention ["The world's first automobile fatality"](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aap.2005.02.002), though by one reckoning the automobile had not yet been born. A history of the car published by the German patent office, following Olaf von Fersen's book Ein Jahrhundert Automobiltechnik, places the birth of the modern automobile in 1886, with Carl Benz's three-wheeled motor carriage, [patent DE 37 435](https://web.archive.org/web/20170102082130/https://www.dpma.de/service/klassifikationen/ipc/ipcprojekt/einekurzegeschichtedesautomobils/geburtstagdesautos/index.html), **17 years** after Ward was killed.

## A steam coach near Glasgow killed passengers in 1834

Steam vehicles had killed people on a public road before, though. In **April 1834** a company running coaches designed by the engineer John Scott Russell started an hourly service between Glasgow and Paisley, and the coaches were so popular that [30 to 40 persons](https://archive.org/details/cu31924022808731/page/n153/mode/2up) repeatedly crowded onto one built for 26, William Fletcher wrote in his 1891 history of steam on common roads.

The Spectator's issue of 9 August 1834 reported that one of the wheels of the steam carriage broke on the Paisley Road and the machine came down so hard that the boiler was crushed flat and burst, and that ["all the passengers, twelve in number, were more or less injured."](https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/9th-august-1834/8/scotland) Six people had since died, three of them named. Fletcher, writing in 1891, put the toll at [five of the passengers](https://archive.org/details/cu31924022808731/page/n157/mode/2up), and the Court of Session stopped the coaches from running.

Russell's machine was a passenger coach with its engine on board, towing a two-wheeled tender that carried the water and coke and had seats of its own: closer to a bus than to a family car.

## Cugnot's wall is the story with the thinnest paperwork

Many lists start earlier, with the French army engineer Nicolas-Joseph Cugnot and his steam wagon, said to have knocked down a wall in Paris in 1771. The trouble is that the story will not hold still. Fletcher, writing more than a century after the event, told it about the original machine, tried in 1769: ["Very early in its career, it displayed its power by knocking down a stone wall"](https://archive.org/details/cu31924022808731/page/n45/mode/2up).

Three pages on, he describes a different crash with the improved 1770 machine, which overbalanced on a corner near where the Church of the Madeleine now stands and [fell over with a crash](https://archive.org/details/cu31924022808731/page/n48/mode/2up), after which the machine was locked up and Cugnot was imprisoned. [Other tellings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas-Joseph_Cugnot) move the wall to the Arsenal, or the year to 1771, or turn the jail into an arrest for dangerous driving. A story that changes its date, place and outcome from teller to teller cannot carry a "first".

## Petrol and electric cars came next, and so did the myths

On **17 August 1896**, Bridget Driscoll, 44, was hit by a demonstration car in the grounds of the Crystal Palace in London. At the inquest, the [BBC recounted in 2010](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/magazine-10987606), the driver said he was doing 4 mph, while a witness described a car going "as fast as a good horse could gallop", and the jury returned a verdict of accidental death.

Driscoll's case gathers two myths. The road-victims charity RoadPeace once called her ["the first road fatality in the world"](https://web.archive.org/web/20020618205639/http://www.roadpeace.org/articles/WorldFirstDeath.html), though she died **27 years** after Mary Ward, and put the line "This must never happen again" in the coroner's mouth. [The BBC's account](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/magazine-10987606), drawn from newspaper reports of the inquest, says only that the coroner hoped hers would be the last death in this sort of accident.

In New York on **13 September 1899**, Henry Bliss stepped off a trolley car at Central Park West and 74th Street and was run over by an electric cab. The New York Times reported the next day that [two wheels of the cab passed over his head and body](https://timesmachine.nytimes.com/timesmachine/1899/09/14/102413700.pdf) and that the driver, Arthur Smith, was arrested and locked up. Bliss died, and a centenary campaign in 1999 remembered him as ["the first person killed by Automobile in North America"](https://web.archive.org/web/20000413212657/http://www.rememberbliss.org/).

Ohio History Central, as Snopes quotes it, calls an 1891 crash in Ohio City the first automobile accident in American history: a gasoline car carrying an inventor named Lambert and a passenger [hit a tree root](https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/only-two-cars-ohio-crashed-1895/) and smashed into a hitching post, with minor injuries. The same fact-check found no documentation at all for the popular tale that the only two cars in Ohio crashed into each other in 1895.

## The claimed firsts leave very different paper trails

| Claimed first | When | What survives |
|---|---|---|
| Cugnot's steam wagon, Paris | 1769 to 1771 | Later retellings that disagree |
| Russell's steam coach, near Glasgow | 1834 | A Spectator report of 9 August 1834 and an 1891 history |
| Mary Ward, Parsonstown | 31 August 1869 | Next-day report and inquest testimony |
| Lambert's car, Ohio City | 1891 | An Ohio history website's account; minor injuries |
| Two cars in Ohio | 1895 | Nothing found |
| Bridget Driscoll, London | 17 August 1896 | Inquest coverage in the press |
| Henry Bliss, New York | 13 September 1899 | A New York Times report the next day |
