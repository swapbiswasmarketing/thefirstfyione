One mezcal carries its worm in its name: Emperatriz del Mezcal Artesanal, "Abocado con Gusano," flavored with worm. It is one of **21 commercially available mezcals** that went into [a 2023 paper in PeerJ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10007961/), bought between 2018 and 2022 from North American distributors and from distilleries the team visited in Oaxaca. Each larva was caught in a metal sifter as the mezcal was poured off, then photographed and stored in ethanol at the Florida Museum of Natural History.

Table 2 of the paper lists the rest: Gusano Rojo, Monte Alban, Wahaka, Don Nilo and more. Every one is a mezcal, and not one is tequila.

## The worm belongs to some mezcals, and only since the mid-20th century

The bottle with a worm in it is a mezcal, and in the bottles the study tested, the "worm" was a moth caterpillar. In the broad sense mezcal is any distilled agave spirit, and tequila is one kind of it, made from blue agave in a defined region. The PeerJ authors put it flatly: ["authentic tequila never includes a worm."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10007961/)

The habit is young. Mezcal has been part of Mexican culture since the 17th century, but it was not until the 1940-50s, the study's abstract says, that the worm ["was placed inside bottles of non-tequila mezcal"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36915657/) before distribution. It started as a marketing gimmick, credited to a Mexico City entrepreneur.

## Mexico's two standards treat the worm differently

Tequila and mezcal each have an official Mexican standard, and the worm shows up in only one of them.

The tequila standard, NOM-006-SCFI-2012, defines "abocado" as a ["Procedimiento para suavizar el sabor del Tequila"](https://web.archive.org/web/20160116220158/http://www.dof.gob.mx/nota_detalle.php?codigo=5282165&fecha=13/12/2012), a process to soften the flavor, by adding one or more of **four ingredients**: caramel color, natural oak extract, glycerin and sugar syrup. The word gusano appears nowhere in it.

The same standard requires tequila to be made from the heads of **Agave tequilana Weber, blue variety**, grown in the protected territory, hydrolyzed or cooked, fermented with yeast and distilled. A bottle labeled simply "Tequila" may take [up to 49% of its sugars](https://web.archive.org/web/20160116220158/http://www.dof.gob.mx/nota_detalle.php?codigo=5282165&fecha=13/12/2012) from other sources.

The mezcal standard, NOM-070-SCFI-2016, defines maguey or agave as [any species of the genus Agave](https://web.archive.org/web/20261019123803/https://dof.gob.mx/nota_detalle.php?codigo=5472787&fecha=23%2F02%2F2017). It sets out a class called "Abocado con," mezcal to which ingredients are added directly for flavor, ["tales como gusano de maguey, damiana"](https://web.archive.org/web/20261019123803/https://dof.gob.mx/nota_detalle.php?codigo=5472787&fecha=23%2F02%2F2017), lime, honey, orange and mango. A neighboring class, "Destilado con," is distilled with ingredients such as [turkey or chicken breast, rabbit, mole or plums](https://web.archive.org/web/20261019123803/https://dof.gob.mx/nota_detalle.php?codigo=5472787&fecha=23%2F02%2F2017).

| | Tequila (NOM-006-SCFI-2012) | Mezcal (NOM-070-SCFI-2016) |
|---|---|---|
| Agave | Blue agave; plain "Tequila" may take up to 49% of its sugars elsewhere | Any species of the genus Agave |
| Added for flavor | Caramel color, oak extract, glycerin, sugar syrup | A class "Abocado con" that names the maguey worm |
| Worm mentioned | No | Yes |

## A Mexico City entrepreneur gets the credit, around 1950

The usual name is **Jacobo Lozano Paez**. In a 1999 Straight Dope column, Cecil Adams answered the idea of an ancient tradition with ["Not unless your idea of ancient is 1950,"](https://www.straightdope.com/21342472/why-is-there-a-worm-in-bottles-of-tequila) and wrote that the Mexico City entrepreneur hit on putting a worm in each bottle "as a marketing gimmick."

The PeerJ authors are more careful. Lozano Paez, they write, ["is thought to have been the first"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10007961/) mezcal master to put larvae in bottles as a marketing strategy, to enhance the flavor and color of the drink, and they date the practice to the 1940s-50s. Adams added that the worms were said to have aphrodisiac properties, a belief the paper records too.

## Three suspects, and the DNA picked one

Before the study, the larva had been pinned on [three distantly related insects](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36915657/). The white maguey worm was thought to be the agave snout weevil, Scyphophorus acupunctatus, or the tequila giant skipper butterfly, Aegiale hesperiaris.

The red one, the gusano rojo de maguey, also called chinicuil or chilocuil from the Nahuatl words for pepper and worm, was thought to be the agave redworm moth, Comadia redtenbacheri. [Another name turns up for the moth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tequila), Hypopta agavis, but it is not a fourth suspect: [taxonomic catalogs](https://ftp.funet.fi/index/Tree_of_life/insecta/lepidoptera/ditrysia/cossoidea/cossidae/cossinae/comadia/) list agavis, a name Blasquez gave it in 1870, as a synonym of Comadia redtenbacheri.

Telling the larvae apart by eye only goes so far. "It's relatively easy to broadly determine the kind of larva based on the shape of the head, but their identity has never been confirmed," said Akito Kawahara, a curator at [the Florida Museum](https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/science/scientists-uncover-the-unexpected-identity-of-mezcal-worms/) who worked on the study.

> "This is probably because most biologists are not looking inside mezcal bottles." - [Akito Kawahara, Florida Museum, 2023](https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/science/scientists-uncover-the-unexpected-identity-of-mezcal-worms/)

The worms from 21 commercial mezcals in [a 2023 DNA study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10007961/) all turned out to be one moth, the agave redworm. Of those larvae, 18 gave usable DNA, and **all 18** came back as **Comadia redtenbacheri**. The other three were identified as the same species by their anatomy, from features such as the head capsule and the legs.

Why those three gave no DNA is unknown, the authors write. Many distilleries now toast larvae before bottling for hygiene, which could break up the DNA, but warm storage, UV light or something in the liquor could have done the same.

The result also points to an explanation for the white worms. Adams wrote that ["Some bottlers substitute a species of white worm"](https://www.straightdope.com/21342472/why-is-there-a-worm-in-bottles-of-tequila) that lives in agave leaves. The study found pale and pinkish larvae alike were the red moth, and the researchers suspect, the museum reports, that the white ones were kept in alcohol so long that they have ["consequently leached their color."](https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/science/scientists-uncover-the-unexpected-identity-of-mezcal-worms/)

The caterpillars are harvested in the wild, not farmed. "Agave worms are still fairly common," Kawahara said, but the mezcal boom ["can have long-term negative effects on local populations because they are harvested in the wild,"](https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/science/scientists-uncover-the-unexpected-identity-of-mezcal-worms/) and the museum notes that collecting them often kills the agave.

The paper reports early work on rearing the larvae on agaves in greenhouses. Outside the bottle, they are one of the most popular edible insects in Mexico, the authors note, eaten for their flavor and their high protein and fat content.

## Mescal and mescaline share a word, not a drug

Mescal and mescaline sound alike, and in its main entry Merriam-Webster even gives [peyote as the second sense of mescal](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/mescal), after the agave liquor. The likeness invites a guess that the worm carries the drug, but **mescaline** comes from a cactus, and the worm is a caterpillar that lives on agave.

Peyote, Lophophora williamsii, is ["a cactus that contains various biologically active alkaloids"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38138432/), and mescaline is the one that produces its psychoactive effects, according to a 2023 review in Molecules. The same review dates the first study of the effects of ["mescal buttons"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10746114/) to 1887.

The PeerJ paper names the agaves the caterpillar feeds on, such as Agave americana and Agave salmiana, and nothing in it or in the review ties the larva to mescaline.

The gusano in "Abocado con Gusano" is a flavoring the mezcal standard names outright, bottled since about the middle of the last century, and in every bottle tested so far it has been the same moth. An authentic tequila bottle is the one place the tequila worm is sure not to be. Strictly FYI.
