In 1919, A.A. Stewart wrote a primer for printing apprentices, issued by the United Typothetae of America, and gave away the whole secret of the em dash in one line about blank metal. "An _em_ is a square of the body," he told them in [Typesetting](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/46113), "and varies in size with each size of type."

A square that changes size with the type is a measurement, not a letter. The trade even had spoken names for the two units that read like a butcher's order: Matthew Butterick's [Practical Typography](https://practicaltypography.com/hyphens-and-dashes.html) admits the homophony is confusing and records, "To disambiguate, loud print shops referred to them as mutton and nut."

## Why is it called an em dash?

Because the mark was cast **one em wide**, and an em is a unit of typographic measurement equal to the current type size. Stewart's [primer](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/46113) states the arithmetic without decoration, "Thus, an 8-point em is 8 points deep and 8 points wide; a 12-point em is 12 points deep and 12 points wide," and adds that "the em dash is one cast on a square body." The name records a width that scales with the text, which is the whole reason a unit was required at all.

## The em is a square of the type body

The unit has no fixed width of its own, which is what makes it useful for describing a mark that has to work at every size. It is centuries old: Joseph Moxon's printing manual [Mechanick Exercises](https://archive.org/details/mechanickexercis00moxo_0), printed in 1683, writes it as the bare letter, sorting letters into those "m thick", as thick as the body is high, and those "n thick", half that.

That square was once a thing you could drop on your foot. Butterick's [guide](https://practicaltypography.com/hyphens-and-dashes.html) describes the metal original: "In a traditional metal font, the em was the vertical distance from the top of a piece of type to the bottom. The en was half the size of the em." Stewart's apprentices learned the blank metal by name in his [primer](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/46113): "trade custom gives the name _spaces_ to the small type blanks and _quads_ to the larger type blanks"; the en quad, half the square, "is sometimes called the thick space"; and his glossary gives the dashes as the "mutton dash" and the "nut dash."

The square became a unit of pay as well. The bibliographer Ronald McKerrow's [Introduction to Bibliography](https://archive.org/details/dli.ernet.503054) explains that compositors' wages came to be reckoned by the number of ems they set.

## The capital M does not fill the square

The letter is not a coincidence. [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/em) traces the printing sense of em to "the square corresponding in dimensions to the capital M of that type," and its entry for [en](https://www.etymonline.com/word/en) dates the printing sense of en to 1793, defining it as "a space half as wide as an em." The alphabet supplied the label.

Then the label came loose from the thing. McKerrow, whose book first appeared in 1927, gave the usual origin story in its [early pages](https://archive.org/details/dli.ernet.503054): "From the fact that the letter m (or M) used to be cast on a square body, such a square space is called an em-quad." Nearly three hundred pages later in the [same book](https://archive.org/details/dli.ernet.503054) he narrowed the claim to "certain early founts of type" and added in a footnote: "Actually in ordinary roman founts the M also is narrower than its height and, I believe, always has been."

The measurements agree. Type designer Thomas Phinney added research done for a client to his [2011 post on point size](https://www.thomasphinney.com/2011/03/point-size/): "cap size was most usually 62%-78% of the em square, averaging right around 67-70%." A capital M set in 12-point type is not 12 points of anything. Butterick is blunter in [Practical Typography](https://practicaltypography.com/hyphens-and-dashes.html): "Em and en refer to units of typographic measurement, not to the letters M and N." He notes that "Originally, the width of the em and en dashes corresponded to these units," and puts today's mark at "typically about as wide as a capital H," with the en dash "about half as wide."

> The unit borrowed a name from the letter M and then stopped tracking it. What remains is a measurement pinned to the type size, so an em dash in a headline is physically longer than an em dash in the footnote below it.

| Term | What it measures | Printers' spoken name |
|---|---|---|
| Em | The square of the type body | Mutton |
| En | Half an em | Nut |
| Em dash | One em, in metal type | Mutton dash |
| En dash | One en, about half an em dash | Nut dash |

## Dashes reached English print by 1588

The dash has been on the English page for more than four centuries. The [University of Cambridge](https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/features/dot-dot-dot-how-the-ellipsis-made-its-mark) wrote up the literary scholar Anne Toner's research in October 2015 and reported that "the earliest ellipsis in Toner's case studies occurs in an edition of Terence's *Andria*, a play translated into English in 1588 by Maurice Kyffin." That mark, Cambridge records, "takes the form not of dots but a series of short dashes or hyphens (sometimes three, sometimes four), also known as breaks."

Whoever chose it, the author or the printing house, the page shows a row of short dashes or hyphens, and [Cambridge](https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/features/dot-dot-dot-how-the-ellipsis-made-its-mark) adds that "The mark that was chosen would often have depended on what a printer had available." About three centuries later, typists would arrive at the same solution.

## The typewriter deleted it and Unicode gave it back

On the typewriter the dash became two hyphens struck in a row. The [Correspondent's Manual](https://archive.org/details/correspondentsma00hickrich), a 1902 guide for stenographers and typewriter operators by the Boston shorthand teacher William Hickox, put the rule plainly: "Strike the hyphen twice to make the dash." Butterick still lists "Multiple hyphens instead of dashes" among the [typewriter habits](https://practicaltypography.com/typewriter-habits.html) he says were "left over from the typewriter era," so the substitute outlived the machine.

The guides that tell writers how to set the restored mark still disagree about the space around it. Stewart's 1919 [primer](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/46113) had already tied the space to the line: "The em dash usually should have a little space on each side of it, especially if the line is spaced with 3-spaces or wider," though in a thin-spaced line "the dash also may be set close to the adjoining words." Karen Yin's [AP vs. Chicago](https://apvschicago.com/2011/05/em-dashes-and-ellipses-closed-or-spaced.html) puts the two modern American rules side by side: in AP style an em dash "has a space before and after, except when used to introduce items in a vertical list," while in Chicago style "An em dash has no space before or after." Yin ties the newspaper habit to journalism's "space limitations and fast turnaround," since spacing the dash leaves copy "easily broken across two lines."

British practice splits again. The [Guardian style guide](https://www.theguardian.com/guardian-style-guide-d) rules that "Dashes should be en dashes rather than em dashes or hyphens," and sets its own examples with a space on each side. The [MHRA Style Guide](https://www.mhra.org.uk/style/chapter2.html#section_2_7) keeps the long dash for scholarly writing, describing the short dash ("en-rule") and long dashes ("em-rules"), "with a space on either side."

The dash got a permanent address in [Unicode](https://util.unicode.org/UnicodeJsps/character.jsp?a=2014) as **U+2014 EM DASH**, general category Dash Punctuation, in the General Punctuation block, carrying the annotation that it "may be used in pairs to offset parenthetical text." The Unicode Character Database [age file](https://www.unicode.org/Public/UCD/latest/ucd/DerivedAge.txt) dates the whole run of punctuation from HYPHEN to HYPHENATION POINT, the em dash included, to **Unicode 1.1**, with the caveat that "Versions are only tracked from 1.1 onwards." In [UTF-8](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3629.txt), the encoding that keeps every ASCII character to a single byte, the em dash takes [three bytes](/blog/what-is-a-byte/).

The metal went away and the square stayed. Phinney's [post](https://www.thomasphinney.com/2011/03/point-size/) describes what replaced it: "In digital type, the font's letters are drawn on a grid, where an arbitrary number of units (often 1000 or 2048) are set to equal the 'em' which is then scaled to the current point size for output." The square is now a yardstick rather than a box, since, as [Phinney](https://www.thomasphinney.com/2011/03/point-size/) puts it, "there is no necessary and required relationship between the size of the letters and the em."

## The chatbot story says more about the models than the mark

The em dash now circulates online as a fingerprint of chatbot prose. Writing in [The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2025/08/20/pop-culture/em-dash-use-ai-artificial-intelligence-chatgpt-google-gemini) on 20 August 2025, Brian Phillips dated the theory's viral spread to "around this past February" and answered it flatly: "I haven't seen any hard evidence that chatbots, in practice, use more em dashes than anyone else." He read the pattern the other way round, as a sign of "how reliant the AI companies are on the human writers they want to replace."

Measurements followed. A March 2026 [preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.27006v1) by E.M. Freeburg tested twelve models from five providers and found em dash rates running from none at all in Meta's Llama models to 10.62 per 1,000 words for GPT-4.1, which still produced 9.10 when told to write without markdown; the paper reads em dash frequency and suppression resistance as "a signature of the specific fine-tuning procedure applied." Some models lean on the mark and others never use it, so the habit belongs to particular models rather than to machine prose as a whole.

The [Emily Dickinson Museum](https://www.emilydickinsonmuseum.org/emily-dickinson/poetry/tips-for-reading/major-characteristics-of-dickinsons-poetry/) notes that she "most often punctuated her poems with dashes, rather than the more expected array of periods, commas, and other punctuation marks," in an age when "the dash was liberally used by many writers," long before there was a machine to blame for it. Set in type, her dashes take their name from a blank square of metal that almost nobody working today has ever held, sized in a unit that the letter on its label has never quite managed to fill.
