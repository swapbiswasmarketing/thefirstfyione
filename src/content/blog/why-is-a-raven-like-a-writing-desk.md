Bound into the front of later printings of *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland* sits a [preface "to the Eighty-Sixth Thousand"](https://archive.org/details/carroll-1898-alice-in-wonderland) dated **Christmas, 1896**, and most of it is printing business: fresh electrotypes from the original wood-blocks, new type, and a price cut for the Nursery "Alice". Its first paragraph is different. It points the reader to page 97, where a joke has been sitting unexplained for thirty-one years, and answers a riddle the book itself had declared unanswerable. Then its last clause admits the riddle was invented without one.

## Why is a raven like a writing desk?

Lewis Carroll's own answer, from a [preface dated Christmas 1896](https://archive.org/details/carroll-1898-alice-in-wonderland), is "Because it can produce a few notes, though they are very flat; and it is never put with the wrong end in front!" In the same paragraph he called it merely an after-thought and said the riddle, ["as originally invented, had no answer at all."](https://archive.org/details/carroll-1898-alice-in-wonderland) That answer came more than three decades after the book was [first published in 1865](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice%27s_Adventures_in_Wonderland).

## The Hatter has no idea either

The riddle arrives in Chapter VII of [*Alice's Adventures in Wonderland*, "A Mad Tea-Party"](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/11). The Hatter and the March Hare have shouted "No room! No room!" at Alice, she has sat down anyway, and the Hatter has told her "Your hair wants cutting." His next line is the riddle, and Alice likes her chances: "Come, we shall have some fun now!" she thinks, then adds aloud, "I'm glad they've begun asking riddles."

She never gets to guess. Her claim that she can find the answer sets off the March Hare's "Then you should say what you mean," and the table argues over whether saying what you mean is the same as meaning what you say. Then the conversation drops, and the party sits silent for a minute while Alice thinks over all she could remember about ravens and writing-desks, "which wasn't much."

When the Hatter finally asks whether she has guessed it, the exchange is short. "No, I give it up," Alice replied: "what's the answer?" The Hatter says ["I haven't the slightest idea,"](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/11) and the March Hare adds "Nor I."

Alice's verdict is that they might do something better with the time "than waste it in asking riddles that have no answers." The scene is a dead end: a riddle-shaped object with nothing inside it, handed to a child who expects riddles to behave.

## The note Carroll added three decades later

Readers did not accept the dead end, and by Carroll's own account they kept writing to ask, so the preface in the [1898 Macmillan printing](https://archive.org/details/carroll-1898-alice-in-wonderland) opens with his reply:

> Enquiries have been so often addressed to me, as to whether any answer to the Hatter's Riddle (see p. 97) can be imagined, that I may as well put on record here what seems to me to be a fairly appropriate answer, viz. "Because it can produce a few notes, though they are very flat; and it is never put with the wrong end in front!" This, however, is merely an after-thought: the Riddle, as originally invented, had no answer at all.

The first half is a double pun. A raven produces **notes** and so does a writing desk, and "flat" fits a croak that misses the key as well as a sheet of paper lying on a desk. The second half is weaker on its own: a desk never being put with the wrong end in front is a remark about furniture. It only becomes a joke once you know how Carroll first spelled one word.

The clause at the end matters more than either pun. **Carroll said the riddle, as originally invented, had no answer at all.** The 1896 line is correspondence management by a man who had been asked once too often.

## The pun that a correction deleted

In the winter of 1976, the Carroll scholar **Denis Crutch** reported in *Jabberwocky*, the Lewis Carroll Society's journal, that Carroll's preface as first printed spelled "never" as "nevar", which is **raven spelled backwards**. Martin Gardner's [*The Annotated Alice*](https://archive.org/details/annotatedaliceal0000carr_y4s7) relays the find that Carroll clearly intended the backwards raven and adds that the word was corrected to "never" in all later printings, "perhaps by an editor" who took it for a printer's error.

The Lewis Carroll Society of North America's [*Knight Letter*](https://archive.org/details/knightletterno113313lewi) gives the 86th-thousand reading as "nevar [sic]", and the later printings fit the account: the one quoted above and [the ninetieth thousand of 1903](https://archive.org/details/dli.bengal.10689.3336) both read "never". [Gardner adds](https://archive.org/details/annotatedaliceal0000carr_y4s7) that the original spelling was never restored, and that whether Carroll noticed the damage is not known. He died on [14 January 1898](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Carroll).

Read with the spelling restored, the second half describes itself: in "it is nevar put with the wrong end in front," a raven has been put with the wrong end in front. It is also a joke that cannot survive being read aloud. The notes-and-flats half works in the ear; the raven half exists only as ink, spelled one way, which is why a single tidy correction was enough to erase it.

## The Poe answer is a puzzle-book suggestion

The answer "because Poe wrote on both" is not Carroll's. It appears in [Sam Loyd's *Cyclopedia of Puzzles*](https://archive.org/details/CyclopediaOfPuzzlesLoyd), where the American puzzle-maker states that "Lewis Carroll never vouchsafed any replies" to the puzzles of Alice's trip through Wonderland, then offers a guess of his own about "the notes for which they are noted" and lists the Poe line among the answers that "might be suggested". The book came out in 1914, [published by his son](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Loyd) after Loyd's death in 1911, so its claim that Carroll never answered was nearly two decades out of date when it was printed.

The pairing was there to be made. Edgar Allan Poe's "The Raven" was printed in the [*Evening Mirror* of New York on January 29, 1845](https://www.eapoe.org/works/poems/ravenb.htm), twenty years before Wonderland. Nothing in Carroll's text or his preface mentions Poe.

## Huxley used the riddle to make a point about philosophy

In the September 1928 issue of *Vanity Fair*, Aldous Huxley published an essay called ["Ravens and Writing Desks"](https://archive.org/details/sim_vanity-fair_1928-09_31_1), and the riddle was his example of a nonsensical question. "If I ask, for example, why a raven is like a writing desk, I can either reply: because there's a b in both, or else: because there's an n in neither." The [popular retelling](https://archive.org/details/sim_boston-phoenix_april-18-24-1997_26_16) fuses those into one answer, "Because there is a B in both and an N in neither." Huxley offered them as alternatives, and that was [his point](https://archive.org/details/sim_vanity-fair_1928-09_31_1): "It really makes no difference. Each answer is equally true and equally false."

Both answers work by moving the letters out of the birds and furniture and into the words "both" and "neither". Huxley then aimed the trick at metaphysics, arguing that the classic riddles of the universe are questions asked [not about reality but about words](https://archive.org/details/sim_vanity-fair_1928-09_31_1).

| Answer | From | Year | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| A few notes, very flat; never put with the wrong end in front | Lewis Carroll | 1896 (preface) | The author's after-thought, disclaimed in the same paragraph |
| Because Poe wrote on both | Sam Loyd's Cyclopedia | 1914 | One of several answers a puzzle book said might be suggested |
| A b in both, or an n in neither | Aldous Huxley | 1928 | Two alternatives, offered to show a nonsense question takes any answer |

## The riddle was not in the first version of the story

The handwritten book Carroll gave Alice Liddell, *Alice's Adventures Under Ground*, was a [Christmas gift in November 1864](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice%27s_Adventures_in_Wonderland), and Carroll later published it in facsimile. The [1886 facsimile](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/19002) runs to four chapters. Much of the famous furniture is already there: the caterpillar smoking a long hookah, gardeners painting white roses red, croquet with live hedgehogs and ostriches, the Mock Turtle and the Lobster Quadrille. There is no Hatter, no March Hare, no tea party and no raven. Alice goes from the caterpillar's mushroom back to the long hall and on into the garden.

The tea party was [written for the published book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice%27s_Adventures_in_Wonderland), riddle and all. When Carroll finally supplied an answer, the cleverer half of it was in the spelling, and [that spelling lasted a single printing](https://archive.org/details/annotatedaliceal0000carr_y4s7).
