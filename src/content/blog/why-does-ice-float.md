Drop two cubes into a glass of water and look at where they settle. Each one rides with a thin sliver of itself in the air, and the sliver is about the same size every time, cube after cube, glass after glass. Almost nobody asks why the fraction never changes.

The fraction is the answer. A [University of Illinois physics answer](https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/23153) gives ice at 0.9167 grams per cubic centimetre against 0.9998 for water at 0 C, which makes ice about 8.3 percent less dense than the water it sits in, and since a floating object displaces its own mass, about that same share stands above the line.

Sea ice shows the same arithmetic at scale. Where floes crush together into pressure ridges, the [National Snow and Ice Data Center](https://nsidc.org/learn/parts-cryosphere/sea-ice/science-sea-ice) says, "most of the ice in a ridge is below the surface", because of the difference in density between the ice and the water.

## Why does ice float?

Ice floats because water expands when it freezes. In the liquid, molecules "move freely and slide past each other" and are "more tightly packed" than in the solid, the [LibreTexts chemistry library](https://chem.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Physical_and_Theoretical_Chemistry_Textbook_Maps/Supplemental_Modules_(Physical_and_Theoretical_Chemistry)/Physical_Properties_of_Matter/States_of_Matter/Properties_of_Liquids/Unusual_Properties_of_Water) explains; as water freezes they settle "into an open crystalline, hexagonal structure" held apart by hydrogen bonds, and the [volume goes up](https://van.physics.illinois.edu/ask/listing/16809) by about 9 percent. That makes **ordinary ice less dense than liquid water**, so it weighs less than the water it pushes aside and rises.

## The gaps in the lattice are empty

A popular version of the story has ice floating because it traps air. Ice needs no air to float. The [reference equation of state for ice Ih](https://iapws.org/technical-guidance/release/Ice-2009), issued by the International Association for the Properties of Water and Steam, describes pure ice and gives 916.72 kilograms per cubic metre at the normal melting point, the same density in different units.

Nothing is trapped inside that number, so a perfectly clear cube floats on the lattice alone.

## Several elements run the same trick

The classroom line says no other substance expands when it freezes. That one is folklore. Even [LibreTexts](https://chem.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Physical_and_Theoretical_Chemistry_Textbook_Maps/Supplemental_Modules_(Physical_and_Theoretical_Chemistry)/Physical_Properties_of_Matter/States_of_Matter/Properties_of_Liquids/Unusual_Properties_of_Water) hedges it correctly, calling water "one of the few substances whose solid state can float on its liquid state".

Bismuth and gallium do it in plain sight. The Royal Society of Chemistry's [bismuth page](https://periodic-table.rsc.org/element/83/bismuth) says that "like water, the solid expands as it freezes and it floats on the liquid", and its [gallium page](https://periodic-table.rsc.org/element/31/gallium) says the metal "contracts as it melts, rather like water".

[Plutonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutonium) "increases in density when it melts - by 2.5%". Liquid [cerium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerium) is "more dense than its solid form at the melting point", and [germanium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanium) is "one of the few substances that expands as it solidifies".

[Silicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon) is the closest relative, because it works by water's own method: "Upon melting, silicon contracts, as the long-range tetrahedral network of bonds breaks up and the voids in that network are filled in, similar to water ice when hydrogen bonds are broken upon melting."

The rosters disagree at the edges. The same [RSC gallium page](https://periodic-table.rsc.org/element/31/gallium) calls a solid that floats on its own liquid "a property shared only by a couple of other elements, Bismuth and Antimony", which leaves out four of the six above and adds a seventh.

Few people ever watch the others melt or freeze. Gallium comes nearest to everyday reach: the RSC's [fact box](https://periodic-table.rsc.org/element/31/gallium) puts its melting point at 29.7646 C, low enough that "on a hot day, you hold it in your pocket at your peril".

## Ordinary ice floats on a technicality

The ice anyone has handled is one crystal form out of more than twenty. A 2021 commentary in [Nature Communications](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8154907/) counted "twenty crystalline phases of water ice" as "now identified", and a 2025 paper in [Nature Materials](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12867761/) reported another, "which we call ice XXI".

Only one of them matters at the surface of the Earth: [ice Ih](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phases_of_ice), the form of "virtually all ice in the biosphere", stable "under applied pressures of up to about 210 megapascals (2,100 atm) where it transitions into ice III or ice II".

> Virtually all ice in the biosphere, the cube in your glass included, is ice Ih, one of more than twenty known crystalline phases of water. Keep squeezing past 210 megapascals and water hardens instead into phases reaching 1.31 and 1.65 g/cm3, heavier than the liquid they came from.

Past that pressure the numbers invert. The [IAPWS melting-curve release](https://iapws.org/technical-guidance/release/MeltSub) puts the pressures at which ice III, ice V, ice VI and ice VII each first freeze out of liquid water at 208.566, 350.1, 632.4 and 2216 megapascals, and the [measured densities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phases_of_ice) read the other way.

| Phase or liquid | Density (g/cm3) | Denser than liquid water? |
|---|---|---|
| Liquid water at 3.98 C | 1.00 | - |
| Ice Ih, ordinary ice | 0.9167 | No |
| Ice III, freezes from the liquid above about 210 MPa | 1.16 | Yes |
| Ice V, above about 350 MPa | 1.24 | Yes |
| Ice VI, above about 630 MPa | 1.31 | Yes |
| Ice VII, above about 2.2 GPa | 1.65 | Yes |

Ice that dense does not bob. None of it is hypothetical, either: a [2018 paper in Science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29590042/) reported that "ice-VII occurs as inclusions in natural diamond", where it "remains at pressures as high as 24 gigapascals", and ice VII is now recognized as a mineral by the International Mineralogical Association.

Further out, a 2014 NASA-funded model of [Ganymede](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/ganymede-may-harbor-club-sandwich-of-oceans-and-ice/), the largest moon in the solar system, has ice that can become "so dense that it is heavier than water and falls to the bottom of the sea".

Not every exotic phase is heavy. **Ice XVI**, made by pumping the neon out of a gas hydrate for five days, "has a density of 0.81 grams per cubic centimetre", which a [2014 paper in Nature](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25503235/) called "the least dense of all known crystalline water phases".

## Swap the hydrogen and the cube goes down

There is a second lever besides pressure. Make the ice from deuterium instead of ordinary hydrogen and it sinks in ordinary water, at ordinary pressure, a trick Theodore Gray wrote up as a cocktail-party bet in a [Popular Science column](https://web.archive.org/web/20071216010940/http://www.popsci.com/popsci/how20/a07160a72252c010vgnvcm1000004eecbccdrcrd.html). [Heavy water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_water) is "10.6% denser than ordinary water", a difference that "can be seen without equipment if a frozen sample is dropped into normal water, as it will sink".

The demonstration has a second half. Heavy ice melts at 276.97 kelvin, about 3.8 C, in [NIST's reference data](https://webbook.nist.gov/cgi/cbook.cgi?ID=C7789200&Mask=4), so a glass of ice-cold normal water, at 0 C, is too cold to melt it. Chemically, as [Gray's column](https://web.archive.org/web/20071216010940/http://www.popsci.com/popsci/how20/a07160a72252c010vgnvcm1000004eecbccdrcrd.html) puts it, D2O "is real water"; one heavier hydrogen isotope flips the result.

## Many frozen lakes stay warm at the bottom

The usual closing beat is fish surviving under a frozen pond, credited to ice floating. Buoyancy is only part of it. Liquid water hits its maximum density before it ever freezes, and a [LibreTexts thermodynamics module](https://chem.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Physical_and_Theoretical_Chemistry_Textbook_Maps/Topics_in_Thermodynamics_of_Solutions_and_Liquid_Mixtures/01%3A_Modules/1.23%3A_Water/1.23.2%3A_Water%3A_Molar_Volume) puts that peak at 3.98 C at ambient pressure. Water at 4 C is heavier than water at 1 C, so it sinks.

That is what layers a cold lake. A [2021 study of 19 ice-covered lakes](https://dspace.emu.ee/handle/10492/8000), published in Geophysical Research Letters, found that when ice forms on smaller and deeper lakes, it leaves "a thin buoyant layer of cold water (near 0 C) below the ice, which remains above a deeper 4 C layer". Floating ice then [insulates the water beneath it](https://chem.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Physical_and_Theoretical_Chemistry_Textbook_Maps/Supplemental_Modules_(Physical_and_Theoretical_Chemistry)/Physical_Properties_of_Matter/States_of_Matter/Properties_of_Liquids/Unusual_Properties_of_Water). The lid keeps heat in; the density maximum is what carried the warmer water down there.

Even the tidy version has fine print. The [same study](https://dspace.emu.ee/handle/10492/8000) found that "the entire water column can cool to" about 0 C "in larger and shallower lakes", and proposed calling the two regimes "cryostratified" and "cryomictic". Not every frozen lake keeps a 4 C basement for its fish.

So the full answer runs longer than "ice is less dense than water". Ice Ih, built from the lighter isotope of hydrogen and kept below about 210 megapascals, is less dense than water. Swap in deuterium and the cube sinks in ordinary water; squeeze it past that pressure and it turns into a phase heavier than the liquid. The cube in your drink floats on conditions you happen to live inside. Strictly FYI.
