Photographed with a flash in a dim room, a cat often stares back through two discs of [blue or greenish light](https://www.msdvetmanual.com/cat-owners/eye-disorders-of-cats/eye-structure-and-function-in-cats), while a person in the same shot can come out with red pupils. Both colors are the flash, bounced back out of an eye. A person's red comes mostly from ["a high concentration of blood in the choroid,"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red-eye_effect) the layer behind the retina.

The cat's glow comes from a reflective layer behind the retina, the tapetum lucidum, which [humans lack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapetum_lucidum). Eyeshine appears only when light shines into the eye, because a cat's eye makes no light of its own.

## Can cats see in the dark?

Cats cannot see in total darkness, where there is no light for any eye to use, but they see in light too dim for people. In a test reported in 1951, six cats **[detected light almost six times dimmer](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1392083/pdf/jphysiol01455-0021.pdf)**, on average, than two people could, and when no light was showing, they chose at random.

The MSD Veterinary Manual's guide for cat owners, by the veterinary ophthalmologist Kirk Gelatt of the University of Florida, says cats ["can see 6 times better in dim light than people, giving rise to the myth that cats can see in the dark."](https://www.msdvetmanual.com/cat-owners/eye-disorders-of-cats/eye-structure-and-function-in-cats)

## Six cats in a black box saw lights too dim for people

The test was the work of Ralph Gunter of the Medical Research Council's Vision Research Unit in London, [reported in *The Journal of Physiology*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1392083/) in 1951. [Six cats, four females and two males,](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1392083/pdf/jphysiol01455-0021.pdf) were released one at a time into a Y-shaped box painted flat black inside, where two doors each held a 3-inch-square panel of milk glass.

One panel was lit from behind. Dishes of milk stood behind both doors to even out the smell, but a cat could reach only the one behind the lit door.

Each cat needed [between 110 and 250 trials](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1392083/pdf/jphysiol01455-0021.pdf) to learn the rule, and before testing it spent an hour adjusting to the dark. Gunter then dimmed the light in steps until its choices fell to chance. With both panels unlit, the cats' choices were completely random, so they had been following the light and nothing else.

Two human observers took the same test, and "the animals' absolute threshold is lower by a factor of almost 6," [Gunter wrote](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1392083/pdf/jphysiol01455-0021.pdf). An earlier study he cited, by Bridgman and Smith in 1942, had found the human threshold ["higher by a factor of 7."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1392083/pdf/jphysiol01455-0021.pdf)

> "A lower absolute threshold was vividly illustrated in the present experiment where the cat gave consistently correct responses to an illuminated stimulus panel invisible to the writer, who was as dark-adapted as the experimental animals." - Ralph Gunter, [*The Journal of Physiology*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1392083/pdf/jphysiol01455-0021.pdf), 1951

## The cat's eye gathers more light and uses it twice

Gunter credited the cat's optics more than its retina. He granted that the cat's "higher rod-cone ratio" might help, but the tapetum, together with "the greater amount of light which reaches the cat's retina owing to the increased aperture of its dioptric system," could account for the low threshold, [he argued](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1392083/pdf/jphysiol01455-0021.pdf), so the human retina was "not necessarily any less" sensitive than the cat's.

A cat's pupil also opens and closes over a far wider range than a person's. In a [2015 study of pupil shapes in 214 land species](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4643806/), researchers at the University of California, Berkeley and Durham University, citing earlier measurements, gave the change in area of the domestic cat's vertical-slit pupil as **135-fold**, against about 15-fold for a human's round one. Species with vertically elongated pupils, [they found](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4643806/), "are very likely to be ambush predators and active day and night."

An older explanation for the slit is light control: animals active around the clock ["need to dilate sufficiently under dim conditions while constricting enough to prevent dazzle in daylight."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4643806/) The Berkeley and Durham team [called that idea "persuasive"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4643806/) but noted that it "only explains why some species evolved elongated pupils, not why they are vertical in some species and horizontal in others."

Behind the retina, the tapetum gives the light a second pass. It provides the light-sensitive cells with ["a second opportunity for photon-photoreceptor stimulation,"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14738502/) according to a 2004 survey of tapeta across species from the University of Florida's College of Veterinary Medicine, which found that the species it examined without one, primates among them, ["usually are diurnal animals."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14738502/)

## Cats see less fine detail than people

Bisti and Maffei, at the neurophysiology laboratory of Italy's National Research Council in Pisa, [trained cats to press a pedal](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1331081/pdf/jphysiol00921-0213.pdf) when a striped grating appeared on a screen 30 centimeters away, and reported the results in 1974. The training ["proved to be very laborious and took several months,"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1331081/pdf/jphysiol00921-0213.pdf) and it fully succeeded with only two cats.

Those two cats made out stripes up to slightly above **5 cycles per degree** of visual angle, a cycle being one dark and one light stripe, while one of the authors, tested in exactly the same conditions, [resolved about 30](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1331081/pdf/jphysiol00921-0213.pdf), though with coarse stripes the cats did better. That fit what Campbell, Maffei and Piccolino had concluded a year earlier from recordings in the visual cortex of cats: ["while the cat cannot see such high spatial frequencies as man, it can see lower frequencies better than man."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1350559/)

Bisti and Maffei read the gap as a different range rather than worse vision. The cat ["uses another range of spatial frequencies,"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1331081/pdf/jphysiol00921-0213.pdf) they wrote, "most probably matched to the distance at which the visual world appears to the cat more relevant."

| | Cat | Person |
|---|---|---|
| Mirror layer behind the retina | Tapetum lucidum | None |
| Change in pupil area | 135-fold | About 15-fold |
| Finest stripes resolved, 1974 paper | Slightly above 5 per degree | About 30 per degree |
| Types of cone | Two | Three |

## Cats see color, though a 1954 paper said they did not

A paper in the *Journal of Neurophysiology* in 1954 was titled ["Absence of color vision in cat."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/13163715/) Ten years later, Sechzer and Brown reported in *Science* that red-green discrimination by cats ["was demonstrated in a situation which effectively eliminated cues based on relative brightness."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14169338/) The cat's habit of answering to brightness differences, they suggested, ["may help to account for the failure of previous experiments."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14169338/)

Recording from cat retinal ganglion cells, Guenther and Zrenner reported in 1993 that every cell received input from a long-wavelength cone system [peaking at 550 nanometers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6576706/) under daylight conditions, but only 15% received input from a short-wavelength system, [peaking at 450](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6576706/). A small group of cells, 8% of the sample and all in the area centralis, the central patch of the retina, hinted at more, and the authors went on to ["postulate an additional input of a midspectral receptor system."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6576706/) The standard count is still two cone types, which makes cats [dichromats](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2781854/), like dogs and most other mammals, while people, like other apes and Old World monkeys, normally have three. To people, the shorter of those two peaks falls in [violet to blue light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_spectrum) and the longer in green.

In the dark-adapted cat eye, [92% of the ganglion cells](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6576706/) Guenther and Zrenner recorded responded through rods, and rods have [little role in color vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rod_cell).

The two discs in a flash photo are the flash coming back out after a second pass through the cat's retina. In a room with no light at all, there is nothing to send back and nothing for the cat to see. Strictly FYI.
