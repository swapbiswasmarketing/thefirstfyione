Photographed with a flash in a dim room, a cat often stares back through two discs of [blue or greenish light](https://www.msdvetmanual.com/cat-owners/eye-disorders-of-cats/eye-structure-and-function-in-cats), while a person in the same shot can come out with red pupils. Both colors are the flash, bounced back out of an eye. A person's red comes mostly from ["a high concentration of blood in the choroid,"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red-eye_effect) the layer behind the retina.

The cat's glow comes from a reflective layer behind the retina, the tapetum lucidum, which [humans lack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapetum_lucidum). Eyeshine appears only [when light shines into the eye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapetum_lucidum), because a cat's eye makes no light of its own.

## Can cats see in the dark?

Cats cannot see in total darkness, where there is no light for any eye to use, but they see in light too dim for people. In a 1951 test, six cats **[detected light almost six times dimmer](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1392083/pdf/jphysiol01455-0021.pdf)**, on average, than two people could, and when no light was showing, they chose at random.

The MSD Veterinary Manual's guide for cat owners, by the veterinary ophthalmologist Kirk Gelatt of the University of Florida, says cats ["can see 6 times better in dim light than people, giving rise to the myth that cats can see in the dark."](https://www.msdvetmanual.com/cat-owners/eye-disorders-of-cats/eye-structure-and-function-in-cats) The myth fails at its extreme, since with no light at all a cat is as blind as a person.

## Six cats in a black box saw lights too dim for people

The test was the work of Ralph Gunter of the Medical Research Council's Vision Research Unit in London, [reported in *The Journal of Physiology*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1392083/pdf/jphysiol01455-0021.pdf) in 1951. Six cats, four females and two males, were released one at a time into a Y-shaped box painted flat black inside, where two doors each held a 3-inch-square panel of milk glass.

One panel was lit from behind. Dishes of milk stood behind both doors to even out the smell, but a cat could reach only the one behind the lit door.

Each cat needed [between 110 and 250 trials](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1392083/pdf/jphysiol01455-0021.pdf) to learn the rule. Then, after an hour adjusting to the dark, it faced the panels while Gunter dimmed the light in steps until its choices fell to chance. With both panels unlit, the cats' choices were [completely random](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1392083/pdf/jphysiol01455-0021.pdf), so they had been following the light and nothing else.

Two human observers took the same test, and "the animals' absolute threshold is lower by a factor of almost 6," [Gunter wrote](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1392083/pdf/jphysiol01455-0021.pdf). An earlier study he cited, by Bridgman and Smith in 1942, had found the human threshold ["higher by a factor of 7."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1392083/pdf/jphysiol01455-0021.pdf)

> "A lower absolute threshold was vividly illustrated in the present experiment where the cat gave consistently correct responses to an illuminated stimulus panel invisible to the writer, who was as dark-adapted as the experimental animals." - Ralph Gunter, [*The Journal of Physiology*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1392083/pdf/jphysiol01455-0021.pdf), 1951

## The cat's eye gathers more light and uses it twice

Gunter credited the cat's optics more than its retina. The tapetum, together with "the greater amount of light which reaches the cat's retina owing to the increased aperture of its dioptric system," could account for the low threshold, [he argued](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1392083/pdf/jphysiol01455-0021.pdf), so the human retina was "not necessarily any less" sensitive than the cat's.

A cat's pupil also opens and closes over a far wider range than a person's. In a [2015 study of pupil shapes in 214 land species](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4643806/), researchers at the University of California, Berkeley and Durham University put the change in area of the domestic cat's vertical-slit pupil at [**135-fold**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4643806/), against [about 15-fold](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4643806/) for a human's round one. Species with vertically elongated pupils, [they found](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4643806/), "are very likely to be ambush predators and active day and night," and such animals "need to dilate sufficiently under dim conditions while constricting enough to prevent dazzle in daylight."

Behind the retina, the tapetum gives the light a second pass. It provides the light-sensitive cells with ["a second opportunity for photon-photoreceptor stimulation,"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14738502/) according to a 2004 survey of tapeta across species from the University of Florida's College of Veterinary Medicine, which found that the species it examined without one, primates among them, ["usually are diurnal animals."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14738502/) [A scallop](/blog/what-are-scallops/) focuses its image with a mirror rather than its lens; a cat's eye [focuses with its cornea and lens](https://www.msdvetmanual.com/cat-owners/eye-disorders-of-cats/eye-structure-and-function-in-cats) and saves its mirror for light that passed through the retina uncaught.

## Cats see less fine detail than people

In 1974 Bisti and Maffei, at the neurophysiology laboratory of Italy's National Research Council in Pisa, [trained cats to press a pedal](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1331081/pdf/jphysiol00921-0213.pdf) when a striped grating appeared on a screen 30 centimeters away. The training ["proved to be very laborious and took several months,"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1331081/pdf/jphysiol00921-0213.pdf) and it fully succeeded with only two cats.

Those two cats made out stripes up to slightly above **5 cycles per degree** of visual angle, a cycle being one dark and one light stripe, while a person tested in exactly the same conditions [resolved about 30](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1331081/pdf/jphysiol00921-0213.pdf). A year earlier, recording from the visual cortex of cats, Campbell, Maffei and Piccolino had concluded that ["while the cat cannot see such high spatial frequencies as man, it can see lower frequencies better than man."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1350559/)

Bisti and Maffei read the gap as a different range rather than worse vision. The cat ["uses another range of spatial frequencies,"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1331081/pdf/jphysiol00921-0213.pdf) they wrote, "most probably matched to the distance at which the visual world appears to the cat more relevant." The tapetum does carry a cost of its own: reflecting light back through the retina [slightly blurs the image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapetum_lucidum).

## Cats see color, though a 1954 paper said they did not

A paper in the *Journal of Neurophysiology* in 1954 was titled ["Absence of color vision in cat."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/13163715/) Ten years later, Sechzer and Brown reported in *Science* that red-green discrimination by cats ["was demonstrated in a situation which effectively eliminated cues based on relative brightness."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14169338/) The cat's habit of answering to brightness differences, they suggested, ["may help to account for the failure of previous experiments."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14169338/)

Recording from cat retinal ganglion cells in 1993, Guenther and Zrenner found input from a long-wavelength cone system [peaking at 550 nanometers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6576706/) in every cell under daylight conditions, but input from a short-wavelength system, [peaking at 450](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6576706/), in only 15% of them. Two cone types make cats [dichromats](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2781854/), like dogs and most other mammals, while people normally have [three types of cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_cell). To people, the shorter of those two peaks falls in [violet to blue light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_spectrum) and the longer in [green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_spectrum).

In the dark-adapted cat eye, [92% of the ganglion cells](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6576706/) Guenther and Zrenner recorded responded through rods, and rods have [little role in color vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rod_cell). The same limit leaves [a faint aurora looking gray to human eyes](/blog/what-causes-the-northern-lights/).

The two discs in a flash photo are the flash coming back out after a second pass through the cat's retina. In a room with no light at all, there is nothing to send back and nothing for the cat to see. Strictly FYI.
