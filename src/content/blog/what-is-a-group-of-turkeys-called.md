Watch wild turkeys cross a back road and they go one bird at a time, strung out from the field edge while the cars wait. Ask what to call the group and trivia lists answer rafter, a word with the ring of a medieval hunting term. It has not been found in print before 1908.

## What is a group of turkeys called?

A group of turkeys is called a **flock**, the word the Missouri Department of Conservation and the National Wild Turkey Federation use. Trivia lists add **rafter**, not found in print before a **1908** English bird book. **Gang**, an older American word, was in print by **1821**. No medieval list names turkeys, because the bird reached Europe only in the 1500s.

"Turkeys flock in winter," says the [Missouri Department of Conservation](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/wild-turkey), and a National Wild Turkey Federation (NWTF) biologist speaks of [winter flocks](https://www.nwtf.org/content-hub/how-turkeys-survive-winter-with-a-boost-from-conservation). Turkeys were a flock in print by **1730**, when Henry Fielding's farce [Tom Thumb](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_tom-thumb-a-tragedy-as_fielding-henry_1730) compared its tiny hero, parading captive giants, to "some Cock-Sparrow in a Farmer's Yard" that "Hops at the Head of an huge Flock of Turkeys."

## The turkey reached Europe after the word lists

Many of the famous animal-group names come from fifteenth-century lists, the best known printed in the [Book of St Albans in **1486**](https://archive.org/details/thebokeofsaintalbanswblades), the source of [an unkindness of ravens](/blog/what-is-a-group-of-ravens-called/).

Turkeys are [native to North America](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/wild-turkey), and people there had domesticated them long before Europeans arrived. The bird "was taken to Europe at the beginning of the 16th century," a [study of turkey genetics](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psj.2022.102311) in *Poultry Science* says, and Spain "imported a large number of turkeys coming from America."

Some of those European birds "later arrived in North America," the study adds, and crossed with wild ones there to produce the bronze turkey, "the ancestor of all commercial turkeys." Their descendants are the birds blamed every November for [a nap their tryptophan does not explain](/blog/why-does-turkey-make-you-sleepy/).

## Gang was an American word

On large tobacco farms a gang of turkeys had a job. An **1821** article on growing tobacco in the Baltimore farm paper [American Farmer](https://archive.org/details/americanfarmer2021balt/page/383/mode/1up) recommended raising turkeys to eat the worms: "The gang of turkeys is slowly driven regularly over every part of the field," and the planter "reckons the worm killing services of turkey above the value of his meat."

Hunters used the word too. In Davy Crockett's [**1834** memoir](https://archive.org/details/narrativeoflifeo00crocrich/page/119/mode/1up), a shot at a hawk flushes wild birds: "At this a large gang of turkeys rose from the cane brake, and flew across the creek." His friend then "fired on a large gobler, and I heard it fall."

In August **1895** a Hampshire wildfowler named Salter sent the London journal *Notes and Queries* seven "sporting names" for groups of birds, from a covey of partridges to a whiting of swans, and asked, "Can these be added to?" Readers sent back dozens, many copied from old books. [One reply](https://archive.org/details/s8notesqueries08londuoft/page/192/mode/1up), signed only "O." from San Francisco, proposed "a 'gang' of wild turkeys, as used by old hunters in our Western States."

Hunters still use the word today. The NWTF's [Life of a Longbeard](https://www.nwtf.org/content-hub/life-of-a-longbeard) says young males "usually travel in jake gangs."

## Rafter turns up in a 1908 bird book

Rafter does not appear in the longest list that came out of the 1895 exchange, when the lexicographer E. Cobham Brewer pointed readers to the new edition of his *Dictionary of Phrase and Fable*, where "nearly ten times seven are mentioned." Brewer's list, on [pages 901 and 902](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofphra02brew/page/901/mode/1up), runs from an army of soldiers to a tribe of goats, and gives a muster of peacocks but no word for turkeys.

The earliest printing that can be traced is in Herbert Knight Horsfield's [English Bird Life](https://archive.org/details/englishbirdlife00hors/page/405/mode/1up), published in London in **1908**, in a list of bird gatherings:

> "A nye or nide of Pheasants. A rafter of Turkeys. A muster of Peacocks." - Herbert Knight Horsfield, *English Bird Life*, 1908

Horsfield credits the list to "the old-time fowlers," who "appear to have coined a variety of terms to express gatherings of birds, some of which are now obsolete." He names no fowler and cites no book.

By **1930** a state wildlife journal was printing it as correct. [California Fish and Game](https://archive.org/details/california-fish-and-game_1930-04_16_2) reprinted a list from the magazine *Pacific Sportsman* that dismissed talk of "bunches or coveys of ducks" as "entirely wrong" and offered "the correct names of different gatherings of birds," including "A rafter of turkeys."

## The barn-rafter story is unverified

The explanation repeated online is that the term comes from farm turkeys roosting in barn rafters. Horsfield gives no reason for the word, and nobody has traced the barn story to an early source either.

"At night, they roost in trees," the University of Michigan's [Animal Diversity Web](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Meleagris_gallopavo/) says of wild turkeys, and the NWTF says poults take flight within two to three weeks and begin roosting in trees soon after. A farmyard turkey may well have perched on a beam, so the story is unproven rather than false.

## Hunters name turkeys by sex and age

The [Illinois Natural History Survey's hunting glossary](https://hunt.inhs.illinois.edu/hunting-trapping/wild-turkey-hunting/wild-turkey-sign/) gives the usual set of names, and the NWTF uses the same ones, describing poults that "develop into jakes and jennies."

| Name | What it means |
|---|---|
| Gobbler | Any male turkey |
| Tom | Mature male, 2 or more years old |
| Hen | Mature female |
| Jake | Immature male |
| Jenny or jennie | Immature female |
| Poult | Young turkey of either sex |

**Poult** is not reserved for turkeys: "Young turkey and other fowl are called poults," the Missouri Department of Conservation says. Illinois notes that jennie is "rarely used in hunting" and that young females are generally grouped with hens. Young males stay with their mother through the fall and young females into early spring, according to Animal Diversity Web.

The trivia answer has the shortest paper trail of the three. Flock was in a farce by 1730 and gang in a farm paper by 1821, while rafter waits until 1908, credited to unnamed old-time fowlers. Strictly FYI.
