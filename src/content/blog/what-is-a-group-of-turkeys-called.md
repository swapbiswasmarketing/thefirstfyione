Watch wild turkeys cross a back road and they go one bird at a time, strung out from the field edge while the cars wait. Ask what to call the group and trivia lists answer rafter, a word with the ring of a medieval hunting term. It has not been traced in print before [1908](https://archive.org/details/englishbirdlife00hors/page/405/mode/1up).

## What is a group of turkeys called?

A group of turkeys is called a **flock**, and a National Wild Turkey Federation (NWTF) biologist talks of the birds' [winter flocks](https://www.nwtf.org/content-hub/how-turkeys-survive-winter-with-a-boost-from-conservation). Trivia lists add **rafter**, not traced in print before a [**1908** English bird book](https://archive.org/details/englishbirdlife00hors/page/405/mode/1up). **Gang**, the hunters' word, was in print by **1709**, in John Lawson's [A New Voyage to Carolina](https://archive.org/details/newvoyagetocarol00laws). No medieval list names turkeys, because the bird reached Europe only in the [sixteenth century](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9758563/).

"Turkeys flock in winter," says the [Missouri Department of Conservation](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/wild-turkey). As a noun for the birds, flock was in print by **1657**, when Richard Ligon's [history of Barbados](https://archive.org/details/trueexacthistory00ligo) gave the planters' remedy for caterpillars in the potato slips: "to drive a flock of Turkies into the place where they are." By **1730** the word was a joke on the London stage, where Henry Fielding's farce [Tom Thumb](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_tom-thumb-a-tragedy-as_fielding-henry_1730) compared its tiny hero, parading captive giants, to "some Cock-Sparrow in a Farmer's Yard" hopping at the head of "an huge Flock of Turkeys."

## The turkey reached Europe after the word lists

Many of the famous animal-group names come from fifteenth-century lists. The one printed in the [Book of St Albans in **1486**](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/71266) gives "a Mustre of Pecockys" and [an unkindness of ravens](/blog/what-is-a-group-of-ravens-called/), but no turkey.

Turkeys are [native to North America](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/wild-turkey), and their domestication "began in Mexico between 200 and 700 BC," according to a [study of turkey genetics](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9758563/) in *Poultry Science*. The bird "was taken to Europe at the beginning of the 16th century," the [study says](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9758563/), and Spain "imported a large number of turkeys coming from America."

Some of those European birds "later arrived in North America," [the study adds](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9758563/), and crossed with wild ones there to produce the bronze turkey, "the ancestor of all commercial turkeys."

## Gang came out of the American woods

John Lawson, Surveyor-General of North Carolina, put the word in print in [A New Voyage to Carolina](https://archive.org/details/newvoyagetocarol00laws), published in London in **1709**. His journal of a thousand miles among the Indian nations begins "December the 28th, 1700," and on the road to one town his party passed "great Gangs of Turkies."

An English visitor who sailed from Plymouth for Maryland in October **1705** heard it there. His [account](https://archive.org/details/jstor-1834054) of the voyage, printed two centuries later in the American Historical Review from an anonymous manuscript in the British Museum, records a planter who "told me he mett with a gang of Turkies in the Woods." The visitor saw them himself: "I have seene mighty gangs of these Turkies in the woods as I have bin Rideing," he [wrote](https://archive.org/details/jstor-1834054).

On large tobacco farms a gang of turkeys had a job. An **1821** article on growing tobacco in the Baltimore farm paper [American Farmer](https://archive.org/details/americanfarmer2021balt/page/383/mode/1up) recommended raising turkeys to eat the worms: "The gang of turkeys is slowly driven regularly over every part of the field," and the planter "reckons the worm killing services of turkey above the value of his meat."

Hunters used the word too. In Davy Crockett's [**1834** memoir](https://archive.org/details/narrativeoflifeo00crocrich/page/119/mode/1up), a shot at a hawk flushes wild birds: "At this a large gang of turkeys rose from the cane brake, and flew across the creek." His friend then "fired on a large gobler, and I heard it fall."

In August **1895** the London journal [Notes and Queries](https://archive.org/details/s8notesqueries08londuoft/page/88/mode/1up) printed a query from a sportsman named Salter, writing from Basingfield near Basingstoke, listing seven terms for groups of birds, from a covey of partridges to a whiting of swans, and asking, "Can these be added to?" Readers sent back dozens, many copied from old books. [One reply](https://archive.org/details/s8notesqueries08londuoft/page/192/mode/1up), signed only "O." from San Francisco, proposed "a 'gang' of wild turkeys, as used by old hunters in our Western States."

Hunters still use the word today. The NWTF's [Life of a Longbeard](https://www.nwtf.org/content-hub/life-of-a-longbeard) says young males "usually travel in jake gangs."

## Rafter turns up in a 1908 bird book

Rafter appears nowhere in the 1895 exchange. The lexicographer E. Cobham Brewer [replied](https://archive.org/details/s8notesqueries08londuoft/page/191/mode/1up) that in the new edition of his *Dictionary of Phrase and Fable* "nearly ten times seven are mentioned." Brewer's list, on [pages 901 and 902](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofphra02brew/page/901/mode/1up), runs from an army of soldiers to a tribe of goats, and gives a muster of peacocks but no word for turkeys.

The trail, as far as it can be followed in digitized books, begins with Herbert Knight Horsfield's [English Bird Life](https://archive.org/details/englishbirdlife00hors/page/405/mode/1up), published in London in **1908**, in a list of bird gatherings:

> "A nye or nide of Pheasants. A rafter of Turkeys. A muster of Peacocks." - Herbert Knight Horsfield, *English Bird Life*, 1908

Horsfield [credits the list](https://archive.org/details/englishbirdlife00hors/page/404/mode/1up) to "the old-time fowlers," who "appear to have coined a variety of terms to express gatherings of birds, some of which are now obsolete." He names no fowler and cites no book.

The rafter keeps old company on that page. Its neighbors, the nye of pheasants and the muster of peacocks, are four centuries older: the [1486 list](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/71266) has "a Nye of ffesaunttys" as well as its peacocks.

By **1930** a state wildlife journal was printing it as correct. [California Fish and Game](https://archive.org/details/california-fish-and-game_1930-04_16_2) reprinted a list from the magazine *Pacific Sportsman* that dismissed talk of "bunches or coveys of ducks" as "entirely wrong" and offered "the correct names of different gatherings of birds," including "A rafter of turkeys."

## The barn-rafter story is unverified

The explanation repeated online is that the term comes from farm turkeys roosting in barn rafters. Horsfield gives no reason for the word, and nobody has traced the barn story to an early source either.

"At night, they roost in trees," the University of Michigan's [Animal Diversity Web](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Meleagris_gallopavo/) says of wild turkeys, and the NWTF says [poults take flight within two to three weeks](https://www.nwtf.org/content-hub/life-of-a-longbeard) and begin roosting in trees soon after. A farmyard turkey may well have perched on a beam, so the story is unproven rather than false.

## Hunters name turkeys by sex and age

The [Illinois Natural History Survey's hunting glossary](https://hunt.inhs.illinois.edu/hunting-trapping/wild-turkey-hunting/wild-turkey-sign/) gives the usual set of names.

| Name | What it means |
|---|---|
| Gobbler | Any male turkey |
| Tom | Mature male, 2 or more years old |
| Hen | Mature female |
| Jake | Immature male |
| Jenny or jennie | Immature female |
| Poult | Young turkey of either sex |

**Poult** is not reserved for turkeys: "Young turkey and other fowl are called poults," the [Missouri Department of Conservation](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/wild-turkey) says. [Illinois notes](https://hunt.inhs.illinois.edu/hunting-trapping/wild-turkey-hunting/wild-turkey-sign/) that jennie is "rarely used in hunting" and that young females are generally grouped with hens. Young males stay with their mother through the fall and young females into early spring, according to [Animal Diversity Web](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Meleagris_gallopavo/).

The flocks sort themselves the way the names do. Once fall and winter arrive, the NWTF's [Life of a Longbeard](https://www.nwtf.org/content-hub/life-of-a-longbeard) says, turkeys "flock up according to their peers," adult males, jakes and hens each in their own groups, and pecking orders are "sorted out, which minimizes fighting come the spring breeding season." [Animal Diversity Web](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Meleagris_gallopavo/) calls the winter groups bands and says that in some populations, "each band may defend a territory against other bands."

"During the winter, turkeys reduce their range, diminish their daily activities, and often form large flocks," the [New York State Department of Environmental Conservation](https://dec.ny.gov/nature/animals-fish-plants/wild-turkey) says, adding that they spend time in farm fields feeding on waste grain. Spring breaks the arrangement up: jake gangs that had been trailing the hen flocks split off to "travel as true jake flocks," the [NWTF](https://www.nwtf.org/content-hub/life-of-a-longbeard) says, and before most hens go off to nest, large flocks of hens can gather around one or a few adult males.

The trivia answer has the shortest paper trail of the three. Flock was in print by 1657 and gang by 1709, while rafter waits until 1908, credited to unnamed old-time fowlers. Strictly FYI.
