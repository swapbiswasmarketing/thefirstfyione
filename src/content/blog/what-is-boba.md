The chewy black pearls in a cup of boba are usually made from the starch of **cassava**, a root that a 1999 genetic study in PNAS found was likely domesticated [along the southern border of the Amazon basin](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10318928/). The name on the cup, by most accounts, was borrowed from a Hong Kong movie star's nickname.

## What is boba?

**Boba is the chewy tapioca pearls in bubble tea**, and the word is also used for the whole drink. A nutrition study published in 2017 describes the black pearls as ["usually made from cassava starch, sweet potato, and brown sugar"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5217910/), boiled into round balls. The drink is sweetened tea, usually with milk, often shaken with ice.

## The bubbles were the foam, not the pearls

Bubble tea had its name before it had pearls. In 1980s Taiwan, the Taipei Times explains, "bubble tea" was brewed tea sweetened with sugar syrup and [shaken through ice in a cocktail shaker](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/feat/archives/2018/11/13/2003704115) to chill it and throw up a head of froth, like the head on a pint of beer. Only later were chewy tapioca balls added, which gave the drink the name pearl milk tea.

> "Bubble tea originally referred to foam black tea." - Angela Liu, managing director of Chun Shui Tang, to [Marketplace](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2024/03/14/bubble-tea-economic-survival-national-identity-and-betrayal)

## Tapioca is cassava starch, and its route to Taiwan is unclear

Tapioca is made from the starch of cassava, a root also called manioc or yuca. A crop profile in Purdue University's NewCROP collection lists cassava as [native to South America](https://hort.purdue.edu/newcrop/duke_energy/Manihot_esculenta.html) and names tapioca among the products obtained from the manioc tuber, along with starch, flour and farina.

How the balls reached Taiwan is less clear. The Taipei Times says that fenyuan, as chewy tapioca balls are called in Chinese, ["somehow made their way to Taiwan from abroad"](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/feat/archives/2018/11/13/2003704115), possibly by way of China, and became an ingredient in sweet snacks at night markets.

Hanlin Tea Room still lets customers choose between the original white tapioca balls and the more common black ones, made with brown sugar, the Taipei Times reports. The pearls do not keep their texture long: at Chun Shui Tang, Angela Liu told Marketplace, they are [tossed out after three hours](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2024/03/14/bubble-tea-economic-survival-national-identity-and-betrayal) and a fresh batch is cooked.

Popping boba, the balls of juice that burst, are a different thing. A University of Delaware engineering lesson explains that they are [made by adding sodium alginate to a flavored liquid](https://sites.udel.edu/k12engineering/activities/hydrogels-in-the-kitchen-hydrogels-in-the-body), a polysaccharide from brown algae, then dropping the mixture into a bath of calcium chloride, which forms a thin gel skin that bursts when squeezed. A recipe from the ingredient seller Modernist Pantry runs it the other way, a method called [reverse spherification](https://blog.modernistpantry.com/recipes/popping-boba/): juice mixed with calcium lactate is dropped into a bath of sodium alginate.

## A cup with pearls has more calories than a cola

With the pearls in, the 2017 study measured a 16-ounce milk tea at [**299 calories and 38 grams of sugar**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5217910/). The authors note that their lab method, bomb calorimetry, "may slightly overestimate metabolizable energy." Their table sets the drink beside other sweet drinks of the same size:

| 16-ounce drink | Sugar (g) | Calories (kcal) |
|---|---|---|
| Milk tea with tapioca | 38 | 299 |
| Milk tea | 38 | 263 |
| Cola | 56 | 200 |
| Sweetened iced tea | 44 | 168 |

## The word boba is a film star's nickname

The name did not start on the drink. According to CNN, boba, now a synonym for bubble tea in general, [initially referred to the large black tapioca balls](https://edition.cnn.com/travel/bubble-tea-origin-history-taiwan-intl-hnk/index.html). By most accounts, CNN reports, the word boba was coined by a hawker in Tainan who borrowed **the nickname of the Hong Kong movie star Amy Yip**, which loosely translated means "champion of breasts," to tell the big black balls apart from the smaller fenyuan sold in tea shops.

Angela Liu of Chun Shui Tang tells a different version. She told Marketplace that the term appeared around the 2000s, when some chains upgraded to bigger pearls, and those pearls were called boba, the [nickname given to 1990s Hong Kong actress Amy Yip](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2024/03/14/bubble-tea-economic-survival-national-identity-and-betrayal), known for her large bosom. "So, using the name boba milk tea is kind of objectifying women," she said.

The two accounts agree on the actress and on the size of the pearl. CNN gives no date for the hawker, but Taiwan Panorama magazine, writing in 2012, put the naming [at the end of the 1980s](https://www.taiwan-panorama.com/en/Articles/Details?Guid=a8faaaff-6149-4e10-9501-45bf904b801d&langId=3&CatId=7), when Cao Meng Bubble Tea in Tainan renamed its large pearls boba, a decade or more before Liu's date.

Inside Taiwan, Marketplace reports, bubble tea is mainly called just "milk tea." Outside it, the 2017 study notes, the drink [has gained more popularity in the United States and in Europe since 2000](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5217910/).

In September 2004 Taiwan's defense ministry asked the public to [cut back on one cup of milk tea per week](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/taiwan/archives/2004/09/22/2003203860) to help pay for a **NT$610 billion** arms procurement budget. TVBS, reporting at the time, named the drink in the ministry's pamphlet as [pearl milk tea](https://news.tvbs.com.tw/life/475446).

## Two teahouses claim the first cup, and a court declined to pick one

The Hanlin Tea Room in Tainan dates its claim to **1986**. Its founder, Tu Tsong He, was in debt after a failed hot pot restaurant when he saw fenyuan at the Yamuliao wet market. The white balls in golden green tea reminded him, he told CNN, of ["my mother's pearl necklace"](https://edition.cnn.com/travel/bubble-tea-origin-history-taiwan-intl-hnk/index.html), so he called it pearl green tea, and his first bubble tea shop, Hanlin, opened in October 1986.

The bigger black balls he moved on to would not fit through the straws on the market, so his customers had to scoop them out with spoons, and he worked with a plastic factory to make custom straws. Taiwan Panorama tells it with milk tea instead of green tea and [dates his visit to the Yamuliao market to 1987](https://www.taiwan-panorama.com/en/Articles/Details?Guid=a8faaaff-6149-4e10-9501-45bf904b801d&langId=3&CatId=7).

Chun Shui Tang in Taichung tells a rival story. The Taipei Times gives its version as a [then-20-year-old employee, Lin Hsiu-hui](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/feat/archives/2018/11/13/2003704115), mixing her favorite childhood snack into iced milk tea in 1987. Lin herself told CNN it happened at [a staff meeting in 1988](https://edition.cnn.com/travel/bubble-tea-origin-history-taiwan-intl-hnk/index.html), and that the drink outsold the shop's other iced teas within a couple of months.

Each company insists on its own story, and neither has been proven. The rivals took each other to court, and according to CNN [a 10-year litigation kicked off in 2009](https://edition.cnn.com/travel/bubble-tea-origin-history-taiwan-intl-hnk/index.html) and **ended in 2019** with a ruling that bubble tea was a drink any person or shop could make, so it was unnecessary to debate who created it.

Marketplace reports the 2019 decision as a judge finding that bubble tea [is not a product that can be patented](https://www.marketplace.org/story/2024/03/14/bubble-tea-economic-survival-national-identity-and-betrayal). Because neither company had managed to patent or trademark the drink, the Taipei Times notes, pearl milk tea was already [on the menus of Taiwan's teahouses by the mid-1990s](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/feat/archives/2018/11/13/2003704115). The paper also offers a third guess: that the drink began in Taiwan's night markets, perhaps after a vendor tasted cendol, a sweet iced drink with gelatinous strands, during a trip to Singapore or Malaysia.

So boba is starch from a South American root, usually made with brown sugar and boiled into chewy balls, and named, by most accounts, after a Hong Kong film star. Two teahouses each tell the story of the first cup, and after ten years of litigation the court ruled that any person or shop could make it. Strictly FYI.
