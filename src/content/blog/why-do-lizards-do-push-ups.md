Halfway along the garden wall, a lizard stops, turns its head so that one eye is on you, and starts doing push-ups. Its chest rises on straightened front legs, drops, and rises again, a few quick lifts in a row, and then it holds still and watches.

Researchers have filmed that movement, graphed it beat by beat and provoked it with model snakes. It is a message, and in one Puerto Rican anole the message goes to predators as well as to other lizards.

## Why do lizards do push-ups?

Lizards do push-ups and head bobs to send visual signals. In anoles, males use them to hold a territory, challenge rivals and court, and females use them to accept or reject a male and in disputes with each other. Many anole species bob in a signature rhythm, and in field trials crested anoles did extra push-ups at a model snake, apparently telling the predator it had been seen.

- **Territory:** the assertion display, which a male gives as he moves around his territory, sometimes with no other lizard in sight.
- **Challenge:** a close-range display that rival males take turns giving, usually until one flees or they fight.
- **Courtship:** displays by males to females, and by females in reply, accepting or rejecting the male.
- **Predators:** push-ups by crested anoles at a model snake, apparently telling the predator it has been spotted.

Leo Fleishman of Union College describes the territory, challenge and courtship contexts in a [2026 review of anole vision](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13603757/). **Nearly all anole species** communicate with motion patterns, he writes: up-and-down movements of the head, push-ups made by flexing the front legs, and the spreading and folding of a throat fan. Most of the studies below are on anoles and on *Sceloporus* lizards, so they describe those groups rather than every lizard that bobs.

## The head bob is a species signature

When an anole bobs its head, much of the information is in the timing. In most of the anole species studied closely, [Fleishman writes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13603757/), the displays include a "signature display": a "species-specific, highly stereotyped temporal pattern" of up-and-down head and body movements, which appears to transmit the species' identity. In some cases the signature differs from lizard to lizard and may also identify the individual.

In [robot-lizard trials](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13603757/) with *Anolis grahami*, Macedonia and colleagues showed that the timing of the movements, like the dewlap's color, is an important cue for recognizing a lizard's own species.

The patterns can be drawn as graphs. A [2015 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4400845/) led by Emilia Martins shows species-typical displays as display action pattern graphs, adapted from Carpenter's 1978 comparison of display behavior across the genus *Sceloporus*, with the height of the head on the vertical axis and time in seconds along the bottom.

The genus includes the [eastern fence lizard](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8216924/), whose males carry vivid blue badges on the belly and throat. Across *Sceloporus*, [Martins and colleagues write](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4400845/), males show their colorful belly patches mainly in male-male combat, while head-bob displays are used "primarily in long-distance broadcasts of individual, sex and species identity."

## Crested anoles do push-ups at snakes

Some of those signals are aimed at predators. In [a 1997 study in *Animal Behaviour*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9398368/), Leal and Rodriguez-Robles exposed wild Puerto Rican crested anoles, *Anolis cristatellus*, to a model of a natural snake predator. The lizards responded in six ways: immobility, predator inspection, flight, a lateral face-off, dewlap extension and push-ups. They gave [**significantly more push-ups**](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9398368/) and push-up bouts when the snake model was present.

The results supported the pursuit-deterrent hypothesis. It holds that a predator's chances of a successful attack drop once its prey knows it is coming, so a prey animal that signals it has spotted the predator can discourage the attack. Because the anoles use the same displays on each other, the authors suggest predation may have reinforced sexual selection in shaping them.

In a [1999 paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10479367/), Leal staged more encounters in the field between crested anoles and a model predator and counted the push-ups. Lizards with more stamina did more of them: the count was **significantly positively correlated with endurance**, which can limit how well a crested anole escapes an attack. The display can potentially tell a predator how hard this lizard would be to catch, and Leal classes it as an honest signal.

> "[P]redation pressure and sexual selection may simultaneously favour the evolution of honest communication to allow both the predator and the potential mate or male rival to assess individual quality using the same signal." - M. Leal, [*Animal Behaviour*, 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10479367/)

Being seen has costs too. In the Bahamas, on small islands where the researchers had introduced a predatory curly-tailed lizard, male brown anoles made head-bob displays of **smaller maximum amplitude**, though the share of time they spent displaying showed no statistically significant drop, [David Steinberg and colleagues reported in 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4078856/).

Martins's study hints at a slower, evolutionary version of the same trade-off. In two *Sceloporus* species that lost the blue belly patch during their evolution, perhaps under pressure from visual predators or because rival males fought less, males moved around less but spent more time on head-bob displays. The authors describe these as signals that ["can be turned off in the presence of predators and other unwanted receivers,"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4400845/) and they add that more work is needed to confirm that predators drove the change.

## Wind and shade change how anoles display

Male crested anoles and *Anolis gundlachi* in Puerto Rico's Caribbean National Forest have to be seen by neighbors **up to 25 meters away**, through almost constant motion from windblown plants. Terry Ord and colleagues filmed them and found that [males facing more of that visual noise produced faster displays](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2124473/), while males in calmer conditions bobbed more slowly. Birds adjust their songs to noisy habitats, and the 2007 paper calls this the first evidence that animals change how they produce moving visual signals in noisy motion habitats.

In the deep shade of the Luquillo Mountains, *A. gundlachi*, the yellow-chinned anole, adds a preamble. [Ord and Judy Stamps found in 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2596256/) that in dimmer light, with more visual noise and with neighbors farther off, males opened more of their displays with **exaggerated four-legged push-ups** before the species' head-bob sequence.

Robot lizards programmed with those displays tested what the push-ups do. In poor light, with the robot at a typical neighbor's distance, real lizards turned toward it sooner when its display began with push-ups. In bright light the push-ups made no difference. [Ord and Stamps](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2596256/) call the push-up the "alert" and the head bob the "message."

Time of day matters as well. Counting displays through the day in four Jamaican anoles, *A. grahami* among them, Ord [found peaks at dawn, dusk or both](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18729743/). The males are inactive at night and return to their territories at dawn, and he draws the parallel with the dawn and dusk choruses of animals that call.

## The puffed-out throat is a dewlap

On an anole, the throat that puffs out is the dewlap, which [Fleishman's review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13603757/) calls "a colorful expandable throat fan." Its color tends to suit the light: species in darker, shaded habitats often have **yellow or white dewlaps**, while **red or orange dewlaps** are common where light is bright.

Some of the difference is invisible to people. Anole eyes have a class of cone cells sensitive to ultraviolet light. Crested anoles and *Anolis cooki* share part of their habitat in southwestern Puerto Rico, and their dewlaps look nearly identical to a human observer, but the crested anole's reflects ultraviolet and the other's does not, which, Fleishman writes, makes the two dewlaps easy for the lizards to tell apart.

## The studies do not test cooling or shedding

Popular explainers add three more reasons: push-ups fan cooling air under a lizard's belly, help it loosen skin when it sheds, or help it judge distance. The studies above, from Leal's model snakes to Ord's robot lizards, treat push-ups as signals between animals and test none of these ideas.

Whatever the lizard on the garden wall means, it is probably saying it in a rhythm its own species can read and a biologist can plot on a graph. For a crested anole facing a predator, even the number of reps is part of the message. Strictly FYI.
