When John Vaughan Thompson checked his glass of seawater on the night of May 8, 1826, two of the tiny swimmers he had collected a week earlier had shed their skins and, "wonderful to say," [he wrote](https://archive.org/details/zoologicalresear00thom), were "firmly adhering to the bottom of the vessel and changed into young Barnacles!"

The animal had first come up on April 28, 1823, in [a small muslin net](https://archive.org/details/zoologicalresear00thom) that Thompson threw from the ferry at Passage on his way home: a creature a tenth of an inch long that at rest looked like a tiny mussel, yet had eyes on stalks "as in the Crab and Lobster."

Thompson was an army surgeon who had [settled at Cork as district medical inspector](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography,_1885-1900/Thompson,_John_Vaughan) after service in the West Indies and Mauritius. His memoir on barnacles [came out in 1830](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography,_1885-1900/Thompson,_John_Vaughan), in the third number of his *Zoological Researches*, and argued from the larva and its change that barnacles belong with the crustaceans, the group of crabs and lobsters.

## What are barnacles?

Barnacles are **crustaceans**, sea animals related to crabs and lobsters, and NOAA counts [more than 1,400 species](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/barnacles.html) of them. A typical barnacle starts out as a swimming larva, then cements itself head-first to a rock, a ship or a whale and [stays there for life](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3364257/), inside a wall of hard plates, sweeping plankton into its shell with feathery legs called cirri. The group's scientific name, Cirripedia, [comes from](https://websters1913.timcieplowski.com/word/cirripedia/) the Latin cirrus, a curl, and pes, a foot, after those legs. The commonest kind, the acorn barnacle, grows its shell straight onto the surface, while goose barnacles [hang by a stalk](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8220330/).

## A barnacle spends its adult life upside down

The larva that does the gluing, called a cyprid, [cannot feed](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3364257/): it "is non-feeding and operates with a finite energy reserve," so it has to find a home before it runs out. It probes surfaces with a pair of antennae, and settlement involves "the attachment of the cypris antennules onto the substratum by a cement," followed by a moult and the change into a juvenile, according to [a 2021 genome study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8220330/) of the stalked barnacle *Lepas anserifera*, which describes adults "with their forehead fixed to a solid substrate and their body placed upside down."

In an acorn barnacle, [NOAA explains](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/barnacles.html), a white cone of **six** plates rings the animal and four more form a "door" that it shuts when the tide goes out, to hold in moisture; under water the cirri reach out to "comb the water for microscopic organisms," and the cement that holds the barnacle down is "among the most powerful natural glues known."

Most free-living barnacles are [hermaphrodites](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2600913/) that still mate, and a barnacle glued to a rock cannot go looking for a partner. It reaches instead: "For their size, barnacles possess the longest penis of any animal (up to eight times their body length)," a [2008 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2600913/) in *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* reported, adding that in most species self-fertilization "is rare or non-existent." One gooseneck barnacle of the northeast Pacific can also catch sperm from the water: in a [2013 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3574338/), every isolated animal tested had done so.

## Barnacles ride on hulls and whales

Moving hulls and whales are "particularly vulnerable," [NOAA says](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/barnacles.html). Barnacles can add more than **60 percent** to a ship's drag, the [Office of Naval Research said in 2009](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/library/news/2009/06/mil-090605-nns12.htm), adding that fouled vessels can need as much as **40 percent** more fuel to counter the added drag.

A [NOAA photograph](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/barnacles.html) shows barnacles crowding the blowholes of a California gray whale, and its description adds that barnacles are typical on gray whales and do not hurt them.

Some barnacles [live only on particular hosts](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1617185/), from sponges and corals to sea turtles and whales. In [a 2006 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1617185/), larvae raised from a whale barnacle, *Coronula diadema*, taken from a humpback whale stranded in Japan would not settle in plain seawater but did once a small piece of the whale's skin was added, which the authors read as a chemical cue from the host.

People eat barnacles too. The goose barnacle *Pollicipes pollicipes* is "the most heavily exploited intertidal resource along the Iberian Peninsula, due to its high market value and growing consumer demand," [a 2016 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4904244/) reported.

## Naturalists filed barnacles with the mollusks

Carl Linnaeus [placed barnacles with the mollusks](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/commentary/life-sciences/darwin-and-barnacles) in 1758, the Darwin Correspondence Project at Cambridge notes; Jean-Baptiste Lamarck set them between the segmented worms and the bivalves in 1809, and Georges Cuvier returned them in 1812 to the mollusks, as his "Mollusca Cirrhopoda."

In a memoir on barnacles, Cuvier granted that they had jointed limbs and a mouth with jaws, but partly because their shell "seems to be modelled after that of many Bivalves," he decided "to leave this Order amongst the Mollusca," in [Thompson's translation](https://archive.org/details/zoologicalresear00thom); Thompson found the confusion understandable: "That these animals should have been a great stumbling block to systematists is not much to be wondered at, considering the complete disguise under which they usually present themselves."

Thompson's own view "was by no means immediately accepted," [the Darwin Correspondence Project](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/commentary/life-sciences/darwin-and-barnacles) says: the *Penny Cyclopaedia* of 1837 still called barnacles a group "whose place in the system has occasioned much doubt and difference of opinion among zoologists."

## The name barnacle belonged to a goose first

The word barnacle [first named a bird](https://www.etymonline.com/word/barnacle), the barnacle goose, and it is not recorded for the animal itself until the **1580s**.

Gerald of Wales, [a royal chaplain whom King Henry sent to Ireland with Prince John in 1185](https://archive.org/details/historicalworksc00girauoft) as the prince's secretary, explained where the geese came from in his *Topography of Ireland*, completed in 1187. "I have often seen with my own eyes more than a thousand minute embryos of birds of this species on the seashore, hanging from one piece of timber, covered with shells, and already formed," he wrote, in [Thomas Forester's 1863 translation](https://archive.org/details/historicalworksc00girauoft). Because the birds were "not born of flesh," [he added](https://archive.org/details/historicalworksc00girauoft), in some parts of Ireland "bishops and men of religion make no scruple of eating these birds on fasting days."

The story outlived Gerald by centuries, and it reached even [one of the founders of the Royal Society](https://www.st-andrews.ac.uk/history/ssne/item.php?id=6599), Sir Robert Moray, who died in 1673. In [a paper](https://archive.org/details/jstor-101787) that the Royal Society's *Philosophical Transactions* printed after his death, Moray described a fir log on a shore in the Western Isles of Scotland hung with shells "having within them little Birds perfectly shap'd," each with "The little Bill like that of a Goose, the Eyes marked."

The real nests were out of sight: the barnacle geese that winter on the coasts and islands of northwest Scotland and western Ireland come largely from a population that [breeds in Greenland](https://www.bto.org/understanding-birds/birdfacts/barnacle-goose). [Thompson traced the belief](https://archive.org/details/zoologicalresear00thom) to "a fancied resemblance in the plumose members of the animal-inhabitant to the wing of a bird," meaning the feathery cirri.

## Darwin gave barnacles eight years

Thompson's *Zoological Researches* was one of the books on board when Charles Darwin sailed on the Beagle at the end of 1831, the [Darwin Correspondence Project](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/commentary/life-sciences/darwin-and-barnacles) notes. On the coast of Chile, Darwin found a barnacle that [burrowed into the shells](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2010) of Concholepas, a sea snail whose English names include the [barnacle rocksnail](https://www.marinespecies.org/aphia.php?p=taxdetails&id=395353), and in October 1846 he began work on it.

He planned only a paper on the creature he called Mr Arthrobalanus, which, his notebook recorded, had no shell and [only three pairs of cirri instead of six](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/commentary/life-sciences/darwin-and-barnacles). To understand it, he "had to examine and dissect many of the common forms," Darwin wrote in his [autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2010), and he worked on barnacles for the next **eight years**, ultimately publishing "two thick volumes" on the living species and "two thin quartos on the extinct species."

In his own summary of the work, he [proved](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2010) "the existence in certain genera of minute males complemental to and parasitic on the hermaphrodites," tiny males living on barnacles that were already both sexes, and he "discovered the cementing apparatus, though I blundered dreadfully about the cement glands."

By October 1852 he was "wonderfully tired" of barnacles, he told his [second cousin](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/letters/favourite-letters/be-envious-ripe-oranges-w-d-fox-may-1832) William Darwin Fox in [a letter](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/letter/DCP-LETT-1489.xml), adding that the only part of his first volume worth looking at was the one "on the sexes of Ibla & Scalpellum."

> "I hate a Barnacle as no man ever did before, not even a Sailor in a slow-sailing ship." - Charles Darwin to W. D. Fox, [October 1852](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/letter/DCP-LETT-1489.xml)

He was sure that the novelist Edward Bulwer-Lytton "had me in his mind" when he invented a Professor Long, author of "two huge volumes on limpets," [Darwin recalled](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2010).

Between Gerald of Wales and Darwin, the barnacle went from a goose's shell to a crustacean:

| Year | Who | What a barnacle was |
|---|---|---|
| 1187 | Gerald of Wales | The shell of a growing goose |
| 1758 | Carl Linnaeus | A shelled animal, filed with the mollusks |
| 1812 | Georges Cuvier | A group of mollusks, his Mollusca Cirrhopoda |
| 1830 | John Vaughan Thompson | A crustacean, argued from its larva |
| 1846 | Charles Darwin | A crustacean, described over eight years in four volumes |

Gerald of Wales looked at barnacles hanging from driftwood and saw unborn geese, and Cuvier looked at the shells and saw a cousin of the clam. It took Thompson's net and a week in a glass of seawater to show the animal inside: a crustacean, kin to the crab and the lobster, glued to its rock by the forehead. Strictly FYI.
