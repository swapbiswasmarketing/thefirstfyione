When John Vaughan Thompson checked his glass of seawater on the night of May 8, 1826, two of the tiny swimmers he had collected a week earlier had shed their skins and, "wonderful to say," [he wrote](https://archive.org/details/zoologicalresear00thom), were "firmly adhering to the bottom of the vessel and changed into young Barnacles!"

The animal had first come up on April 28, 1823, in [a small muslin net](https://archive.org/details/zoologicalresear00thom) that Thompson, a [British military surgeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Vaughan_Thompson) posted to County Cork, threw from the ferry at [Passage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passage_West), on Cork Harbour, on his way home. It was a tenth of an inch long and at rest looked like a tiny mussel, yet its eyes sat on stalks "as in the Crab and Lobster," [Thompson wrote](https://archive.org/details/zoologicalresear00thom).

From the larva and its change, Thompson concluded in a [memoir](https://archive.org/details/zoologicalresear00thom) that barnacles are "clearly referable to the class of Crustacea," the class of crabs and lobsters. The memoir [came out in 1830](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnacle).

## What are barnacles?

Barnacles are **crustaceans**, sea animals [related to crabs and lobsters](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/barnacles.html). A typical barnacle starts out as a swimming larva, then cements itself head-first to a rock, a ship or a whale and [stays there for life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnacle), inside a wall of hard plates, sweeping plankton into its shell with **six pairs** of feathery legs called cirri.

The group's scientific name, Cirripedia, [means "curly-footed,"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnacle) after those legs, and [some **2,100** species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnacle) have been described. The commonest kind, the acorn barnacle, grows its shell straight onto the surface, while goose barnacles [attach by a stalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnacle).

## A barnacle spends its adult life upside down

In an acorn barnacle, the cement comes from glands at the base of the first pair of antennae, so that, [as one description puts it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnacle), "in effect, the animal is fixed upside down by means of its forehead." The larva that does the gluing, called a cyprid, does not eat: it [tests surfaces with its antennae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnacle), attaches head-first with a cement it secretes, then glues itself down for good and becomes a juvenile barnacle.

A white cone of **six** plates rings the animal, and four more plates form a "door" that the barnacle shuts when the tide goes out, to hold in moisture, [NOAA explains](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/barnacles.html). Under water the cirri reach out and "comb the water for microscopic organisms," and the cement that holds the barnacle down is "among the most powerful natural glues known," [the agency says](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/barnacles.html).

Like [earthworms](/blog/how-do-worms-reproduce/), most barnacles are [hermaphrodites](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/barnacles.html) that still mate, and a barnacle glued to a rock cannot go looking for a partner. It reaches instead: "For their size, barnacles possess the longest penis of any animal (up to eight times their body length)," a [2008 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2600913/) in *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* reported, adding that in most species self-fertilization "is rare or non-existent." One gooseneck barnacle of the northeast Pacific can also catch sperm from the water: in a [2013 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3574338/), every isolated animal tested had done so.

## Barnacles ride on hulls and whales

Moving hulls and whales are "particularly vulnerable," [NOAA says](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/barnacles.html). Barnacles can add more than **60 percent** to a ship's drag, the [Office of Naval Research said in 2009](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/library/news/2009/06/mil-090605-nns12.htm), and the Naval Surface Warfare Center at Carderock estimates that fouled vessels can need as much as **40 percent** more fuel to counter the added drag.

A [NOAA photograph](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/barnacles.html) shows barnacles crowding the blowholes of a California [gray whale](/blog/how-do-whales-sleep/). Barnacles are typical on gray whales and do not hurt them, [the photo's caption adds](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/barnacles.html).

Some barnacles make their homes on animals: the whale barnacle *Coronula diadema* grows on [humpback whales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronula_diadema) and certain other whales, and laboratory experiments suggest its larvae are induced to settle by chemical cues from the skin of a suitable host. In [a 2006 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1617185/), larvae raised from a barnacle on a stranded humpback would not settle in plain seawater but did once a small piece of the whale's skin was added.

People eat barnacles too: one goose barnacle, *Pollicipes pollicipes*, is [a widely consumed and expensive delicacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gooseneck_barnacle) in Spain and Portugal, where it is called percebes.

## Naturalists filed barnacles with the mollusks

Carl Linnaeus [placed barnacles with the mollusks](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/commentary/life-sciences/darwin-and-barnacles) in 1758, the Darwin Correspondence Project at Cambridge notes. Jean-Baptiste Lamarck set them between the segmented worms and the bivalves in 1809, and Georges Cuvier returned them in 1812 to the mollusks, as his "Mollusca Cirrhopoda," [the project adds](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/commentary/life-sciences/darwin-and-barnacles). The mollusks are the clams, snails and their relatives, among them the [oysters that grow pearls](/blog/where-do-pearls-come-from/) and the [scallops, most of which swim by clapping their shells](/blog/what-are-scallops/).

In a memoir on barnacles, Cuvier granted that they had jointed limbs and a mouth with jaws, but partly because their shell "seems to be modelled after that of many Bivalves," he decided "to leave this Order amongst the Mollusca," in [Thompson's translation](https://archive.org/details/zoologicalresear00thom). Thompson's own view "was by no means immediately accepted," [the Darwin Correspondence Project](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/commentary/life-sciences/darwin-and-barnacles) says: the *Penny Cyclopaedia* of 1837 still called barnacles a group "whose place in the system has occasioned much doubt and difference of opinion among zoologists."

> "That these animals should have been a great stumbling block to systematists is not much to be wondered at, considering the complete disguise under which they usually present themselves..." - John Vaughan Thompson, [*Zoological Researches*](https://archive.org/details/zoologicalresear00thom)

## The name barnacle belonged to a goose first

In English, the word barnacle [first named a bird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnacle), the barnacle goose, in the early 13th century, and it was not applied strictly to the animal on the rocks until the **1580s**.

Gerald of Wales, a [royal clerk who went to Ireland with Prince John in 1185](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_of_Wales), explained where the geese came from in his *Topography of Ireland*, first circulated in 1188. "I have often seen with my own eyes more than a thousand minute embryos of birds of this species on the seashore, hanging from one piece of timber, covered with shells, and already formed," he wrote, in [Thomas Forester's 1863 translation](https://archive.org/details/historicalworksc00girauoft). Because the birds were "not born of flesh," [he added](https://archive.org/details/historicalworksc00girauoft), in some parts of Ireland "bishops and men of religion make no scruple of eating these birds on fasting days."

The story outlived Gerald by centuries. Even [one of the founders of the Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Moray), Sir Robert Moray, who died in 1673, reported of barnacles on driftwood in the Western Isles of Scotland, "In every shell that I opened I found a perfect Sea-Fowl," as [Thompson quoted him](https://archive.org/details/zoologicalresear00thom).

The real nests were out of sight: the barnacle geese that winter in western Ireland and the Hebrides [breed in eastern Greenland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnacle_goose), and the myth owed its long life to "an early ignorance of the migration patterns of geese," as [one account of the myth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnacle_goose_myth) puts it. Thompson [traced the belief](https://archive.org/details/zoologicalresear00thom) to "a fancied resemblance in the plumose members of the animal-inhabitant to the wing of a bird," meaning the feathery cirri.

## Darwin gave barnacles eight years

Thompson's *Zoological Researches* was one of the books on board when Charles Darwin sailed on the Beagle at the end of 1831, the [Darwin Correspondence Project](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/commentary/life-sciences/darwin-and-barnacles) notes. On the coast of Chile, Darwin found a tiny barnacle that burrowed into the shells of [Concholepas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concholepas), a predatory sea snail, and in October 1846 he [began work](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2010) on it.

He planned only a paper on the creature he called Mr Arthrobalanus, [the project records](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/commentary/life-sciences/darwin-and-barnacles). To understand it, he "had to examine and dissect many of the common forms," Darwin wrote in his [autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2010), and he worked on barnacles for the next **eight years**, ultimately publishing "two thick volumes" on the living species and "two thin quartos on the extinct species." In his own summary of the work, he [proved](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2010) "the existence in certain genera of minute males complemental to and parasitic on the hermaphrodites," tiny males living on barnacles that were already both sexes.

By October 1852 he was "wonderfully tired" of barnacles, he told his [second cousin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Darwin_Fox) William Darwin Fox in [a letter](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/letter/DCP-LETT-1489.xml), adding, "I hate a Barnacle as no man ever did before, not even a Sailor in a slow-sailing ship." He was sure that the novelist Edward Bulwer-Lytton "had me in his mind" when he invented a Professor Long, author of "two huge volumes on limpets," [Darwin recalled](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2010), limpets being [aquatic snails with cone-shaped shells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limpet).

Between Gerald of Wales and Darwin, the barnacle went from a goose's shell to a crustacean:

| Year | Who | What a barnacle was |
|---|---|---|
| 1188 | Gerald of Wales | The shell of a growing goose |
| 1758 | Carl Linnaeus | A shelled animal, filed with the mollusks |
| 1812 | Georges Cuvier | A group of mollusks, his Mollusca Cirrhopoda |
| 1830 | John Vaughan Thompson | A crustacean, argued from its larva |
| 1846-1854 | Charles Darwin | A crustacean, described in four volumes |

Gerald of Wales looked at barnacles hanging from driftwood and saw more than a thousand unborn geese, and Cuvier looked at the shells and saw a cousin of the clam. It took Thompson's net and a week in a glass of seawater to show the animal inside: a crustacean, kin to the crab and the lobster, glued to its rock by the forehead. Strictly FYI.
