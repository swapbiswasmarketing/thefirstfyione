**Fifty-one years** separate a London newspaper of 1798, where ["cocktail" as a drink is first recorded](https://wordhistories.net/2019/09/17/cocktail-second-occurrences/), from the low end of the dates given for the New Orleans product at the centre of the story most people get told about the word. [Peychaud's Bitters were created between 1849 and 1857](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peychaud%27s_Bitters). The name was half a century old before the bitters existed.

The answer that does work has nothing to do with bars. It comes from a horse market, and from something people did to horses that most drinkers would rather not picture.

## Why are cocktails called cocktails?

Because "cocktail" was a horse word first: [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/cocktail) records it for a horse with its tail docked. Word historians including [John Ayto](https://www.etymonline.com/word/cocktail) and [Anatoly Liberman](https://blog.oup.com/2007/03/etymological_co/) derive the drink from that docked horse, which was not a thoroughbred. Drinks historian David Wondrich [traces it to the ginger trick of the horse trade](https://www.grubstreet.com/2015/04/david-wondrich-knows-the-origins-of-cocktail.html), which made a horse cock its tail up. On his reading, the drink is named for what it was meant to do to you.

## The horse came first, docked or gingered

[Merriam-Webster dates "cocktail" for a horse with its tail docked to 1781](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/cocktail), seventeen years before any known drink carried the name. John Ayto's Diner's Dictionary, as [the Online Etymology Dictionary reports](https://www.etymonline.com/word/cocktail), derives the drink from that horse, because the word went on to mean a horse of mixed rather than thoroughbred pedigree, and from there, it is surmised, a mixture.

Etymologist Anatoly Liberman reached the same place in [a 2007 OUPblog column](https://blog.oup.com/2007/03/etymological_co/) summarizing work by a Swedish scholar in 1946, a Belgian in 1960 and a Cornell professor in 1978: "It was customary to dock the tails of horses that were not thoroughbred (for example, of hunters' and stagecoach horses). They were called cocktailed horses, later simply cocktails." His verdict: ["the suggestion by the three scholars seems to be right."](https://blog.oup.com/2007/03/etymological_co/)

Alongside that came the trick. [Gingering, historically called feaguing, meant inserting a piece of ginger, onion, pepper or tobacco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gingering) under a horse's tail so the animal carried it high and looked younger and livelier than it really was. Francis Grose's [1796 Classical Dictionary of the Vulgar Tongue](https://archive.org/details/aclassicaldicti01grosgoog) defines "to feague a horse" as "to put ginger up a horse's fundament, and formerly, as it is said, a live eel, to make him lively and carry his tail well".

Grose adds a piece of trade hearsay: ["it is said, a forfeit is incurred by any horse-dealer's servant, who shall shew a horse without first feaguing him."](https://archive.org/details/aclassicaldicti01grosgoog) This was fraud at the point of sale, practised on the animal and aimed at the buyer. [Nearly all US horse show organisations now forbid it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gingering) and can disqualify a horse that has been treated this way.

## A London newspaper printed the giveaway in 1798

The earliest known mention of a drink called cock-tail is [20 March 1798, in London's Morning Post and Gazetteer](https://wordhistories.net/2019/09/15/cocktail-first-occurrence/), in a satirical item headed "Old Scores". It lists the drinks the nobility and gentry supposedly owed a publican at the corner of Downing Street, who on winning a lottery prize had [washed out all scores with a mop](https://wordhistories.net/2019/09/15/cocktail-first-occurrence/), and one of Mr. Pitt's lines reads: "cock-tail (vulgarly called ginger)".

> A London paper in 1798 put the word ginger in brackets next to the word cocktail, eight years before anyone in America bothered to define it. That bracket is the closest thing this question has to a confession.

Not every word historian reads the bracket as a horse. Pascal Treguer, who transcribed the item, suggests the drink may have been called cock-tail [because the noun ginger was at that time used to denote a cock with red plumage](https://wordhistories.net/2019/09/15/cocktail-first-occurrence/), a term Grose traced to the cockpit.

Wondrich, who [has a Ph.D. in Comparative Literature from NYU and spent nine years as editor in chief](https://www.epicurious.com/shopping/the-oxford-companion-to-spirits-and-cocktails-will-answer-every-question-youve-ever-had-about-cocktails-article) of the Oxford Companion to Spirits and Cocktails, takes the horse side. ["I actually know where 'cocktail' came from, pretty solidly,"](https://www.grubstreet.com/2015/04/david-wondrich-knows-the-origins-of-cocktail.html) he told Grub Street in 2015, describing how a dealer with an old horse to sell would put ginger into it so "it would cock its tail up and be frisky." In Imbibe!, his version of the drink is [something that cocks up your tail](https://www.etymonline.com/word/cocktail). The drink, he went on, ["became this morning thing,"](https://www.grubstreet.com/2015/04/david-wondrich-knows-the-origins-of-cocktail.html) an eye-opener, and he ended the answer "almost positive that's where it's from."

## The first definition came from a man Hamilton had defended

The [earliest definition](https://wordhistories.net/2019/09/19/cocktail-earliest-definition/) ran on 13 May 1806 in [The Balance and Columbian Repository](https://archive.org/details/sim_balance-and-state-journal_1806-05-13_5_19), a newspaper printed by Harry Croswell in Hudson, New York, after a reader wrote in about the previous week's paper. That issue had mocked a democratic candidate's [account of Loss and Gain](https://archive.org/details/sim_balance-and-state-journal_1806-05-06_5_18), which, in [Treguer's transcription](https://wordhistories.net/2019/09/19/cocktail-earliest-definition/), runs from 720 rum grogs down to "25 do. cock-tail" and "My Election", against a Gain column reading "NOTHING".

The puzzled subscriber, who had heard of phlegm-cutters and fog drivers but [never "did I hear of cock tail before"](https://wordhistories.net/2019/09/19/cocktail-earliest-definition/), asked the question this entry is about: "Is the name expressive of the effect which the drink has on a particular part of the body?" The editor answered that a cock-tail is [a stimulating liquor, composed of spirits of any kind, sugar, water, and bitters](https://wordhistories.net/2019/09/19/cocktail-earliest-definition/), vulgarly called bittered sling, and supposedly an excellent electioneering potion, because it "renders the heart stout and bold, at the same time that it fuddles the head."

Croswell knew what print could cost him. Two years earlier, after his paper The Wasp accused President Jefferson of paying for attacks on his opponents, [Alexander Hamilton argued his appeal in a six-hour closing argument](https://history.nycourts.gov/case/people-v-croswell/), and on April 6, 1805 the New York legislature made truth a defence to a libel charge ["where published with good motive and for justifiable ends."](https://history.nycourts.gov/case/people-v-croswell/)

Those four ingredients never left. Order an Old Fashioned and you are ordering [the 1806 definition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_fashioned_%28cocktail%29) under a name that grew out of drinkers asking for something simpler than the bar had become. As David Wondrich points out, the Chicago Daily Tribune [was already discussing "old fashioned cocktails" in February of 1880](https://cincinnaticocktails.wordpress.com/2010/07/20/in-the-beginning/).

## The New Orleans egg cup story fails on dates

The popular version goes like this: Antoine Amedee Peychaud, a New Orleans apothecary, served brandy and bitters in a French egg cup, a coquetier, and English speakers slurred the word into "cocktail". [Bartender Dale DeGroff is the modern name attached to it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocktail).

The calendar finishes the job. Even at the early end of [the 1849 to 1857 dating for Peychaud's bitters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peychaud%27s_Bitters), the word had been in print in London for fifty-one years and defined in a New York newspaper for forty-three. The second-earliest known use, in [The Farmer's Cabinet of 28 April 1803](https://wordhistories.net/2019/09/17/cocktail-second-occurrences/), comes from a New Hampshire paper's purported diary of a lounger, who at 11 in the morning "Drank a glass of cocktail - excellent for the head".

## Ranking the stories by what actually backs them

| Story | Earliest hard evidence for it |
|---|---|
| Docked-tail horse | "Cocktail" used of a horse by 1781, before any drink use |
| Ginger under the tail | The 1798 newspaper gloss "vulgarly called ginger" |
| New Orleans egg cup | Peychaud's Bitters, dated 1849 to 1857 |

The usual answer to this question is a list rather than a verdict. [One roundup runs six theories plus Betsy Flanagan's feathers](https://www.saltdogslims.com/why-are-cocktails-called-cocktails/) - an Aztec princess named Xochitl, the French egg cup, a Spanish cola de gallo, the cock-tailings drained from a cask, cock ale - then calls the docked horse the most plausible and signs off with "we don't know".

If Wondrich is right, the word has been carrying its own instructions the whole time. A cocktail was a stimulating liquor, taken at eleven in the morning, excellent for the head, and named for a trick that made an old horse look lively on the way to being sold. Strictly FYI.
