Burn a candle to the end and audit the scene. A few grams of stub, a spent bit of wick, maybe a thin ring of residue in the jar. The other two hundred grams of wax, say, are gone - no puddle on the shelf, no stain, no drips. Over a week or two of quiet evenings, most of a physical object left the room, and nobody saw it go.

## Where does the wax go?

The wax becomes gas. A candle flame melts the wax below it, pulls the liquid up the wick, vaporizes it and burns it, and the burning [releases light, heat, carbon dioxide and water vapor](https://www.mcgill.ca/oss/article/student-contributors-you-asked-general-science/candles-what-do-they-emit-when-lit), as McGill University's Office for Science and Society puts it. Both gases are invisible. The wax disperses into the air around you, molecule by molecule, for as long as the flame stays lit.

Which means a candle is stranger than it looks: it is a device for slowly converting a solid block into the atmosphere of your living room.

## The wick only delivers the fuel

The most common candle wax is paraffin, a **hydrocarbon**, and a [fire-research study by NIST](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=101159) gives its formula as CnH2n+2, where n runs from 19 to 36 and averages 25. Each molecule is a chain of carbon atoms wrapped in hydrogen, and both elements burn readily.

The process runs in a [self-sustaining loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candle):

- The flame's heat **melts** a shallow pool of wax at the top of the candle.
- **Capillary action** draws the liquid up through the braided wick.
- Near the flame, the heat **vaporizes** the liquid wax.
- The vapor meets **oxygen** from the air and burns, giving off heat, light, water vapor and carbon dioxide.

That heat melts more wax, which feeds more vapor, which makes more heat. Michael Faraday, who lectured on this loop for children at the Royal Institution, explained the second step to them plainly: ["It is by what is called capillary attraction that the fuel is conveyed to the part where combustion goes on."](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/14474)

So the wick is plumbing. What burns is wax vapor, and you can prove it at home. Blow a candle out gently and hold a lit match in the rising white wisp a little above the wick: the flame runs down the vapor and relights it without touching it. Faraday did this with a lighted taper held "two or three inches from the wick," promising his audience ["a train of fire going through the air till it reaches the candle."](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/14474)

He went further. With a glass tube held in the middle of the flame he drew off the unburned vapor and lit it at the far end, joking that ["we can actually lay on a candle"](https://archive.org/details/courseofsixlectu00fara) the way a city lays on gas.

## The exhaust weighs more than the candle

The chemistry also says how much comes out. Take the average paraffin molecule in the [NIST paper](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=101159), C25H52, and balance the burning: one molecule takes 38 molecules of oxygen and gives 25 of carbon dioxide and 26 of water. Weighed with standard atomic masses, every gram of wax becomes about **3.1 grams of carbon dioxide and 1.3 grams of water**.

That is 4.4 grams of products from 1 gram of fuel, and the difference is oxygen pulled from the room, about 3.4 grams of it for every gram of wax. A 200-gram candle, like the one in the opening, burns into roughly 620 grams of carbon dioxide and 270 grams of water, more than four times its own weight.

> "They go off into the air as a perfectly invisible substance, about which we shall know hereafter." - Michael Faraday on the carbon in a candle flame, *The Chemical History of a Candle*

The water is the part nobody believes, and Faraday's test for it needs only a kitchen drawer. Hold a spoon that has been in the cold air over a candle, he told his audience, and it ["becomes dim"](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/14474) with condensation; in the next lecture he collected the condensate and announced that "that part of the product is just water."

## The flame radiates only about a sixth of its heat

A candle is a small heater as well as a lamp. In the NIST measurements, a white paraffin taper 305 millimeters long settled into burning [0.105 grams of wax a minute, from which they calculated a heat release of 77 watts](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=101159), give or take 9. By the arithmetic above, that is about 20 grams of carbon dioxide and 8 grams of water every hour.

Only a modest share of that heat travels outward as radiation. The same team measured the flame's [radiative fraction at 0.17](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=101159), about one-sixth, and reported that above the visible flame convective heat transfer apparently took over, presumably carried by the plume of hot combustion products.

## Soot is unburned carbon, and it makes the light

The light of a candle flame comes from solid carbon. Faraday told his audience that the flame's heat [breaks down the wax vapor and frees carbon particles](https://archive.org/details/courseofsixlectu00fara), which rise up hot and glowing. The [visible yellow color is due to radiative emission from hot soot particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candle), while the faint blue at the base comes from chemiluminescence. In a steady flame those glowing particles burn away before they can leave; a flame that grows longer than its [laminar smoke point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candle) emits soot.

When the flame is disturbed, some escape. In a 2021 chamber study in the journal *Indoor Air*, candles made to burn in fluctuating air gave off particle emissions ["dominated by soot (black carbon; BC)"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34297865/). That is the dark wisp from a candle in a draft: same fuel, same wick, and some carbon leaving before combustion could finish.

## Faraday built a whole course out of this

None of this is a new observation. Faraday gave the Royal Institution's Christmas Lectures on [The Chemical History of a Candle in 1848 and again in 1860](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Institution_Christmas_Lectures), and the lectures were [first printed as a book in 1861](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chemical_History_of_a_Candle). His opening claim was that ["there is no more open door by which you can enter into the study of natural philosophy, than by considering the physical phenomena of a candle."](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/14474)

He ended the course where this entry ends. His sixth and closing lecture takes up ["respiration and its analogy to the burning of a candle"](https://archive.org/details/courseofsixlectu00fara), because a body turns carbon into carbonic acid, his name for carbon dioxide, much as the flame does.

So, where does the wax go? Into the air, as water and carbon dioxide, some of which you inhale. Every candlelit dinner involves, in the most literal chemical sense, breathing the centerpiece. Strictly FYI.
