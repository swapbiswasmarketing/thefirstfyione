Every moment of the day is meant to be either a.m. or p.m., and one moment refuses. At noon the time is not before midday and not after it, so the [US National Institute of Standards and Technology](https://www.nist.gov/pml/time-and-frequency-division/times-day-faqs) (NIST) concludes that "a designation of either a.m. or p.m. is incorrect." Midnight has the opposite problem: twelve hours from noon in both directions, it is as much after one midday as before the next.

The everyday rule papers over the gap by giving midnight to a.m. and noon to p.m., and not every authority has followed it. In its [**2000** edition](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2000/pdf/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2000-13.pdf), the US government's own style manual called noon 12 a.m. and midnight 12 p.m., the reverse of today's convention.

## What does AM stand for?

AM stands for **ante meridiem**, Latin for ["before noon,"](https://www.etymonline.com/word/a.m.) and PM stands for **post meridiem**, ["after noon."](https://www.etymonline.com/word/p.m.) A.m. covers the twelve hours from midnight to noon and p.m. the twelve from noon to midnight. By everyday convention, [**12 a.m. is midnight and 12 p.m. is noon**](https://www.npl.co.uk/resources/q-a/is-midnight-12am-or-12pm), though noon is strictly neither.

Etymonline finds p.m. attested from the [**1660s**](https://www.etymonline.com/word/p.m.), a.m. in reference to hours [from **1762**](https://www.etymonline.com/word/a.m.), and AM as a type of radio broadcast, short for amplitude modulation, from 1921. The Latin phrases are much older. In his *Tusculan Disputations*, Cicero recorded that he and his friends gave their time ["ante meridiem"](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/cicero/tusc2.shtml) to speaking practice and went down "post meridiem" to the Academy at his Tusculan villa, which the introduction to [Charles Duke Yonge's translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/14988/pg14988-images.html) describes as "a gallery, called the Academy, which he had built for the purpose of philosophical conferences."

## Timekeepers call 12 a.m. and 12 p.m. ambiguous

Britain's [National Physical Laboratory](https://www.npl.co.uk/resources/q-a/is-midnight-12am-or-12pm) (NPL), whose atomic clocks are "the basis of all UK time," says "there are no official standards established for the meaning of 12am and 12pm," though "it is generally accepted that 12am means midnight and 12pm means midday." NPL also records another convention, "sometimes used," in which 12am is midnight at the start of a day and 12pm is midnight at its end, 24:00, a reading under which 12 p.m. is never noon at all.

The logic of the everyday rule is that p.m. begins the instant noon has passed. In [NPL's words](https://www.npl.co.uk/resources/q-a/is-midnight-12am-or-12pm), "By the time you have seen a clock showing 12:00 at midday it is already post meridiem." [NIST likewise holds](https://www.nist.gov/pml/time-and-frequency-division/times-day-faqs) that "the shortest measurable duration after noon should be designated as p.m.," so in NIST's example a digital clock would show p.m. as soon as 12:00 appears.

> "12 a.m. and 12 p.m. are ambiguous and should not be used." - NIST, Times of Day FAQs

NIST's own example of opening hours shows the way around it: a business open ["on Saturdays from 8 a.m. to noon or weekends from 3:30 p.m. until midnight."](https://www.nist.gov/pml/time-and-frequency-division/times-day-faqs)

## The federal style manual has written noon three ways

The US Government Printing Office's Style Manual covers "references to meridian in statements of time" in its [rule 9.54](https://archive.org/details/123457_1984), and the rule has changed. (Congress [redesignated the agency](https://www.gpo.gov/who-we-are/our-agency/history) the Government Publishing Office in December 2014.) The [**1984** edition](https://archive.org/details/123457_1984) wrote noon as **"12 m."**, with m. for meridies, and midnight as "12 p.m."

The [**2000** edition](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2000/pdf/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2000-13.pdf) kept midnight and moved noon to "12 a.m. (formerly 12 m.)." Its [numerals chapter](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2000/pdf/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2000-16.pdf) put **"12 a.m. (noon)"** a quarter of an hour before "12:15 p.m. (15 minutes past noon)."

The [**2008** edition](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2008/pdf/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2008-11.pdf) reversed both, to "12 p.m. (12 noon)" and "12 a.m. (12 midnight)," and the [2016 edition](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2016/pdf/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2016-11.pdf) kept them.

| GPO Style Manual | Noon | Midnight |
|---|---|---|
| 1984 edition | 12 m. | 12 p.m. |
| 2000 edition | 12 a.m. | 12 p.m. |
| 2008 and 2016 editions | 12 p.m. | 12 a.m. |

## Midnight sits on the line between two dates

"At midnight on Friday, October 20th" could mean the start of that day or its end, [NIST notes](https://www.nist.gov/pml/time-and-frequency-division/times-day-faqs), so it calls naming 11:59 p.m. or 12:01 a.m. instead "a good idea, especially legal documents such as contracts and insurance policies." NIST's other answer is the 24-hour clock, with **0000** for midnight at the beginning of a date and **2400** for midnight at its end. The 2000 GPO manual's [numerals chapter](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2000/pdf/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2000-16.pdf) also listed four-digit clock times: "0025, 2359 (astronomical and military time)."

[NPL](https://www.npl.co.uk/resources/q-a/is-midnight-12am-or-12pm) says it is "generally accepted that the moment the clock hits midnight marks the start of a new day," and finds it "common for transport timetables to use 23:59 or 00:01 to make it really clear which day they mean." Its general advice is that "it is always best to use the words midday, noon and midnight."

## In Latin, midday also meant the south

The Latin word behind both abbreviations came with its own explanation. Varro derived meridies, midday, ["ab eo quod medius dies,"](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/varro.ll6.html) from its being the middle of the day. Older Latin, he added, put a d in the word where his own had an r, as he had seen it cut into a sundial at Praeneste: ["ut Praeneste incisum in solario vidi."](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/varro.ll6.html)

Cicero noticed the same change in his *Orator*, where he asked why Latin said meridiem and not medidiem and offered a guess: ["Credo, quod erat insuavius,"](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/cicero/orator.shtml) I believe because it was less pleasant. The [English translation by E. Jones](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/9776/pg9776-images.html) states the cause flatly: Latin had "substituted meridiem for medidiem, because the latter was offensive to the ear." In [Jones's English](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/9776/pg9776-images.html), the same passage, on setting aside etymology "to improve the sweetness of our language," prefers pomeridianas, built from post meridiem, to the regular postmeridianas.

The word also meant the south: Latin meridies meant ["noon, south."](https://www.etymonline.com/word/meridian) English has used [Midi](https://www.etymonline.com/word/Midi) for southern France since at least **1883**, from French midi, "south," literally "midday," and the entry explains: "At midday in the northern hemisphere the sun is in the south of the sky."

[English meridian](https://www.etymonline.com/word/meridian) meant "noon, midday" from the mid-14th century, and by the late 14th it also named a great circle through the poles, a sense that was "originally astronomical."

## Noon was once read off the sky

Pliny the Elder wrote that early Rome announced midday by voice. In [Pliny's *Natural History*](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.02.0137:book=7:chapter=60), the consuls' summoner proclaimed it "as soon as, from the senate-house, he caught sight of the sun between the Rostra and the Graecostasis," which worked only in clear weather and lasted until the First Punic War.

Pliny reports an earlier dial at the temple of Quirinus, but the first sundial for public use, by Varro's account, arrived during that war, ["brought from the capture of Catina, in Sicily."](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.02.0137:book=7:chapter=60) In the [Bostock and Riley translation](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.02.0137:book=7:chapter=60), its lines "did not exactly agree with the hours," yet it regulated Roman time for **ninety-nine years**. [Bostock's note](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.02.0137:book=7:chapter=60) gives the cause: the dial had been made for the latitude of Catina, "about four degrees south of Rome."

Solar noon now has an exact definition. In [NOAA's glossary](https://gml.noaa.gov/grad/solcalc/glossary.html), it "is the time when the sun crosses the meridian of the observer's location," and a vertical pole's shadow then points directly north or directly south.

On a modern clock, 12:00 comes from the time zone rather than the sun. A zone, in [NOAA's definition](https://gml.noaa.gov/grad/solcalc/glossary.html), keeps "a common time" across a region that generally spans 15 degrees of longitude. [Daylight saving time](/blog/did-daylight-saving-start-for-farmers/), the period when, [in NIST's words](https://www.nist.gov/pml/time-and-frequency-division/popular-links/daylight-saving-time-dst), "clocks are moved one hour ahead," moves solar noon an hour later on the clock.

## Noon itself began as 3 p.m.

The word noon has moved as well. [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/noon) traces it to Old English non, **"3 o'clock p.m., the ninth hour from sunrise,"** from Latin nona hora, the ninth hour of daylight. The shift toward midday began in the 12th century and seems to have been complete by the 14th.

[Etymonline's](https://www.etymonline.com/word/noon) candidate causes include unreliable medieval timekeeping, [daylight hours whose length changed with the seasons](/blog/how-long-is-a-moment/), fasts that ended at nones in monasteries and on holy days, and an advance in the customary time of the midday meal. Roman reckoning had put midday at the sixth hour, the sexta hora behind the Spanish [siesta](https://www.etymonline.com/word/siesta).

From the 17th century to the 19th, noon sometimes also meant midnight, ["the noon of the night."](https://www.etymonline.com/word/noon) The one moment that is neither a.m. nor p.m. once lent its name to the moment that is both. Strictly FYI.
