Every moment of the day is meant to be either a.m. or p.m., and one moment refuses. At noon the time is not before midday and not after it, so the [US National Institute of Standards and Technology](https://www.nist.gov/pml/time-and-frequency-division/times-day-faqs) (NIST) concludes that "a designation of either a.m. or p.m. is incorrect." Midnight has the opposite problem: twelve hours from noon in both directions, it is as much after one midday as before the next.

The everyday rule, 12 a.m. for midnight and 12 p.m. for noon, papers over the gap, and not every authority has followed it. As late as its **2000** edition, the US government's own style manual called noon 12 a.m. and midnight 12 p.m., the reverse of today's convention.

## What does AM stand for?

AM stands for **ante meridiem**, Latin for "before midday," and PM stands for **post meridiem**, "after midday." A.m. covers the twelve hours from midnight to noon and p.m. the twelve from noon to midnight. By everyday convention, **12 a.m. is midnight and 12 p.m. is noon**, though noon is strictly neither.

[Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/p.m.) finds p.m. in English from the **1660s**, and a.m., in reference to hours, [from **1762**](https://www.etymonline.com/word/a.m.). The Latin phrases are much older. In his *Tusculan Disputations*, Cicero recorded that he and his friends gave their time ["ante meridiem"](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/cicero/tusc2.shtml) to speaking practice and went down to the Academy, as he called a gallery at his villa in Tusculum, "post meridiem."

## Is 12 a.m. midnight or noon?

The everyday convention has no official backing. Britain's [National Physical Laboratory](https://www.npl.co.uk/resources/q-a/is-midnight-12am-or-12pm) (NPL), whose atomic clocks are "the basis of all UK time," says "there are no official standards established for the meaning of 12am and 12pm," though "it is generally accepted that 12am means midnight and 12pm means midday."

The logic is that p.m. begins the instant noon has passed. In NPL's words, "By the time you have seen a clock showing 12:00 at midday it is already post meridiem." NIST likewise holds that "the shortest measurable duration after noon should be designated as p.m.," so in NIST's example a digital clock would show p.m. as soon as 12:00 appears.

NPL also describes another convention, "sometimes used," in which 12am is midnight at the start of a day and 12pm is midnight at its end, 24:00. On that reading, 12 p.m. is never noon at all.

> "12 a.m. and 12 p.m. are ambiguous and should not be used." - NIST, Times of Day FAQs

NIST's own example of opening hours shows the way around it: a business open "on Saturdays from 8 a.m. to noon or weekends from 3:30 p.m. until midnight." NPL's advice points the same way: "it is always best to use the words midday, noon and midnight."

## The federal style manual has written noon three ways

The US Government Printing Office's Style Manual (the office became the Government Publishing Office in 2014) covers "references to meridian in statements of time" in its rule 9.54, and the rule has changed. The [**1984** edition](https://archive.org/details/123457_1984) wrote noon as **"12 m."**, with m. for meridies, and midnight as "12 p.m."

The [**2000** edition](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2000/pdf/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2000-13.pdf) kept midnight and moved noon to "12 a.m. (formerly 12 m.)." Its [numerals chapter](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2000/pdf/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2000-16.pdf) put **"12 a.m. (noon)"** a quarter of an hour before "12:15 p.m. (15 minutes past noon)."

The [**2008** edition](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2008/pdf/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2008-11.pdf) reversed both, to "12 p.m. (12 noon)" and "12 a.m. (12 midnight)," and the [2016 edition](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2016/pdf/GPO-STYLEMANUAL-2016-11.pdf) kept them.

| GPO Style Manual | Noon | Midnight |
|---|---|---|
| 1984 edition | 12 m. | 12 p.m. |
| 2000 edition | 12 a.m. | 12 p.m. |
| 2008 and 2016 editions | 12 p.m. | 12 a.m. |

## Midnight sits on the line between two dates

"At midnight on Friday, October 20th" could mean the start of that day or its end, NIST notes, so it calls naming 11:59 p.m. or 12:01 a.m. instead "a good idea, especially legal documents such as contracts and insurance policies." It is also "common for transport timetables to use 23:59 or 00:01 to make it really clear which day they mean," according to NPL.

The 24-hour clock settles most of it. NIST suggests **0000** for midnight at the beginning of a date and **2400** for midnight at its end, and NPL says it is "generally accepted that the moment the clock hits midnight marks the start of a new day." The 2000 GPO manual's numerals chapter wrote times that way too, listing "0025, 2359 (astronomical and military time)."

## In Latin, midday also meant the south

The Latin word behind both abbreviations came with its own explanation. Varro derived meridies, midday, "ab eo, quod medius dies," from its being the middle of the day, in a line quoted by [Lewis and Short's Latin dictionary](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.04.0059:entry=meridies), which traces meridies to a form medidies. In his *Orator*, Cicero asked why Latin said meridiem rather than medidiem and guessed the regular form sounded worse. In the [English translation by E. Jones](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/9776/pg9776-images.html), medidiem "was offensive to the ear."

The same dictionary gives meridies a second sense, "the south." [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/Midi) explains the link: "At midday in the northern hemisphere the sun is in the south of the sky." French midi works the same way, "south," literally "midday," which is why southern France is the Midi.

English meridian meant "noon, midday" from the mid-14th century, and by the late 14th it also named a great circle through the poles, a sense [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/meridian) calls "originally astronomical."

## Noon was once read off the sky

Pliny the Elder, who also noticed that [the biggest tides lag behind the full and new moons](/blog/what-is-a-spring-tide/), wrote that early Rome announced midday by voice. The consuls' summoner proclaimed it "as soon as, from the senate-house, he caught sight of the sun between the Rostra and the Graecostasis," which worked only in clear weather and lasted until the First Punic War.

The first public sundial, by Varro's account in [Pliny's *Natural History*](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.02.0137:book=7:chapter=60), arrived during that war, brought from the capture of Catina, in Sicily. (Pliny also reports an earlier dial at the temple of Quirinus.) Its lines "did not exactly agree with the hours," yet it regulated Roman time for **99 years**.

Solar noon now has an exact definition. In [NOAA's glossary](https://gml.noaa.gov/grad/solcalc/glossary.html), it "is the time when the sun crosses the meridian of the observer's location," and a vertical pole's shadow then points directly north or directly south. Post meridiem, in Etymonline's gloss, means "occurring after the sun has passed the meridian."

On a modern clock, 12:00 comes from the time zone rather than the sun. A zone, in NOAA's definition, keeps "a common time" across a region generally 15 degrees of longitude wide, and [daylight saving time](/blog/did-daylight-saving-start-for-farmers/) moves solar noon an hour later on the clock.

## Noon itself began as 3 p.m.

The word noon has moved as well. [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/noon) traces it to Old English non, **"3 o'clock p.m., the ninth hour from sunrise,"** from Latin nona hora, the ninth hour of daylight. The shift toward midday began in the 12th century and seems to have been complete by the 14th.

Among the reasons Etymonline gives are unreliable medieval timekeeping, [daylight hours whose length changed with the seasons](/blog/how-long-is-a-moment/), and fasts that ended at nones in monasteries and on holy days. Roman reckoning had put midday at the sixth hour, the sexta hora behind the Spanish [siesta](https://www.etymonline.com/word/siesta).

From the 17th century to the 19th, Etymonline adds, noon sometimes also meant midnight, "the noon of the night." The one moment that is neither a.m. nor p.m. once lent its name to the moment that is both. Strictly FYI.
