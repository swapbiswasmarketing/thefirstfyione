For **52 and a half years**, a US federal rule said that a television's size, unless the seller said otherwise, meant the **width** of its picture. The [Federal Trade Commission's rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1966-03-03/pdf/FR-1966-03-03.pdf#page=12) took effect on **July 1, 1966**. Quote any other dimension, and the seller had to name it "clearly and conspicuously" beside the number.

The diagonal, the figure every TV is sold by today, was allowed only with that label attached, in wording such as the rule's own example: "20 inch picture measured diagonally." Manufacturers kept the diagonal. When the FTC [repealed the rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2018-10-09/html/2018-21803.htm) in 2018, effective **January 7, 2019**, it found that "the industry universally measures television screen sizes using the diagonal dimension."

## How are TVs measured?

TVs are measured diagonally, in a straight line from one corner of the visible picture to the opposite corner. The bezel, frame and stand are not counted. A 65-inch screen in the standard 16:9 shape is about **56.7 inches wide** and **31.9 inches tall**, and the set's frame makes the whole TV slightly larger.

To measure a TV yourself, run a tape from the top-left corner of the lit picture to the bottom-right, keeping it off the border. [TCL's support page](https://support.tcl.com/how-is-the-screen-size-of-a-television-determine) says the measurement "includes only the display area and excludes the frame or bezel around the screen," and the FAQ in [LG's measuring guide](https://www.lg.com/ca_en/lg-experience/helpful-hints/how-to-measure-and-read-tv-sizes/) gives the same 56.7 by 31.9 inches for a 65-inch set. The tape may stop up to half an inch short of the number on the box, because many sizes are rounded class figures.

## Early pictures were squeezed into round tubes

Old sets showed their picture on the face of a cathode-ray tube, [the same kind of tube behind the oldest documented video game design](/blog/what-was-the-first-video-game/). In March 1950, [Popular Science](https://www.earlytelevision.org/pdf/pop_sci_3-50-1.pdf) announced a change of shape: "After years of trying to squeeze a rectangular television picture into a round tube, TV manufacturers have come up with the obvious solution - a rectangular tube."

Round screens did not always show a rectangle. On January 1, 1949, the trade newsletter [Television Digest](https://worldradiohistory.com/Archive-TV-Digest/40s/TV-Digest-1949-01.pdf) described Garod's new Tele-Zoom, which had a "round screen, just like Zenith's," and a switch that gave the viewer a "choice of either circular or rectangular picture." It came in **10-, 12- and 15-inch** models, and on a round screen an inch figure has one natural meaning: the distance straight across.

## The round-tube story is plausible, not proven

The FTC's [1994 review](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1994-11-02/html/94-27012.htm) says most manufacturers measured their sets diagonally in 1966, "just as they do today." None of the FTC's notices on the rule says why they started.

The usual explanation runs from circle to rectangle. [Wikipedia's article on display size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Display_size) says that for round tubes "the external diameter of the bulb was used to describe their size," and that the method "continued even when cathode ray tubes were manufactured as rounded rectangles." The geometry fits, since a rectangle drawn inside a circle with its corners on the rim has a diagonal exactly as long as the circle's diameter.

Wikipedia's history section cites no source for this, though. US [picture-tube type numbers](https://archive.bvws.org.uk/405alive/tech/valvenos3.html) did follow the same pattern, giving the diameter in inches for round screens and the diagonal for rectangular ones, but none of the records checked here says that is why the diagonal was chosen.

A blunter theory holds that the diagonal is the longest straight line across a rectangle, so it gives the biggest number. That motive is unproven too. Either way, the habit outlived the tubes, much as [the QWERTY layout](/blog/why-the-keyboard-is-qwerty/) outlived the typewriters that shaped it.

## Sets were sold by glass hidden in the cabinet

Sellers sized rectangular tubes by their overall diagonal. The FTC's 1966 record lists the phrases they used, among them "16 incher" and "19 inch set - overall diagonal measure."

The trouble was the cabinet. "It is a practice in the industry," the Commission wrote, "when installing a picture tube in a television receiving set, to mask or cover the walls of such tube and a minimal amount of the actual picture area thereof." The overall figure counted tube walls that showed no picture, so it was "invariably larger" than the picture itself.

The record was, in the FTC's words, "replete with television advertisements wherein the overall sizes of the advertised sets are emphasized in large figures or print." In the rule's worked example, a set showing a **20-inch** picture measured diagonally could not be described as a "21 inch set," a "21 inch diagonal set" or "Brand Name 21." From then on, any size quoted had to be that of the **viewable picture**, measured on a single flat plane that ignores the curve of the glass.

Viewable screen size still means that: the lit picture, corner to corner. The 1994 review stated that the United States was, "and is to this day, the only country that requires screen measurements to be limited to the viewable picture area," though it noted a Canadian government effort to adopt similar provisions.

## Regulators wanted width, as with rugs

The FTC made width the default because of how people already sized rectangles. Blankets, it noted, were sized as "76 x 107 inches," and rugs as "9 ft. x 12 ft."

> "Except in the matter under consideration, the Commission is not aware of any rectangular shaped object the size of which is stated in terms of the diagonal measurement." - Federal Trade Commission, 1966

Shoppers, it concluded, would take "19 inch set" to mean a picture **19 inches** across.

In the same 1994 review, the Electronic Industries Association "urged the Commission to eliminate the horizontal dimension as the default measurement." The FTC kept it, citing one manufacturer's comment that many shoppers "would not instinctively understand the diagonal measurement." The Consumer Electronics Association asked again, and in [2006](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2006-06-14/html/E6-9233.htm) the FTC said the switch "could potentially cause confusion."

## A 65-inch class TV can measure 64.5 inches

Rounding opens a smaller gap between the box and the glass. A [2017 FTC notice](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2017-06-28/html/2017-13476.htm) recounts that in 2008 the industry asked for guidance on rounding "because promotional material measurements were often off by up to 1/2-inch to avoid waste in cutting glass from standard sheets."

FTC staff replied, without binding the Commission, that they were "unlikely to recommend enforcement action based on an up to 1/2-inch discrepancy," provided the larger number was clearly a class size and the real measurement sat nearby, as in "32'' Class, 31.75'' actual measurement." In ENERGY STAR's list of certified televisions, checked in October 2026, [29 of the 35 sets](https://data.energystar.gov/resource/pd96-rr3d.json?$select=brand_name,model_number,diagonal_viewable_screen_size_inches&$where=diagonal_viewable_screen_size_inches::number%20between%2063.5%20and%2066) with a diagonal between 63.5 and 66 inches give it as exactly **64.5 inches**.

## Widescreen loses area at the same diagonal

The diagonal says nothing about shape. In the FTC's 2006 wording, traditional sets had an aspect ratio of 4 by 3 and high-definition sets 16 by 9. At 65 inches, the wider shape gains 4.7 inches of width but loses 7.1 inches of height, which costs about **11 percent** of the area.

| Screen | Width | Height | Area |
|---|---|---|---|
| 65 in, 4:3 | 52.0 in | 39.0 in | 2,028 sq in |
| 65 in, 16:9 | 56.7 in | 31.9 in | 1,805 sq in |
| 64.5 in, 16:9 ("65-inch class") | 56.2 in | 31.6 in | 1,778 sq in |

The arithmetic is Pythagoras. A 4:3 rectangle has a diagonal of 5 units, so its width is 4/5 of the diagonal and its height 3/5. A 16:9 rectangle has a diagonal of about 18.36 units, so its width is 0.872 of the diagonal and its height 0.490, which puts a 55-inch screen at about 47.9 by 27.0 inches and a 75-inch one at about 65.4 by 36.8.

The 1966 rule was written for curved glass hidden behind cabinet masks, and for shoppers who sized blankets by length and width. The masks went, and then the rule. The diagonal outlasted both, though the number on the box may still run half an inch ahead of the glass. Strictly FYI.
