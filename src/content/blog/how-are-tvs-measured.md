For **52 and a half years**, a US federal rule said that a television's size, unless the seller said otherwise, meant the **width** of its picture. The [Federal Trade Commission's rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1966-03-03/pdf/FR-1966-03-03.pdf#page=13) took effect on **July 1, 1966**, and a seller who quoted any other dimension had to name it "clearly and conspicuously" beside the number.

The diagonal, the figure every TV is sold by today, was allowed only with that label attached. Manufacturers kept the diagonal. When the FTC [repealed the rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2018-10-09/html/2018-21803.htm) in 2018, effective **January 7, 2019**, it found that "the industry universally measures television screen sizes using the diagonal dimension."

## How are TVs measured?

TVs are measured diagonally, in a straight line from one corner of the visible picture to the opposite corner. The bezel, frame and stand are not counted. A 65-inch screen in the standard widescreen shape is about **56.7 inches wide** and **31.9 inches tall**, the figures [LG's sizing guide](https://www.lg.com/ca_en/lg-experience/helpful-hints/how-to-measure-and-read-tv-sizes/) gives, and the set's frame makes the whole TV slightly larger.

To measure a TV yourself, run a tape from the top-left corner of the lit picture to the bottom-right, keeping it off the border. [TCL's support page](https://support.tcl.com/how-is-the-screen-size-of-a-television-determine) says the measurement "includes only the display area and excludes the frame or bezel around the screen." The tape may stop a little short of the number on the box, because many sizes are rounded class figures.

## Early pictures were squeezed into round tubes

Old sets showed their picture on the face of a cathode-ray tube. In March 1950, [Popular Science](https://www.earlytelevision.org/pdf/pop_sci_3-50-1.pdf) announced a change of shape: "After years of trying to squeeze a rectangular television picture into a round tube, TV manufacturers have come up with the obvious solution - a rectangular tube."

Round screens did not always show a rectangle. On January 1, 1949, the trade newsletter [Television Digest](https://worldradiohistory.com/Archive-TV-Digest/40s/TV-Digest-1949-01.pdf#page=14) described Garod's new Tele-Zoom, which had a "round screen, just like Zenith's," and a switch that gave the viewer a "choice of either circular or rectangular picture." It came in **10-, 12- and 15-inch** models, and on a round screen an inch figure has one natural meaning: the distance straight across.

## The round-tube story is plausible, not proven

The FTC's [2006 review](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2006-06-14/html/E6-9233.htm) says that when the rule was adopted in 1966, most manufacturers already measured their sets diagonally, "just as they do today." None of the FTC's notices on the rule says why they started.

The usual explanation runs from circle to rectangle, and one half of it is on record. In August 1949, [Television Digest](https://worldradiohistory.com/Archive-TV-Digest/40s/TV-Digest-1949-08.pdf#page=27) reported growing sentiment in the trade for dropping square-inch claims and "to adhere henceforth strictly to diameter designations," and two weeks later that RCA and Motorola had agreed "to stick to tube diameters in future ads."

The geometry fits too, since a rectangle drawn inside a circle with its corners on the rim has a diagonal exactly as long as the circle's diameter. US [picture-tube type numbers](https://archive.bvws.org.uk/405alive/tech/valvenos3.html) followed the same pattern, giving the diameter in inches for round screens and the diagonal for rectangular ones, but none of these records says that is why the diagonal was chosen.

A blunter theory holds that the diagonal is the longest straight line across a rectangle, so it gives the biggest number. That motive is unproven too. Either way, the habit outlived the tubes.

## Sets were sold by glass hidden in the cabinet

Sellers sized rectangular tubes by their overall diagonal, tube walls included, and the trouble was the cabinet. It was industry practice, [the Commission wrote](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1966-03-03/pdf/FR-1966-03-03.pdf#page=12), "to mask or cover the walls of such tube and a minimal amount of the actual picture area thereof," so a size given as "19 inch set - overall diagonal measure" was "invariably larger" than the picture.

FTC staff had tried a softer fix in 1956, telling the industry they would not object to overall diagonal figures if ads also gave the real picture size and said the figure was overall and diagonal. But the [record](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1966-03-03/pdf/FR-1966-03-03.pdf#page=12) was "replete with television advertisements wherein the overall sizes of the advertised sets are emphasized in large figures or print," and wherever the picture size appeared it was "either inadequate or so inconspicuous as to be of no value in removing or curing the inherent deceptive tendency."

The FTC's [1994 review](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1994-11-02/html/94-27012.htm) spelled out the rule's worked example: a screen "measuring 19 inches horizontally, 15 inches vertically, and 20 inches diagonally" could be sold as a "20 inch diagonal" but not as a "21 inch diagonal set," because "the 21 inch measurement is not accurate." Taken at face value, those three figures describe no true rectangle. A full 19-by-15 rectangle measures about **24.2 inches** corner to corner and covers 285 square inches, but the [same example](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1994-11-02/html/94-27012.htm) gives the screen "a picture area of 262 square inches," so the numbers fit only a picture with its corners trimmed off.

From 1966 on, [the rule required](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2017-06-28/html/2017-13476.htm) any size quoted to be that of the **viewable picture**, measured on a single plane without regard to the curve of the glass. The [1994 review](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1994-11-02/html/94-27012.htm) stated that the United States was, "and is to this day, the only country that requires screen measurements to be limited to the viewable picture area," though it noted a Canadian government effort to adopt similar provisions.

## Regulators wanted width, as with rugs

The FTC made width the default because of how people already sized rectangles. Blankets, [it noted](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1966-03-03/pdf/FR-1966-03-03.pdf#page=13), were sized as "76 x 107 inches" and rugs as "9 ft. x 12 ft.," so, it concluded, shoppers would take a "19 inch set" to mean a picture **19 inches** across.

> "Except in the matter under consideration, the Commission is not aware of any rectangular shaped object the size of which is stated in terms of the diagonal measurement." - Federal Trade Commission, 1966

In the [same 1994 review](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1994-11-02/html/94-27012.htm), the Electronic Industries Association "urged the Commission to eliminate the horizontal dimension as the default measurement." The FTC kept it, citing one manufacturer's comment that many shoppers "would not instinctively understand the diagonal measurement." The Consumer Electronics Association asked again, and in [2006](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2006-06-14/html/E6-9233.htm) the FTC said the switch "could potentially cause confusion," since shoppers used to sizes labeled as diagonal might take an unlabeled figure for a width or an area.

So the width default stayed on the books. Commenting on the proposed repeal in 2018, the Consumer Technology Association [noted](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2018-10-09/html/2018-21803.htm) that the FTC had not brought an enforcement action under it "in the more than 50 years since its adoption."

## A 65-inch class TV can measure 64.5 inches

Rounding opens a smaller gap between the box and the glass. A [2017 FTC notice](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2017-06-28/html/2017-13476.htm) recounts that in 2008 the industry asked for guidance on rounding "because promotional material measurements were often off by up to 1/2-inch to avoid waste in cutting glass from standard sheets."

FTC staff [replied](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2017-06-28/html/2017-13476.htm), without binding the Commission, that they were "unlikely to recommend enforcement action based on an up to 1/2-inch discrepancy," provided the larger number was clearly a class size and the real measurement sat nearby, as in "32'' Class, 31.75'' actual measurement." In [ENERGY STAR's list of certified televisions](https://data.energystar.gov/resource/pd96-rr3d.json?$select=brand_name,model_number,diagonal_viewable_screen_size_inches&$where=diagonal_viewable_screen_size_inches::number%20between%2063.5%20and%2066), most sets listed near 65 inches give their diagonal as exactly **64.5 inches**.

## Widescreen loses area at the same diagonal

The diagonal says nothing about shape. The FTC's [2006 review](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2006-06-14/html/E6-9233.htm) gave traditional sets an aspect ratio of "4 by 3 (1.33 to 1)" and high-definition sets "16 by 9 (1.85 to 1)," a slip, since 16 divided by 9 is about 1.78. At 65 inches, the wider shape gains 4.7 inches of width but loses 7.1 inches of height, which costs about **11 percent** of the area.

| Screen | Width | Height | Area |
|---|---|---|---|
| 65 in, 4:3 | 52.0 in | 39.0 in | 2,028 sq in |
| 65 in, 16:9 | 56.7 in | 31.9 in | 1,805 sq in |
| 64.5 in, 16:9 ("65-inch class") | 56.2 in | 31.6 in | 1,778 sq in |

The arithmetic is Pythagoras. A 4:3 rectangle has a diagonal of 5 units, so its width is 4/5 of the diagonal and its height 3/5. A 16:9 rectangle has a diagonal of about 18.36 units, so its width is 0.872 of the diagonal and its height 0.490, which puts a 55-inch screen at about 47.9 by 27.0 inches and a 75-inch one at about 65.4 by 36.8.

The rule was written for curved glass hidden behind cabinet masks, and for shoppers who sized blankets by length and width. The masks went, and then the rule. The diagonal outlasted both, though the number on the box may still run half an inch ahead of the glass. Strictly FYI.
