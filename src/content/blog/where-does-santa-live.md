Page 825 of *Harper's Weekly* for [**December 29, 1866**](https://archive.org/details/sim_harpers-weekly_1866-12-29_10_522/page/n8/mode/1up) carries a curved band of capitals that reads "SANTA CLAUSSVILLE N.P." Below the lettering, in Thomas Nast's double-page Christmas spread, stands a small town of houses and church steeples, its trees set on round stands like the pieces of a toy village.

The vignettes around it show Santa at work. In one he peers through a telescope under a fringe of icicles, "ON THE LOOK OUT FOR GOOD CHILDREN"; in another he writes in an enormous ledger captioned "ACCOUNT BOOK" and "RECORD OF BEHAVIOUR." N.P. stands for the North Pole.

## Where does Santa live?

Santa lives at the **North Pole** in the American and Canadian tradition, an address the cartoonist Thomas Nast printed as "N.P." in *Harper's Weekly* in 1866. In Finland, he has a home on **Korvatunturi**, a fell on Finland's border with Russia, and an office at Santa Claus Village on the Arctic Circle in **Rovaniemi**.

- **North Pole:** "SANTA CLAUSSVILLE N.P." on Nast's **1866** page, then "near the North Pole" in George P. Webster's verse, dated **1869** by Jeremy Seal.
- **Korvatunturi, Finnish Lapland:** named on a Finnish children's radio show, by the usual account in **1927**.
- **Rovaniemi, Finland:** Santa Claus Village, where Santa's office dates from **1985**, according to Visit Rovaniemi.
- **Canada Post:** Santa Claus, North Pole, H0H 0H0, Canada.

## A children's verse put the North Pole into words in 1869

Clement C. Moore's "A Visit from Saint Nicholas," printed in full in the same 1866 issue, gives its St. Nicholas a sleigh, "eight tiny rein-deer" that it [calls by name, down to Donder and Blitzen](/blog/what-are-the-names-of-santas-reindeer/), and a chimney to come down, but never says where he flies in from. The words "North Pole" came later.

Jeremy Seal writes in [*Nicholas: The Epic Journey from Saint to Santa Claus*](https://archive.org/details/nicholasepicjour00seal) that in **1869** the text for a color edition of Nast's drawings came from "a children's poem called 'Santa Claus and His Works.'" Its author, George P. Webster, set Santa in a town much like the steepled one on Nast's page, "a nice little city called Santa Claus-ville, / With its houses and church at the foot of the hill." The full text is reprinted in E. Willis Jones's [*The Santa Claus Book*](https://archive.org/details/santaclausbook0000jone), and further on, the poem gives Santa's summer address.

> "His home through the long summer months, you must know, / Is near the North Pole, in the ice and the snow." - George P. Webster, *Santa Claus and His Works*, 1869

## The North Pole was the north nobody could reach

The far north collected nicknames in the nineteenth century, Seal notes, "Ultima Thule; the barren grounds; the frozen north," and "The North Pole was particularly popular." Explorers heading anywhere in the Arctic were said to have gone "out to the North Pole," a usage that "reflected the fact that the point at 90 degrees north remained so far beyond human reach as to be unimaginable." That was the geographic pole, not the magnetic one, which in physics terms [is a south pole](/blog/how-does-a-compass-work/).

A fairy-tale queen was already in residence. In Hans Christian Andersen's *The Snow Queen*, from the 1840s, a reindeer explains that the queen's "fixed abode is high up towards the North Pole, on the Island called Spitzbergen," in an [old English translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1597). In Seal's words, "It was no surprise that Santa should have followed her lead."

## In Finland, Santa lives on Korvatunturi, the Ear Fell

By the usual account, Finland's answer came by radio. In **1927**, a child asked the children's hour on Yleisradio, the national broadcaster, where Santa lived, and the host, Markus Rautio, known to listeners as Uncle Markus, answered: Korvatunturi, a fell in Lapland.

Korvatunturi rises to **486 metres** on the Finland-Russia border, in Urho Kekkonen National Park in Savukoski. From a distance its peaks "look like the ears of a fox or a wolf," says the fell's [visitor site](https://korvatunturi.fi/en/region-information/information-about-korvatunturi/), and "these ears can hear even the smallest whispers carried by the north wind."

[thisisFINLAND](https://finland.fi/christmas/the-ambassador-of-christmas/), a site produced by Finland's Ministry for Foreign Affairs, says Santa lives there "with his wife and hundreds of elves," and that "From the top of Korvatunturi, they can hear the Christmas wishes of all the children in the world." A Lapland sleigh would have had a local kind of team, too: the sled reindeer of Finland and Scandinavia were traditionally castrated bulls, which, unlike full males, [kept their antlers through the winter](/blog/do-female-reindeer-have-antlers/). A marked **18 km** trail from Kemihaara leads to a view of the fell, but going all the way to it takes a border zone permit from the Finnish Border Guard.

## Santa Claus Village began as a cabin for Eleanor Roosevelt

The Lapland address that visitors can reach began as a rush job. Eleanor Roosevelt, widow of President Franklin D. Roosevelt, decided to visit Rovaniemi in the summer of 1950 to see its post-war reconstruction, and she wanted to cross the Arctic Circle.

The news reached Rovaniemi "only two weeks before her arrival," says Santa Claus Village's [history of the Roosevelt Cottage](https://santaclausvillage.info/info-and-tips/roosevelt-cottage/), so the cabin "had to be erected and finished in less than two weeks." Its logs were pulled out of the Ounasjoki river eight days before she arrived.

It opened on Sunday, **June 11, 1950**, just over **100 metres** south of the Arctic Circle, because in 1950 the exact line ran through marshland. Santa Claus Village grew up around it.

[Visit Rovaniemi](https://www.visitrovaniemi.fi/love/santa-claus-magic-of-christmas/) describes Korvatunturi as the fell "said to be Santa Claus' original home" and says that "in 1985, Santa Claus decided to establish an office in Rovaniemi." It prints the city's title, "Official Hometown of Santa Claus," with a registered trademark sign, and the Arctic Circle line is [marked in the village's central square](https://santaclausvillage.info/activities-and-experiences/arctic-circle/).

## Canada gave the North Pole a postal code

Canada Post's [letters-to-Santa page](https://www.canadapost-postescanada.ca/cpc/en/our-company/write-letter-to-santa.page) gives his address as "Santa Claus / North Pole / H0H 0H0 / Canada," a postal code that reads HO HO HO when its zeros are read as O's. "No stamps are required to mail a letter to Santa within Canada," it adds, and for more than **40 years** its "postal elves" have been helping Santa sort through "over one million letters per year from around the world."

## The inventor stories for both addresses are unproven

A common shorthand credits Nast with inventing the North Pole home. His own page does not settle it: it abbreviates, and an abbreviation, [like Xmas for Christmas](/blog/why-do-people-say-xmas/), leans on a word readers already know. Seal reads the initials the same way: the "initialized form confirms that the term 'North Pole' was in wide circulation and may imply that Santa's residence there was not entirely unfamiliar even then."

Yle, Finland's own broadcaster, labels the Finnish inventor story a myth. Its [2015 profile of Rautio](https://yle.fi/a/3-7819185) opens the Korvatunturi episode with "Myytti kertoo," the myth tells, and says Rautio never confirmed the idea as his own. His biographer, Paavo Oinonen, thinks he may have heard such a story on theatre tours and then packaged it for the whole nation, and the article adds that a Santa who lived in Finland suited the work of nation-building.

Just before the North Pole line, Andersen's reindeer says the Snow Queen "has her summer-tent" in Lapland, so between the American and Finnish traditions, Santa ended up with both of her addresses, give or take Spitzbergen. Strictly FYI.
