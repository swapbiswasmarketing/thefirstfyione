A worker honey bee makes about **1/12 of a teaspoon** of honey in her whole life. The figure is an estimate: Jon Zawislak's [beekeeping guide](https://www.uaex.uada.edu/farm-ranch/special-programs/beekeeping/documents/Basic%20Beekeeping%20-%204%20-%20Bee%20Biology.pdf) for the University of Arkansas introduces it with "It has been estimated," and the University of Florida's [4-H honey bee project book](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/4H419) gives the same fraction.

A worker born in spring or summer lives only **four to six weeks**, Zawislak writes, and her last job, foraging, usually lasts less than a couple of weeks. Twelve such lifetimes fill a teaspoon, yet the [same guide](https://www.uaex.uada.edu/farm-ranch/special-programs/beekeeping/documents/Basic%20Beekeeping%20-%204%20-%20Bee%20Biology.pdf) says a healthy colony might produce "**well over 100 pounds** of surplus honey under good conditions." Much of the energy behind every pound goes into taking water out of nectar, and some bees start on that before they get home.

## How do bees make honey?

Bees make honey by collecting flower nectar in a storage pouch called the crop, adding enzymes that split its sucrose into glucose and fructose, and evaporating most of its water. Foragers pass the nectar mouth to mouth to house bees, who work droplets on their tongues, spread it through the comb, fan it dry and seal each cell with wax.

1. **Collect.** A forager laps up nectar with her long tongue and stores it in her crop, a pouch that sits in front of her true stomach.
2. **Pre-dry.** On dilute nectar, she appears to start evaporating water from her mouthparts on the flower and on the way home.
3. **Hand over.** At the hive she passes the load mouth to mouth to younger house bees.
4. **Add enzymes.** Invertase from glands in the bees' heads splits the sucrose.
5. **Evaporate.** House bees work droplets on their tongues, then spread the nectar in cells, fan air over it and move it between cells as it thickens.
6. **Cap.** As the honey ripens, the bees seal its cell with a beeswax lid.

## The honey stomach is a storage tank with a valve

The crop is commonly called the honey stomach, but a 2024 [review of honey bee nutrition](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11311006/) in the journal *Animals* states, "As this organ is not functionally a real stomach, the term crop or ingluvies is used." Behind the crop sits a valve, the **proventriculus**, which separates the foregut from the ventriculus, the bee's true stomach, "to prevent untimely digestion," the [same review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11311006/) says.

The esophagus "enlarges into the crop," [the review explains](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11311006/). The valve also strains pollen and some bacteria out of the nectar, Zawislak writes, and a bee that needs energy can release some nectar into her true stomach to digest.

Susan Nicolson and Hannelie Human of the University of Pretoria [treat the crop wall as watertight](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2610047/): if it were not, they point out, water would flow in from the bee's blood, or haemolymph, down a steep concentration gradient and dilute the crop's contents. That, they conclude, leaves evaporation from the mouthparts as the only way to take water out of the crop without the sugar.

## Foragers start drying nectar before they get home

In September 2020 Human, working with Nicolson and Christian Pirk, caught African honey bees (*Apis mellifera scutellata*) foraging in macadamia orchards near Barberton, in South Africa's Mpumalanga province, and [measured the nectar in their crops](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9519551/). While the bees were still on the flowers, the crop contents had **approximately twice** the sugar concentration of the fresh nectar, across four macadamia cultivars.

By the team's [estimate](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9519551/), **57 to 75 percent** of the nectar's water evaporated at the flowers, and an average of **81 percent** was gone by the time foragers reached the hive entrance. The team could not see the evaporation happen, but they concluded that "the only possible route of water elimination from the crop is via evaporation from the mouthparts." Nicolson and Human had reported the same doubling in 2008 on dilute aloe nectar, in a paper titled [Bees get a head start on honey production](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2610047/).

| Stage | Sugar content | Study |
|---|---|---|
| Aloe nectar in the flower | About 20% | Nicolson and Human, 2008 |
| Aloe nectar in returning foragers | 38 to 40% | Nicolson and Human, 2008 |
| Macadamia nectar in the flower | 20 to 30% | Nicolson, Human and Pirk, 2022 |
| Macadamia nectar at the hive entrance | 64% average | Nicolson, Human and Pirk, 2022 |
| Fully capped cells, syrup-fed hives | 84.8% average | Eyer, Neumann and Dietemann, 2016 |

Drying early saves energy, because any water left in the nectar has to be evaporated in the hive. Derek Mitchell, of the University of Leeds' School of Mechanical Engineering, [modelled that cost in 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6364643/). Even with no heat wasted, drying consumes **25 percent** of the energy in nectar delivered to the hive from oilseed rape, and 40 percent for clover nectar, which is more dilute. Allow for heat lost from the hive and the shares rise; in one of his less efficient scenarios they reach 40 and 60 percent. The Pretoria team calculated that their bees' early evaporation saved, on average, [35 times more energy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9519551/) in honey processing than it saved in flight by lightening the load.

> "Typical values show that over 50% of the delivered energy may be used in the process of honey ripening." - Derek Mitchell, [*Journal of the Royal Society Interface*, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6364643/)

## Bees blow bubbles with nectar and add enzymes

At the hive a forager hands her load to younger bees by trophallaxis, which the [4-H book](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/4H419) defines as "the sharing of food from the mouth of one insect to the mouth of another insect in the same colony." The house bees then spend several minutes moving a droplet back and forth between the crop and the mouthparts, Zawislak writes. The 4-H book calls it blowing bubbles with the nectar, and the Pretoria paper calls it tongue lashing.

The enzymes come from the hypopharyngeal glands, paired glands in the head in front of the brain. In young nurse bees they make royal jelly; in foragers they shrink and make enzymes including **invertase** and glucose oxidase "that convert nectar into honey," a [review in *Frontiers in Physiology*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7862731/) says.

Invertase splits sucrose into fructose and glucose, creating what a [2022 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9359632/) in *Frontiers in Nutrition* calls "a stable, high density, and highly energetic food source that occupies a minimum amount of space within the wax cells of the hive." Glucose oxidase turns some of the glucose in the still-dilute nectar into gluconic acid, which lowers the pH, and hydrogen peroxide, which kills microbes, two defenses that guard the nectar while it ripens and help [honey keep for so long](/blog/why-honey-never-spoils/).

Fanning circulates air through the hive while the nectar, spread thin in open cells, keeps losing water. Ripe honey ends up between **13 and 25 percent** water, according to the [same review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9359632/), and around 17 percent is optimal, since wetter honey is vulnerable to fermentation by sugar-tolerant yeasts.

## Bees shuffle nectar between cells and cap it early

To watch honey form in the comb, Michael Eyer, Peter Neumann and Vincent Dietemann [scanned honeycomb](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4999132/) from three colonies in Bern, Switzerland, in a CT scanner in the summer of 2012, five times over 12 days, while the colonies were fed sugar solution. Half of the cells filled on the first day were emptied over the study and their contents moved, and the cells that filled later and faster, with more concentrated syrup, became the capped honey store.

Capping also began before the honey was done. The sugar content of cells the bees were still sealing averaged **81.1 percent**, against **84.8 percent** in fully capped ones, which [the authors](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4999132/) read as a possible "race against honey dilution": as nectar thickens it turns hygroscopic and can absorb water from the hive air, which can lead to fermentation.

Ripening can take anywhere from **1 to 11 days**, depending on colony size, the weather and the plant the nectar came from, the [*Frontiers in Nutrition* review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9359632/) says. The syrup-fed Bern colonies were slower: capping first showed up in the day-12 scan, the first scan after day 8, [the team reports](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4999132/).

The lids are beeswax, which workers secrete as small flakes from eight glands on the underside of the abdomen. Making it costs honey: bees "need to consume a lot of honey or other sugars in order to produce a relatively small amount of wax," [Zawislak notes](https://www.uaex.uada.edu/farm-ranch/special-programs/beekeeping/documents/Basic%20Beekeeping%20-%204%20-%20Bee%20Biology.pdf).

## The bee vomit label is half right

The popular line that honey is bee vomit is right about the direction of travel. Nectar does come back up, many times: the [Pretoria paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9519551/) describes "repeated regurgitation and re-ingestion of nectar droplets."

What it gets wrong is where the nectar comes up from. Honey-bound nectar never gets past the proventriculus, and although enzymes split its sucrose along the way, that happens in a storage organ that is not functionally a stomach.

Zawislak's [guide](https://www.uaex.uada.edu/farm-ranch/special-programs/beekeeping/documents/Basic%20Beekeeping%20-%204%20-%20Bee%20Biology.pdf) calls the vomit claim "absolutely false," on the grounds that "No digestion takes place in the crop," yet he still pictures a spoonful of honey going in and out of hundreds of bees before it reaches a table.

## Bees make honey to eat when nothing is blooming

Honey is the colony's stored food, and the stores carry it through dearth periods "when foraging is not possible," [Eyer's team writes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4999132/), such as bad weather or winter in temperate regions. Without an adequate store, the [*Frontiers in Nutrition* review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9359632/) notes, a colony with no forage would collapse within a few days.

Honey bees are 8 of almost 21,000 known bee species, three bee researchers wrote in [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/most-bees-dont-die-after-stinging-and-other-surprising-bee-facts-227162) in 2024. They count 537 species of stingless bees worldwide, including 11 in Australia that "can also be kept in hives and make honey."

Honey is nectar that has come back up through bee mouths again and again without ever reaching a bee's true stomach, and some of its water may have left the same way while the forager was still on the flowers. Strictly FYI.
