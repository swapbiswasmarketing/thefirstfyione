Joseph Nicephore Niepce slid a polished pewter plate into a camera in 1827 and set the box near a window of [his second-story workroom](https://www.hrc.utexas.edu/niepce-heliograph/). The plate carried a thin coat of bitumen, a natural asphalt that [hardens where light falls on it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/View_from_the_Window_at_Le_Gras), and [after several days of exposure to sunlight](https://www.hrc.utexas.edu/niepce-heliograph/) it held an impression of the courtyard, outbuildings and trees of his family's estate at Saint-Loup-de-Varennes, in Burgundy.

That plate, now known as View from the Window at Le Gras, is [the earliest surviving photograph produced in the camera obscura](https://www.hrc.utexas.edu/niepce-heliograph/), in the words of the Harry Ransom Center at the University of Texas at Austin, which owns it. The camera was the old part of the experiment. Its name is [short for camera obscura](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/camera), New Latin for "dark chamber," and portable camera obscuras had been [aids for drawing and painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_obscura) for well over a century.

## When were cameras invented?

Cameras were invented long before photography: [a portable camera obscura box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_obscura) with a 45-degree mirror was described in **1676**, about 150 years before [the oldest surviving camera photograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/View_from_the_Window_at_Le_Gras) in **1827**. The pinhole image behind it was [described in China's *Mozi* in the 4th century BC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_obscura), and [the first photographic cameras built for sale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_camera) appeared in **1839**.

## The first cameras were rooms

Light passing through [the small hole of a dark chamber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_obscura) projects the scene outside onto the opposite surface, upside down and reversed. The Chinese text known as the *Mozi* [explains how a pinhole inverts such an image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_obscura), and Ibn al-Haytham [studied the effect extensively in the early 11th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_obscura).

[The first cameras were large enough to accommodate one or more people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_camera), and they shrank over time. Gemma Frisius, a Dutch mathematician, used one to study the solar eclipse of January 24, 1544, and [his book of 1545 holds the oldest known published drawing of a camera obscura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_obscura). The earliest known use of the term camera obscura is in [Johannes Kepler's treatise on optics of 1604](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_obscura).

In 1676 the German philosopher Johann Sturm published [an illustrated article on building a portable camera obscura box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_obscura) with a 45-degree mirror and an oiled paper screen. In 1685 Johann Zahn proposed [a hand-held device with a mirror-reflex mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_obscura), a design later used in photographic cameras. Through the 18th century, portable boxes were [extensively used by amateur artists on their travels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_obscura), and the painter Joshua Reynolds owned [a camera disguised as a book, now in the Science Museum in London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_obscura).

## The box had to wait for the chemistry

Thomas Wedgwood, a son of the potter Josiah Wedgwood, tried to record camera images on paper and white leather treated with silver nitrate, but [those attempts were unsuccessful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Wedgwood_(photographer)). Humphry Davy, writing up the experiments in 1802, stated the problem plainly: "The images formed by means of a camera obscura have been found too faint to produce, in any moderate time, an effect upon the nitrate of silver," in the [full text of his account, reprinted in a 1903 biography of Wedgwood](https://archive.org/details/tomwedgwoodfirst00litc).

Wedgwood did capture silhouettes of objects laid on the treated surface in direct sunlight, but [unless kept in complete darkness, they would slowly darken all over](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Wedgwood_(photographer)). Davy saw what was missing:

> "Nothing but a method of preventing the unshaded parts of the delineation from being coloured by exposure to the day is wanting, to render the process as useful as it is elegant." - Humphry Davy, [1802](https://archive.org/details/tomwedgwoodfirst00litc)

Niepce got further. He made [legible but fleeting camera pictures in 1816](https://www.hrc.utexas.edu/niepce-heliograph/), and in 1824, according to the [Nicephore Niepce House Museum](https://bicentennial-of-photography.org/niepce-invention-photography/) in his former home at Saint-Loup-de-Varennes, he put lithographic stones coated with bitumen at the back of a camera obscura and obtained a fixed image of a landscape.

On September 16, 1824, he wrote to his brother Claude in England: "This view was taken from your room facing Le Gras; and for this purpose, I used my largest camera obscura and my largest plate," in [the museum's translation](https://bicentennial-of-photography.org/niepce-invention-photography/). [The museum says](https://bicentennial-of-photography.org/niepce-invention-photography/) the letter "marks the birth of photography." That view has not survived, though, and the 1827 plate is [the only known surviving point de vue](https://www.hrc.utexas.edu/niepce-heliograph/), Niepce's term for a camera picture.

## Eight hours was an estimate read from the sunlight

The exposure usually quoted for the 1827 plate, about eight hours, is [the traditional estimate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/View_from_the_Window_at_Le_Gras), worked out from the picture itself: sunlight strikes buildings on opposite sides of the scene. In 1839 the official account of Niepce's early method ran longer. Reporting to the French Chamber of Deputies that July, Francois Arago said the preparations Niepce first used reacted so slowly that "ten or twelve hours proved hardly sufficient for producing a single design," time enough for the shadows to pass "from the left to the right of objects," in an [English translation published in London that year](https://archive.org/details/b29310301).

Modern work points to days. [A researcher who studied Niepce's notes and recreated his processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/View_from_the_Window_at_Le_Gras) found that the exposure must have continued for several days, and the Ransom Center's own account of the plate gives [several days of exposure to sunlight](https://www.hrc.utexas.edu/niepce-heliograph/).

The date has moved as well. Helmut Gernsheim, who tracked the plate down with his wife, Alison, in 1952, originally dated it to 1826, but [he accepted 1827](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/View_from_the_Window_at_Le_Gras) in the 1982 edition of *The Origins of Photography*, following a 1967 paper by Pierre Harmant and Paul Marillier. Their dating puts the plate [between June 4 and July 18, 1827](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/View_from_the_Window_at_Le_Gras).

## Photographic cameras went on sale in 1839

Niepce had [gone into partnership with Louis Daguerre in 1829](https://www.hrc.utexas.edu/niepce-heliograph/) and died in 1833. Daguerre [continued to make vital improvements after Niepce's death and introduced his "Daguerreotype" process in 1839](https://www.hrc.utexas.edu/niepce-heliograph/). His process and Henry Fox Talbot's were [publicly announced that January](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/View_from_the_Window_at_Le_Gras).

That year Daguerre [signed contracts with two manufacturers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susse_Fr%C3%A8res), Alphonse Giroux and Susse Freres, to produce the first commercially available photographic cameras. Giroux's camera, [built under a contract with Daguerre and Niepce's son Isidore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_camera), sold for [**400 francs**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susse_Fr%C3%A8res), and the plainer Susse Freres version cost 350.

## Kodak took the darkroom out of photography

Until the Kodak, taking photographs was [a complicated process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/You_Press_the_Button,_We_Do_the_Rest) that required photographers to develop their own pictures. George Eastman's company put the Kodak camera on the market in 1888 with the slogan "You press the button - we do the rest," according to [Kodak's own history](https://www.kodak.com/en/company/page/milestones/). The [$25 camera came loaded with film for 100 exposures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kodak), and owners mailed it back to Rochester with $10 to get prints, negatives and a new roll.

In 1900 the company introduced the Brownie, which [sold for $1 and used film that sold for 15 cents a roll](https://www.kodak.com/en/company/page/milestones/), and [more than 150,000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brownie_(camera)) were shipped in its first year.

## Later cameras did away with the darkroom

On February 21, 1947, Edwin Land, whose polarizing filters had already gone into [sunglasses](/blog/when-were-sunglasses-invented/), sat for his portrait at [a meeting of the Optical Society of America](https://www.library.hbs.edu/hc/polaroid/instant-photography/introducing-one-step-photography/) in New York City. He pulled the film from the camera and, [after one minute, peeled away an 8-by-10-inch print](https://www.library.hbs.edu/hc/polaroid/instant-photography/introducing-one-step-photography/). A *New York Times* headline [took after Kodak's slogan](https://www.library.hbs.edu/hc/polaroid/instant-photography/introducing-one-step-photography/): "The Camera Does the Rest."

In 1959 the cameras of the Soviet probe [Luna 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_3) photographed [the far side of the Moon](/blog/why-we-only-see-one-side-of-the-moon/), with a system for processing the film on board. Kodak calls what came in 1975 [the world's first digital camera](https://www.kodak.com/en/company/page/milestones/): a toaster-sized prototype built by its engineer [Steven Sasson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Sasson) that captured black-and-white images of **10,000 pixels**.

The oldest surviving camera photograph needed several days of sunlight, and by then the camera was already a centuries-old invention. What Niepce added was a coating that could keep the picture. Strictly FYI.
