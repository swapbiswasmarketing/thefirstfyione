Joseph Nicephore Niepce slid a polished pewter plate into a camera in 1827 and set the box near a window of [his second-story workroom](https://www.hrc.utexas.edu/niepce-heliograph/). The plate carried a thin coat of bitumen, a natural asphalt that [hardens where light falls on it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/View_from_the_Window_at_Le_Gras), and after a long exposure to sunlight it held an image of the buildings and countryside around his estate, Le Gras, at Saint-Loup-de-Varennes.

That plate, now known as View from the Window at Le Gras, is [the earliest surviving photograph produced in the camera obscura](https://www.hrc.utexas.edu/niepce-heliograph/), in the words of the Harry Ransom Center at the University of Texas at Austin, which owns it. The camera was the old part of the experiment. Its name is [short for camera obscura](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/camera), New Latin for "dark chamber," and the camera obscura had served [as an aid to drawing](https://archive.org/details/aa052-TheHistoryOfPhotography) since the Renaissance.

## When were cameras invented?

Cameras were invented long before photography: the pinhole image behind them was [described in China's *Mozi* in the 4th century BC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_obscura), and a portable camera obscura box with a 45-degree mirror was described in **1676**, about a century and a half before [the oldest surviving camera photograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/View_from_the_Window_at_Le_Gras) in **1827**. [The first photographic cameras built for sale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susse_Fr%C3%A8res) appeared in **1839**.

## The first cameras were rooms

Light entering a darkened room or box through a small hole will [project an upside-down image](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/sun-in-early-modernity/03-optics-telescopes-and-technological-change/new-optical-technologies/) of the scene outside onto the opposite surface. Ibn al-Haytham, an Arabic mathematical scientist who [was born in Basra about 965 CE and died in Cairo about 1040 CE](https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/ibn-al-haytham/), studied the effect in a treatise, *On the Shape of the Eclipse*, that records [the first scientific analysis of the camera obscura](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-319-47991-0), according to the publisher of the historian Dominique Raynaud's critical edition.

[The publisher's summary](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-319-47991-0) adds that Ibn al-Haytham ran his experiments "by varying all that could be changed: the shape and size of the aperture, the focal length of the camera obscura, the distance and shape of the celestial bodies."

The mathematician Gemma Frisius [used a camera obscura to observe the solar eclipse in Leuven, Belgium, on January 24, 1544](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/sun-in-early-modernity/03-optics-telescopes-and-technological-change/new-optical-technologies/), and [his book of 1545 holds the oldest known published drawing of a camera obscura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_obscura); the earliest known use of the term itself is in Johannes Kepler's treatise on optics of 1604.

That book describes a real room. Kepler reported an experiment he had seen in the Dresden Kunstkammer, in "one of the rooms there, which had been turned in its entirety into a camera obscura," as the historian Sven Dupre [summarizes it](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/items/d80721ed-74a7-47b9-b344-fc6fef88151b).

In 1676 Johann Christoph Sturm, a professor of mathematics at Altdorf, described [a portable reflex camera obscura](https://archive.org/details/aa052-TheHistoryOfPhotography) in which a mirror set at 45 degrees threw the image right way up onto oiled paper, and in 1685 Johann Zahn illustrated small box cameras, one of them a reflex type, that the historian Helmut Gernsheim called prototypes of nineteenth-century box and reflex cameras. By the 18th century the box could fold away: the painter Joshua Reynolds [used and owned a folding camera obscura in the form of a book](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co3311/folding-camera-obscura-in-the-form-of-a-book), probably made in Britain between 1751 and 1790 and now in the Science Museum Group collection.

## The box had to wait for the chemistry

Thomas Wedgwood, a son of the potter Josiah Wedgwood, tried to record camera images on paper and white leather treated with silver nitrate, but [those attempts were unsuccessful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Wedgwood_(photographer)). Humphry Davy, writing up the experiments in 1802, stated the problem plainly: "The images formed by means of a camera obscura have been found too faint to produce, in any moderate time, an effect upon the nitrate of silver," in the [full text of his account, reprinted in a 1903 biography of Wedgwood](https://archive.org/details/tomwedgwoodfirst00litc).

Wedgwood did capture silhouettes of objects laid on the treated surface in direct sunlight, but [unless kept in complete darkness, they would slowly darken all over](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Wedgwood_(photographer)). Davy saw what was missing:

> "Nothing but a method of preventing the unshaded parts of the delineation from being coloured by exposure to the day is wanting, to render the process as useful as it is elegant." - Humphry Davy, [1802](https://archive.org/details/tomwedgwoodfirst00litc)

Niepce got further. At his family estate in Saint-Loup-de-Varennes, in Burgundy, he made legible but fleeting camera pictures, which he called points de vue, [in 1816](https://www.hrc.utexas.edu/niepce-heliograph/), and in 1824, according to the [Nicephore Niepce House Museum](https://bicentennial-of-photography.org/niepce-invention-photography/), he put lithographic stones coated with bitumen at the back of a camera obscura and obtained a fixed image of a landscape.

On September 16, 1824, he wrote to his brother Claude in England: "This view was taken from your room facing Le Gras; and for this purpose, I used my largest camera obscura and my largest plate," in [the museum's translation](https://bicentennial-of-photography.org/niepce-invention-photography/). [The museum says](https://bicentennial-of-photography.org/niepce-invention-photography/) the letter "marks the birth of photography." That view has not survived, though, and the 1827 plate is [the only known surviving point de vue](https://www.hrc.utexas.edu/niepce-heliograph/).

## Eight hours was one estimate among several

The exposure usually quoted for the 1827 plate, about eight hours, is the figure Gernsheim gave: he wrote that it ["lasted about 8 hours on a summer day"](https://archive.org/details/aa052-TheHistoryOfPhotography), a length he said was "confirmed by Niepce's son" and evident in the picture itself, "in which the sun seems to be shining on both sides of the courtyard". In 1839 the official account of Niepce's early method ran longer. Reporting to the French Chamber of Deputies that July, Francois Arago said the preparations Niepce first used reacted so slowly that "ten or twelve hours proved hardly sufficient for producing a single design," time enough for the shadows to pass "from the left to the right of objects," in an [English translation published in London that year](https://archive.org/details/b29310301).

Most reconstructions of the process put the exposure at 12 to 18 hours in conditions like those at Le Gras, says an editors' note to [a 1997 French translation of Gernsheim's 1977 account of the find](https://journals.openedition.org/etudesphotographiques/92). [The same note](https://journals.openedition.org/etudesphotographiques/92) calls the 40 to 60 hours, about five days, that the researcher Jean-Louis Marignier obtained an exception, and adds that the two men argued over the point in 1990 and 1991. The Niepce House Museum puts the exposure in a camera obscura at ["quite a few days in broad sunlight"](https://bicentennial-of-photography.org/niepce-invention-photography/).

The date has moved as well. Gernsheim, who tracked the plate down with his wife, Alison, in 1952, originally dated it to 1826, but [in 1982 he accepted 1827](https://journals.openedition.org/etudesphotographiques/92), the date proposed in 1967 by Pierre Harmant and Paul Marillier. Their dating puts the plate [between June 4 and July 18, 1827](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/View_from_the_Window_at_Le_Gras).

## Photographic cameras went on sale in 1839

Niepce [went into partnership with Louis Daguerre in 1829 and died in 1833](https://bicentennial-of-photography.org/life-nicephore-niepce/). His son Isidore [took his place in the company](https://bicentennial-of-photography.org/isidore-niepce-daguerre/), while Daguerre worked on a faster process of his own.

Daguerre's process and Henry Fox Talbot's were [publicly announced in January 1839](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/View_from_the_Window_at_Le_Gras), and on August 19, 1839, the French Academy of Sciences [divulged the processes](https://bicentennial-of-photography.org/isidore-niepce-daguerre/) of heliography, the physautotype and the daguerreotype. That year two firms, Alphonse Giroux and Susse Freres, [made the first photographic cameras sold to the public](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susse_Fr%C3%A8res). Giroux's cameras carried a label hand-signed by Daguerre and [sold for **400 francs**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susse_Fr%C3%A8res), and the plainer Susse Freres version cost 350.

## Kodak took the darkroom out of photography

Until the Kodak, taking photographs was [a complicated process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/You_Press_the_Button,_We_Do_the_Rest) that required photographers to develop their own pictures. George Eastman's company put the Kodak camera on the market in 1888 with the slogan "You press the button - we do the rest," according to [Kodak's own history](https://www.kodak.com/en/company/page/milestones/). The [$25 camera came loaded with film for 100 exposures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kodak), and owners mailed it back to Rochester with $10 to get prints, negatives and a new roll.

In 1900 the company introduced the Brownie, which [sold for $1 and used film that sold for 15 cents a roll](https://www.kodak.com/en/company/page/milestones/), and [more than 150,000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brownie_(camera)) were shipped in its first year.

## Later cameras did away with the darkroom

On February 21, 1947, Edwin Land sat for his portrait at [a meeting of the Optical Society of America](https://www.library.hbs.edu/hc/polaroid/instant-photography/introducing-one-step-photography/) in New York City. He pulled the film from the camera and, [after one minute, peeled away an 8-by-10-inch print](https://www.library.hbs.edu/hc/polaroid/instant-photography/introducing-one-step-photography/). A *New York Times* headline [took after Kodak's slogan](https://www.library.hbs.edu/hc/polaroid/instant-photography/introducing-one-step-photography/): "The Camera Does the Rest."

In 1959 the cameras of the Soviet probe [Luna 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luna_3) photographed [the far side of the Moon](/blog/why-we-only-see-one-side-of-the-moon/), with a system for processing the film on board. Kodak calls what came in 1975 [the world's first digital camera](https://www.kodak.com/en/company/page/milestones/): a toaster-sized prototype built by its engineer [Steven Sasson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Sasson) that captured black-and-white images of **10,000 pixels**.

The oldest surviving camera photograph needed hours or even days of sunlight, and by then the camera was already a centuries-old invention. What Niepce added was a coating that could keep the picture. Strictly FYI.
