In March and September of 1876, a medical student named Sigmund Freud worked at the zoological station in Trieste on a problem suggested by his professor, Carl Claus: the sex organs of the eel. Claus also had a batch of eels sent from Trieste to Vienna for him between October and January, and Freud's report to the Vienna academy, presented on 15 March 1877, says he examined ["etwa 400 Aale"](https://archive.org/details/sitzungsberichte75kais), about **400 eels**.

He did find something. In some of them he found the lobed organ that the zoologist Szymon Syrski had proposed as the male gland, but under the microscope its tissue [neither proved nor ruled out](https://archive.org/details/sitzungsberichte75kais) that it was a testis, and, Freud added, nobody had yet seen a mature lobed organ or a mature male eel. The man who would later build a theory of human sexuality closed his eel paper without an answer, and he was in old company.

## How do eels reproduce?

Freshwater eels such as the European, American and Japanese eels reproduce by spawning far out in the open ocean at the end of a one-way migration, and the adults do not come back. American eels [return to the Sargasso Sea, where they spawn and die](https://asmfc.org/species/american-eel), and European eels make a journey of [5000 to 10,000 km](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9562336/) across the Atlantic to the Sargasso Sea to reproduce.

Every part of that answer took centuries to work out.

## Two thousand years of guessing

The puzzle began with a real observation. In Book VI of the *History of Animals*, [Aristotle](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/history_anim.6.vi.html) wrote that "nor was an eel ever found supplied with either milt or spawn," and that eels cut open showed no passages for eggs.

His answer, in [D'Arcy Thompson's translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/history_anim.6.vi.html), was that "Eels are derived from the so-called 'earth's guts' that grow spontaneously in mud and in humid ground," and that eels had been seen to emerge from such earthworms. No parents, no eggs: a fish assembled by wet soil.

More than two thousand years later, Freud wrote down the likely explanation without being able to prove it. If the lobed organ was the testis, he noted, things would stand as von Siebold had put it in his book on the freshwater fishes of Central Europe: eels go out to sea [not in the least prepared](https://archive.org/details/sitzungsberichte75kais) for breeding.

Later work bore that out. European eels, according to a [review of their swimming physiology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2923712/) by Arjan Palstra and Guido van den Thillart, "leave in a prepubertal condition with less than 2% relative gonad mass," and mature only after months at sea. Syrski had been right about the organ, too: the [Austrian Biographical Lexicon](https://www.biographien.ac.at/oebl/oebl_S/Syrski_Szymon-Adam_1829_1882.xml) credits him with discovering the eel's male gonads.

Freud's paper holds a second clue. He soon stopped examining eels longer than half a meter, because he could not find Syrski's organ in [any of these large animals](https://archive.org/details/sitzungsberichte75kais). Departing females average about 80 cm and males about 40 cm, [the Palstra review says](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2923712/), so the big eels he set aside were most likely females.

## An eel passes through five forms, and the first looks nothing like an eel

The [Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission](https://asmfc.org/species/american-eel) describes the American eel's life cycle, from larvae carried away from the Sargasso Sea to silver eels returning there, and says elvers and yellow eels spend "between 3 and 30 or more years" in estuaries or fresh water.

| Stage | What it looks like | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Leptocephalus | Transparent, leaf-shaped larva | Open ocean |
| Glass eel | See-through, eel-shaped | Continental shelf and coasts |
| Elver | Small pigmented eel | Estuaries and rivers |
| Yellow eel | The familiar eel, still immature | Estuaries and fresh water, for years |
| Silver eel | Maturing migrant | Back to the ocean |

The leptocephalus is so unlike an adult eel that it was classified as a separate species, until Grassi and Calandruccio showed in 1896 that the little fish [described by Kaup in 1856](https://archive.org/details/dli.ministry.09405) as *Leptocephalus brevirostris* is "not an independent species, but the larva of the eel."

Grassi thought he had solved the rest as well. Eels, he argued, bred in the deep Mediterranean, and only the currents of the Straits of Messina, which ["tear up the deep-sea bottom"](https://archive.org/details/dli.ministry.09405), ever brought the larvae up where people could see them.

The silver stage is the turn toward the sea. Each autumn, [the Palstra review says](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2923712/), "a part of the population ceases feeding, becomes restless, starts to mature and changes into migrant silver eels."

## Schmidt followed the larvae to the Sargasso

In May 1904, on the research steamer *Thor* west of the Faroes, the Danish biologist Johannes Schmidt netted a single *Leptocephalus brevirostris*, and the eel larva was [for the first time found outside the Mediterranean](https://archive.org/details/dli.ministry.09405). His logic was simple: the smaller the larva, the nearer its birthplace.

Over seventeen or eighteen years of cruises "ranging from America to Egypt, from Iceland to the Cape Verde Islands," he found larvae less than 10 mm long, too small to have drifted far, in one patch of the western Atlantic between about 22 and 30 degrees north and 48 and 65 degrees west, [in no fewer than five different years](https://archive.org/details/dli.ministry.09405) from 1914 to 1922. He set out the case in the Royal Society's *Philosophical Transactions* as ["The breeding places of the eel"](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.1923.0004).

Schmidt had found babies, not births. A century later, a 2022 paper in *Scientific Reports* still had to note that "despite the passage of almost 100 years since Johannes Schmidt proposed the Sargasso Sea as the breeding place of European eels on the basis of larval surveys, [no eggs or spawning adults have ever been sampled there to confirm this](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9562336/)."

That paper's team, led by Rosalind Wright of England's Environment Agency, fitted satellite tags to 26 female silver eels from the Azores in November and December of 2018 and 2019; five tags surfaced within the Sargasso Sea's boundaries, one eel was located within the presumed breeding area, and the authors called the result ["the first direct evidence of adult European eels reaching their presumed breeding place in the Sargasso Sea"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9562336/).

> The larvae drift out of the Sargasso and the silver adults swim into it. For the Atlantic eel, what happens in between has still only been inferred.

## In the Pacific, the nets finally caught eggs

The western North Pacific has given up more. In May 2009, near the West Mariana Ridge, a Japanese team collected [31 eggs of the Japanese eel](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3105336/), the first eggs of any freshwater eel species found in the ocean, close to where adults in spawning condition and newly hatched larvae were also caught.

Nobody watched the spawning itself; Katsumi Tsukamoto's team read its timing and depth from the catches. Spawning, they concluded, [occurs "during new moon periods"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3105336/) and "in shallower layers of 150-200 m and not at great depths."

The females' ovaries were [polycyclic, "suggesting that freshwater eels can spawn more than once during a spawning season"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3105336/). Their bodies set the limit: the adults had degenerated digestive tracts with no visible stomach or intestine contents, and the team concluded that these eels ["have only one spawning season in their life and die after spawning a few times."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3105336/)

## The gap matters for a fish in trouble

None of this is idle. The European eel is critically endangered, and its recruitment has fallen [by 95% since the 1980s](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9562336/), which the Wright paper attributes partly to what happens during the marine phases of its life. Protecting a spawning ground is harder when nobody has caught an egg there.

So the honest answer to "how do eels reproduce" is: far away, at the end of their lives, and in the Atlantic still off the record. Somewhere in the Sargasso Sea, the fish that Aristotle, Freud and Schmidt all went looking for is doing the one thing nobody has yet caught it doing. Strictly FYI.
