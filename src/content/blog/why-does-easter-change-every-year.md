"Easter Day is always the Sunday after the full moon that occurs on or after the spring equinox on March 21," begins the rule printed in the Episcopal Church's [Book of Common Prayer](https://www.bcponline.org/Misc/tables.html). The same sentence then qualifies its own equinox. March 21, it says, is "a date which is fixed in accordance with an ancient ecclesiastical computation, and which does not always correspond to the astronomical equinox."

The full moon in that rule does not come from the sky either. The prayer book finds it with arithmetic: add 1 to the year and divide by 19, and the remainder is the year's Golden Number. Printed beside a day of March or April in the book's calendar, that number "marks the date of the full moon in that year."

## Why does Easter change every year?

Easter changes every year because it follows the moon, and 12 lunar months fall about 11 days short of a year. Western churches keep it on the first Sunday after the church tables' full moon on or after **March 21**, their fixed date for the equinox, so it can land on any Sunday from **March 22 to April 25**.

The link to the moon goes back to the feast's origins. The New Testament ties the death and resurrection of Jesus to the Jewish Passover, which, a 1997 [World Council of Churches statement](https://www.oikoumene.org/resources/documents/towards-a-common-date-for-easter) notes, was originally "celebrated on the first full moon after the March equinox." By the end of the second century some churches kept Easter on the day of Passover itself, whatever the weekday, "while others celebrated it on the following Sunday." A full moon could hold up more than a feast: in 490 BC, Herodotus writes, Sparta agreed to help Athens at Marathon but [would not march until the moon was full](/blog/why-is-a-marathon-26-2-miles/).

## Church Easter and sky Easter can be a month apart

The tables usually land close to the sky. The [Old Farmer's Almanac](https://www.almanac.com/content/when-is-easter) says the church's full moon "can be a day or two different from the Full Moon we see in the sky," and that the real equinox can fall on March 19, 20 or 21. Near the March 21 cutoff, a day or two is enough to move Easter by a month.

That happened in **2019**. The WCC statement comes with a table of Easter dates for 2001 to 2025, worked out both from astronomy and from the church tables, and it puts 2019's first full moon after the equinox on **March 21**. Easter by the sky would have fallen on March 24. The tables' own March full moon came before the cutoff, so Western churches waited a lunar month and kept Easter on **April 21**. Across the table's 25 years, it is the only year the two methods disagree.

Both the Eastern and Western calculations "rely upon conventional tables for determining the lunar cycle," the statement says, and those tables "at times give results that diverge from the astronomical data."

> "The Church throughout held that the determination of Easter was primarily a matter of ecclesiastical discipline and not of astronomical science." - Herbert Thurston, *Catholic Encyclopedia*, 1909

## March 22 and April 25 are the outer limits

In the prayer book's words, the church full moon "may happen on any date between March 21 and April 18 inclusive," and "if the full moon falls on a Sunday, Easter Day is the Sunday following." The earliest Easter, **March 22**, therefore needs that moon on a Saturday, March 21. The latest, **April 25**, needs it on Sunday, April 18, so that Easter waits a full week.

Both ends are rare. The prayer book's own table, which runs from 1900 to 2089, puts Easter on April 25 twice, in **1943** and **2038**, and never on March 22. Its earliest Easters are March 23, in 1913 and 2008. Easter last fell on March 22 in 1818, and the next will be in 2285.

## The Nicaea rule that no council text contains

The usual story says the Council of Nicaea set the full-moon rule in **325**. The WCC statement repeats it: the council "determined that Easter/Pascha should be celebrated on the Sunday following the first vernal full moon." None of the council's [20 canons](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3801.htm) mentions Easter, though, and the text of its Easter decree, Thurston wrote in his article on the [Easter controversy](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05228a.htm), "has not been preserved to us."

Two letters written at the time report the decision. The council told the church of Alexandria that brethren in the East who had followed the Jewish custom would now keep the feast at the same time as Rome and Alexandria. The emperor Constantine's letter, preserved by [Eusebius](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/25023.htm), says the bishops resolved that Easter "ought to be kept by all and in every place on one and the same day." Neither letter names an equinox or a full moon.

By the sixth century the method carried the council's name. Dionysius Exiguus, a monk in Rome, wrote in the [preface to his Easter tables](https://henk-reints.nl/cal/audette/denys.html) that the 318 bishops at Nicaea had fixed the paschal moons in a 19-year cycle, guided less by worldly learning than by the Holy Spirit. Thurston inferred "from scattered notices" that the council did endorse the equinox principle, and the WCC says it "did not itself undertake a detailed regulation of the Easter calculation." Calling the formula a decree of Nicaea is a later attribution.

## Alexandria's tables won, by way of Rome and Whitby

The working method came from Alexandria, which, in Thurston's words, "adhered to the more accurate nineteen-year cycle of Meton." Dionysius [advocated that cycle](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05010b.htm) and carried a table by Cyril of Alexandria 95 years forward, publishing his tables in **525**; Rome came to follow them. In the same work he counted years from the Incarnation instead of from the reign of Diocletian, unwilling to keep alive the name of "an impious persecutor." That count is the origin of AD dates.

Northumbria, taught by Irish missionaries, kept an older reckoning until the Synod of Whitby in **664**, where its Christians "accepted the Roman system." The dispute "is told at length in the pages of Bede," the Northumbrian monk who [gave the medieval moment its 90 seconds](/blog/how-long-is-a-moment/), and Bede also records that the monks of Iona held out until 716.

## Orthodox Easter runs on the older calendar

By the 16th century the gap between the old calculation and the observed sky "was becoming evident," the WCC statement says, and Pope Gregory XIII changed the calendar in **1582**. Western churches moved to the Gregorian calendar over the following two centuries, while the Eastern churches "generally have continued to follow the older Julian calendar." Calendar changes in some Orthodox churches in 1923 moved their fixed-date feasts, but the Easter calculation "remained linked to the Julian Calendar."

The Julian calendar, [Julius Caesar's 365-day reform of 46 BC](/blog/why-does-february-have-28-days/), now runs **13 days** behind, three more than the 10 days that [separate the Mayflower passengers' dates from ours](/blog/when-did-the-mayflower-land/). Its March 21 is April 3 on the Gregorian calendar. The rule is the same in East and West, and the differences, in the WCC's words, come from "the calendars and lunar tables employed." The old Julian tables also put the church full moon a few days after the real one.

In the WCC table, "by the sky" means astronomical reckoning for the meridian of Jerusalem.

| Year | By the sky | Western | Orthodox |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2017 | April 16 | April 16 | April 16 |
| 2019 | March 24 | April 21 | April 28 |
| 2021 | April 4 | April 4 | May 2 |
| 2023 | April 9 | April 9 | April 16 |
| 2024 | March 31 | March 31 | May 5 |

From 2001 to 2024, Orthodox Easter fell on the same Sunday as Western Easter **7** times, a week later **10** times and four or five weeks later **7** times. It never came first. The table's 2025 row prints the Orthodox date as May 20, a slip: both churches kept Easter on **April 20, 2025**, 1,700 years after Nicaea.

## Britain fixed the date in 1928, on paper

Parliament passed the [Easter Act 1928](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Geo5/18-19/35) to fix the date. It makes Easter "the first Sunday after the second Saturday in April," which would pin it between April 9 and April 15. The Act starts only when an Order in Council, approved in draft by both Houses, brings it in, and before the draft is made "regard shall be had to any opinion officially expressed by any Church or other Christian body." The government's legislation website still notes that it "contains provisions that are prospective."

The statute "has never been brought into force," the British Religion in Numbers project [records](https://www.brin.ac.uk/sources/preference-for-a-fixed-date-for-easter-4345/), "pending agreement within the Churches about the change (which has yet to be reached)."

The Second Vatican Council said in 1963 that it "would not object if the feast of Easter were assigned to a particular Sunday of the Gregorian Calendar," provided that "those whom it may concern, especially the brethren who are not in communion with the Apostolic See, give their assent." The WCC's 1997 proposal went the other way: keep the full-moon rule, but calculate the equinox and full moon "by the most accurate possible scientific means," using the meridian of Jerusalem.

Until the churches agree, Britain's 1928 law stays unused, and Easter keeps following a full moon worked out from the number of the year. Strictly FYI.
