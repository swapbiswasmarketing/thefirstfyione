"Easter Day is always the Sunday after the full moon that occurs on or after the spring equinox on March 21," begins the rule printed in the Episcopal Church's [Book of Common Prayer](https://www.bcponline.org/Misc/tables.html). The same sentence then qualifies its own equinox. March 21, it says, is "a date which is fixed in accordance with an ancient ecclesiastical computation, and which does not always correspond to the astronomical equinox."

The full moon in that rule does not come from the sky either. The prayer book finds it with arithmetic: add 1 to the year and divide by 19, and the remainder, or 19 if nothing remains, is the year's [Golden Number](https://www.bcponline.org/Misc/tables.html). Printed beside a day of March or April in the book's calendar, that number "marks the date of the full moon in that year."

## Why does Easter change every year?

Easter changes every year because it follows the moon, and 12 lunar months fall [about 11 days](https://www.tondering.dk/claus/cal/islamic.php) short of a solar year. Western churches keep it on the first Sunday after the church tables' full moon on or after **March 21**, their fixed date for the equinox, so it can land on any Sunday from **March 22 to April 25**, the limits the [US Naval Observatory](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/easter) gives.

The link to the moon goes back to the feast's origins. The New Testament ties the death and resurrection of Jesus to the Jewish Passover, which, a 1997 [statement](https://www.oikoumene.org/resources/documents/towards-a-common-date-for-easter) from a World Council of Churches consultation in Aleppo notes, was originally "celebrated on the first full moon after the March equinox." By the end of the second century some churches kept Easter on the day of Passover itself, whatever the weekday, "while others celebrated it on the following Sunday."

## Church Easter and sky Easter can be a month apart

The tables usually land close to the sky. The church's full moon "may be one or two days off from the date of the astronomical full Moon," [the Old Farmer's Almanac](https://www.almanac.com/easter-paschal-full-moon) explains, and the church equinox stays on March 21 "even if the astronomical date is March 19 or 20." Near the March 21 cutoff, a day or two is enough to move Easter by a month.

That happened in **2019**. The equinox came on March 20 and the full moon [about four hours later](https://www.almanac.com/easter-paschal-full-moon), so Easter by the sky would have fallen on Sunday, March 24. The church full moon came before the church equinox, though, so Western churches waited a lunar month and kept Easter on **April 21**.

The [US Naval Observatory](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/easter) describes the same split in 1962. The real full moon came on March 21, "about six hours after astronomical equinox," but the tables put it on March 20, a day before the church equinox, and Easter waited until April 22.

> "The Church throughout held that the determination of Easter was primarily a matter of ecclesiastical discipline and not of astronomical science." - Herbert Thurston, [*Catholic Encyclopedia*](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05228a.htm), 1909

## March 22 and April 25 are the outer limits

In the [prayer book's words](https://www.bcponline.org/Misc/tables.html), the church full moon "may happen on any date between March 21 and April 18 inclusive," and "if the full moon falls on a Sunday, Easter Day is the Sunday following." The earliest Easter, **March 22**, therefore needs that moon on a Saturday, March 21. The latest, **April 25**, needs it on Sunday, April 18, so that Easter waits a full week.

Both ends are rare. The prayer book's own [table](https://www.bcponline.org/Misc/tables.html), which runs from 1900 to 2089, puts Easter on April 25 twice, in **1943** and **2038**, and never on March 22. Its earliest Easters are March 23, in 1913 and 2008. Easter [last fell on March 22](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~gent0113/easter/easter_text2c.htm) in 1818, and the next will be in 2285.

## The Nicaea rule that no council text contains

The usual story, repeated on the [US Naval Observatory's](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/easter) Easter page, says the Council of Nicaea "constructed tables to compute the date" in **325**. None of the council's [20 canons](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3801.htm) mentions Easter, though, and the text of its Easter decree, Thurston wrote in his article on the [Easter controversy](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05228a.htm), "has not been preserved to us." Even so, he inferred from scattered notices that the council ruled Easter must be the Sunday after the fourteenth day of the paschal moon, the moon whose fourteenth day followed the spring equinox.

Two letters written at the time report the decision. The [council's letter](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/26011.htm) to the church of Alexandria says that "all the brethren in the East who have heretofore kept this festival when the Jews did, will henceforth conform to the Romans and to us." The emperor Constantine's letter, preserved by [Eusebius](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/25023.htm), says the bishops resolved that Easter "ought to be kept by all and in every place on one and the same day." Neither letter names an equinox or a full moon.

By 386 the method already carried the council's name: Ambrose of Milan [wrote](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/ambrose_letters_03_letters21_30.htm) that the fathers at Nicaea had formed "a plan for nineteen years with the aid of the most skilful calculators." In the sixth century Dionysius Exiguus, a monk in Rome, wrote in the [preface to his Easter tables](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/dionysius_exiguus_easter_01.htm) that the "318 pontiffs" at Nicaea had fixed the paschal moons in a cycle of 19 years, "not so much from secular knowledge as by the illumination of the Holy Spirit."

The [WCC statement](https://www.oikoumene.org/resources/documents/towards-a-common-date-for-easter) says the council "determined that Easter/Pascha should be celebrated on the Sunday following the first vernal full moon," yet "did not itself undertake a detailed regulation of the Easter calculation." The council's own words are lost, and no text from the council sets out the cycle that Ambrose and Dionysius credited to it.

## Alexandria's tables won, by way of Rome and Whitby

The working method came from Alexandria, which, in [Thurston's words](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05228a.htm), "adhered to the more accurate nineteen-year cycle of Meton." Rome, he wrote, finally adopted it with the tables of Dionysius, who had [advocated the cycle](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05010b.htm) and carried a table by Cyril of Alexandria ninety-five years forward. The worked examples in that work count [525 years](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/dionysius_exiguus_easter_01.htm) from the Incarnation. Dionysius counted from the Incarnation instead of from the reign of Diocletian, not wishing, his [preface](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/dionysius_exiguus_easter_01.htm) says, to bind to his cycles "the memory of this impious man and persecutor." That count is the origin of AD dates.

Northumbria, taught by Irish missionaries, kept an older reckoning until the Synod of Whitby in **664**, where its Christians, [in Thurston's account](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05228a.htm), "accepted the Roman system." The story of that controversy, he notes, "is told at length in the pages of Bede." Bede, a Northumbrian monk, records that the monks of Iona, his Hii, began to keep [the canonical Easter](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/basis/bede-book5.asp) only in 716.

## Orthodox Easter runs on the older calendar

By the 16th century the gap between the old calculation and the observed sky "was becoming evident," the [WCC statement](https://www.oikoumene.org/resources/documents/towards-a-common-date-for-easter) says, and Pope Gregory XIII changed the calendar in **1582**. Western churches came to calculate Easter on the Gregorian calendar, while the Eastern churches "generally have continued to follow the older Julian calendar," which "at present diverges from the astronomical by thirteen days." A 1923 Pan-Orthodox congress led some Orthodox churches to revise their calendar for fixed-date feasts, but the Easter calculation "remained linked to the Julian Calendar."

The rule is the same in East and West; the inputs differ. On the [Julian calendar](/blog/why-does-february-have-28-days/), March 21 falls on Gregorian April 3, so the Orthodox count starts thirteen days later, and it uses its own lunar tables.

The statement proposed keeping the full-moon rule but working out the equinox and full moon "by the most accurate possible scientific means," using the meridian of Jerusalem, and [its table](https://www.oikoumene.org/resources/documents/towards-a-common-date-for-easter) sets that result beside the Western and Orthodox dates for 2001 to 2025. Western Easter and Easter by the sky part only once in those 25 years, in 2019; such splits happen, the statement explains, because both the Julian and the Gregorian calculations "rely upon conventional tables for determining the lunar cycle." From 2001 to 2024, Orthodox Easter fell on the same Sunday as Western Easter **7** times, a week later **10** times and four or five weeks later **7** times, and it never came first.

| Year | By the sky | Western | Orthodox |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2017 | April 16 | April 16 | April 16 |
| 2019 | March 24 | April 21 | April 28 |
| 2021 | April 4 | April 4 | May 2 |
| 2023 | April 9 | April 9 | April 16 |
| 2024 | March 31 | March 31 | May 5 |

In 2025, [Easter fell on April 20](https://www.oikoumene.org/news/wcc-invites-all-to-support-vision-for-common-easter-date) for Eastern and Western churches alike, in what the WCC marked as the 1700th anniversary year of the Council of Nicaea.

## Britain fixed the date in 1928, on paper

Parliament passed the [Easter Act 1928](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Easter_Act_1928) to make Easter "the first Sunday after the second Saturday in April." It would replace the rule in the Calendar (New Style) Act 1750, under which Easter "is always the first Sunday after the full moon which happens upon or next after the twenty-first day of March." The Act starts only when an Order in Council, approved in draft by both Houses, brings it in, and before the draft is made "regard shall be had to any opinion officially expressed by any Church or other Christian body."

The second Saturday in April always falls between the 8th and the 14th, so the Act's Easter could only ever land between April 9 and April 15.

The statute "has never been brought into force," the British Religion in Numbers project [records](https://www.brin.ac.uk/sources/preference-for-a-fixed-date-for-easter-4345/), "pending agreement within the Churches about the change (which has yet to be reached)."

The [Second Vatican Council](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19631204_sacrosanctum-concilium_en.html) said in 1963 that it "would not object if the feast of Easter were assigned to a particular Sunday of the Gregorian Calendar," provided that "those whom it may concern, especially the brethren who are not in communion with the Apostolic See, give their assent." The WCC's Aleppo proposal, keeping the moon but measuring it from Jerusalem, went the other way.

Until the churches agree, Britain's 1928 law stays unused, and Easter keeps following a full moon worked out from the number of the year. Strictly FYI.
