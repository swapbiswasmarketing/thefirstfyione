Oxtail comes from oxen. The name says so, and an encyclopedia entry adds a twist: the word ["once meant only the tail of an ox"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxtail) and only later came to cover the tails of other cattle. Read that way, the ox was one particular animal, and the butcher has since swapped in others.

The Oxford English Dictionary gave ox a wider first meaning. In the dictionary's first edition, in the volume dated 1909, the [entry for ox](https://archive.org/details/newenglishdic07murruoft/page/346/mode/1up) defines it first as the "domestic bovine quadruped," noting in parentheses that its sexes are distinguished as "bull and cow." In that sense a cow is an ox, and so is every steer and heifer sent to slaughter.

## Where do oxtails come from?

Oxtail is the tail of cattle killed for beef, sold whole or cut into pieces at the joints. Of the cattle slaughtered under [US federal inspection in 2025](https://esmis.nal.usda.gov/sites/default/release-files/795870/lsan0426.txt), **49.7 percent** were steers, males castrated when young, and **31.7 percent** heifers. The name is no mistake: in its wide sense, ox has long meant any domestic cattle, bull or cow.

The [same USDA summary](https://esmis.nal.usda.gov/sites/default/release-files/795870/lsan0426.txt) counted **29.8 million** cattle slaughtered commercially that year, and most of the rest were cows: dairy cows 9.0 percent and other cows 8.0 percent. The class names are defined in USDA's [grade standards for slaughter cattle](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/Slaughter_Cattle_Standard%5B1%5D.pdf), where a steer is "a male bovine castrated when young" and a heifer "an immature female bovine."

## The USDA files oxtail with the liver and the tongue

The government's own name for the cut uses both words, beef and ox. In the USDA's [Institutional Meat Purchase Specifications](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/LSimps700.pdf), item 721 is "Beef Oxtail, Trimmed," listed in the series for variety meats and edible by-products beside beef liver, heart, tongue, kidneys and tripe.

Under that [specification](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/LSimps700.pdf) the tail comes off the carcass "at the juncture of the second and third coccygeal vertebrae," the tail bones, and unless the buyer says otherwise its thin end is "tipped" to remove two or three more. Buyers can take the tails whole or disjointed.

An older animal's tail stays on the market. The federal rule against mad cow disease makes the vertebral column of cattle **30 months** and older a [specified risk material](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/9/310.22), "inedible and prohibited for use as human food," but it excludes "the vertebrae of the tail." An old cow's backbone is thrown out, and her tail can still be sold.

## Ox named all cattle, and strictly a castrated male

Edward Topsell's natural history of four-footed beasts, first printed in **1607** and [largely a translation](https://archive.org/details/b3033469x) of Conrad Gesner's *Historia animalium*, put the wide sense plainly. When he wrote, vulgar could mean ["in common or general use"](https://archive.org/details/oedxbarch/page/326/mode/1up).

> "The name Bos, or an Oxe as we say in English, is the most vulgar and ordinary name for Bugils, Bulls, Cows, Buffes, and all great cloven-footed horned beasts." - Edward Topsell, [*The History of Four-footed Beasts and Serpents*](https://archive.org/details/historyoffourfoo00tops/page/53/mode/1up), 1658

In the [same passage](https://archive.org/details/historyoffourfoo00tops/page/53/mode/1up) Topsell gave the strict sense too: "in proper speech, it signifieth a beast gelded," a castrated male. Working from Gesner, he also named cattle by age, with calves first, then "in their second age Steeres, in their third Oxen."

Both senses lasted. The [same OED entry](https://archive.org/details/newenglishdic07murruoft/page/346/mode/1up) says that in common use the word was applied to "the male castrated and used for draught purposes, or reared to serve as food." Isabella Beeton's [*Book of Household Management*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10136), first bound in 1861, prints a recipe for ox-tail soup and counts the Ayrshire, a breed she calls "peculiarly adapted for the dairy," among "the principal representative breeds of the ox species."

American usage drew the line tighter. Noah Webster's [1828 dictionary](https://webstersdictionary1828.com/Dictionary/ox) defines an ox as a castrated male "grown to his size or nearly so" and adds: "We never apply the name ox to the cow or female of the domestic kind." The plural was looser: "Oxen in the plural may comprehend both the male and female." "The young male is called in America a steer," Webster [added](https://webstersdictionary1828.com/Dictionary/ox), and the OED's [entry for steer](https://archive.org/details/newenglishdicpt109murruoft/page/902/mode/1up) records that in the United States and the colonies the word was "applied to male beef-cattle of any age."

## The "once only oxen" story is right only about age

The encyclopedia line [cites one source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxtail), the etymology dictionary Etymonline, and Etymonline records no such change: it defines [oxtail](https://web.archive.org/web/20260307210159/https://www.etymonline.com/word/oxtail) as "the tail of an ox, prepared as food." The OED's [definition of ox-tail](https://archive.org/details/newenglishdic07murruoft/page/350/mode/1up), "the tail of an ox; esp. as an article of food," does not limit it to working animals either, and [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/oxtail) now defines oxtail as "the tail of a beef animal."

What changed was the usual age of the cattle sold as beef. Lisle's book on husbandry, written before 1722 and printed in 1757, quoted in the OED's [steer entry](https://archive.org/details/newenglishdicpt109murruoft/page/902/mode/1up), held that "Steers will not be beef till four or five years old." An 1854 Northamptonshire glossary, quoted in the same entry, defined a steer as a bullock past its first year, "till it enters its fourth year, when it is termed an ox."

By those two reckonings, a steer old enough for beef was old enough to be called an ox. Younger steers were eaten too: the [same entry](https://archive.org/details/newenglishdicpt109murruoft/page/902/mode/1up) quotes a 1620 diet book on "The flesh of Steeres, which we commonly call Steere-beefe," along with William Cobbett's verdict, from his stay in the United States in 1817-18, that "Steer-beef is not nearly so good as ox-beef."

Beef now comes from "full-grown cattle about 2 years old," according to the USDA's [Food Safety and Inspection Service](https://web.archive.org/web/20230922194723/https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/meat/beef-farm-table). Those younger steers still fit the OED, whose [1919 volume](https://archive.org/details/newenglishdicpt109murruoft/page/902/mode/1up) defines a steer as "a young ox, esp. one which has been castrated." Lisle's beef cattle were old enough to be called oxen by age and today's are younger, but the wide name has fit them all along.

## Oxtail was food long before the refugee story

In 1882 the [*Standard* newspaper](https://archive.org/details/newenglishdic07murruoft/page/350/mode/1up) told its readers: "It was the Royalist refugees who taught us to prepare soup of the ox tails, which until then were tossed to the dogs." The OED prints the line under ox-tail without comment.

The [same entry](https://archive.org/details/newenglishdic07murruoft/page/350/mode/1up) opens with a line from the Towneley plays of about 1460, in which a shepherd carries ox tail "sothen and rost," boiled and roasted. The refugee story is unverified, and that earlier record cuts against its claim that the tails went to the dogs.

By 1837 the tail was worth a joke about money. In Frederick Marryat's novel [*Snarleyyow*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/21579), published that year, a crowd mocks a man who wants his dog's severed tail back: "To make soup of," replied another, laughing; "he can't afford ox-tail."

## A steer's tail now sells for nearly the price of its hide

Each animal has one tail, and it is small. USDA Market News, in its daily [by-product report](https://web.archive.org/web/20261005114503/https://www.ams.usda.gov/mnreports/ams_2829.pdf), credits a typical **1,400-pound** slaughter steer with 0.24 pounds of oxtail per hundred pounds of live weight, or about **3.4 pounds** in all.

Those few pounds have grown more valuable. The report priced selected oxtails at $308.00 per hundred pounds on [September 9, 2022](https://www.ams.usda.gov/mnreports/nw_ls441.txt) and at **$650.00** in its [October 2, 2026 edition](https://web.archive.org/web/20261005114503/https://www.ams.usda.gov/mnreports/ams_2829.pdf), which is **$6.50 a pound** wholesale.

| Per 1,400-pound steer, Oct. 2, 2026 | Pounds | Wholesale value |
|---|---|---|
| Hide | about 67 | $24.50 |
| Tongue | 3.4 | $24.78 |
| Oxtail | 3.4 | $21.84 |
| Liver | 13.4 | $11.62 |

In the [September 2022 report](https://www.ams.usda.gov/mnreports/nw_ls441.txt) a branded steer hide was priced at $31.50, about three times the value of the tail. The report gives no reason for the change.

## The same cut, cooked slowly in four cuisines

[Beeton's ox-tail soup](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/10136) separates the tails "at the joints" and simmers them for four hours, and cooks elsewhere treat the tail the same way. In Rome, *coda alla vaccinara* is oxtail, *coda di bue* in Italian, cut at the vertebrae and cooked a long time, says the [Treccani dictionary](https://www.treccani.it/vocabolario/vaccinaro/). It files the dish under *vaccinaro*, Roman dialect for a tanner and, in older use, a butcher who sold beef.

Korea's tourism agency describes [kkorigomtang](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr/svc/contents/contentsView.do?vcontsId=179172) as oxtail boiled until "the flesh naturally comes off," eaten with rice. Jamaica's tourist board lists slow-cooked oxtail "simmered with spinners (little dumplings) and butter beans" among the island's [iconic meals](https://www.visitjamaica.com/discover-jamaica/cuisine/iconic-meals/).

An American oxtail today usually comes from a steer, a heifer or a cow. In Topsell's English all three were oxen, so the name on the package has been right the whole time. Strictly FYI.
