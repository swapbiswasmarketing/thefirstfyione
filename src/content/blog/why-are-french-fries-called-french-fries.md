In 1902 an American cookbook told home cooks there were two ways to fry, and it gave one of them a name. French frying meant the food went into a kettle of hot fat deep enough to cover it. Dry frying meant a shallow pan.

Potatoes cooked the first way were French fried potatoes, and the phrase was already about fifty years old in print by the time that cookbook got round to explaining it. That one fact strands the two origin stories that usually get the credit for the name.

## Why are french fries called french fries?

Because "French frying" was a kitchen name for cooking by immersion in a deep kettle of hot fat, as against dry frying in a pan. "French fried potatoes" names the technique, so the word describes how the potato was cooked rather than which country the dish came from. Page 118 of the [1902 Household Cook Book](https://archive.org/details/householdcookboo00smil/page/118/) makes the distinction outright:

> "There are 2 ways of frying known to cooks as (1) wet frying, sometimes called French frying or frying in a kettle of hot fat; and (2) dry frying or cooking in a frying-pan."

The [same page](https://archive.org/details/householdcookboo00smil/page/118/) calls immersion "the first, or French method," and admits that, for all its better results, it "is little used in this country." That is the claim the adjective was making: deep fat was the French way to fry. [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/french%20fries) dates the full phrase to **1856** and the clipped "French fries" to **1903** in American English.

## The print record runs on four dates

| Year | What is in print |
|---|---|
| 1856 | "French fried potatoes" is first recorded in print, in Eliza Warren's Cookery for Maids of All Work |
| 1899 | Good Housekeeping credits "the perfection of French fries" to plenty of fat and full immersion |
| 1902 | The Household Cook Book defines "French frying" as wet frying in a kettle of hot fat |
| 1910 | "French fried potatoes" runs through the official bills of fare of the US Army |

Warren was a British writer and a rival of Mrs Beeton, and the etymologist [Michael Quinion](https://worldwidewords.org/qa-fre2.html) names her the first recorded user of the phrase, in a recipe that reads like a method note and nothing more: "Cut new potatoes in thin slices, put them in boiling fat, and a little salt; fry both sides of a light golden brown colour; drain." The instruction covers fat and slicing and nothing else. Slices fried on both sides sound closer to a crisp than to a modern fry, and the spelling of colour marks a British kitchen.

The two words were in print together before Etymonline's date. An October 1899 [Good Housekeeping](https://archive.org/details/goodhousekeepin04unkngoog) article on French kitchens says "The perfection of French fries is due chiefly to the fact" that cooks there use plenty of fat, "and that the thing fried is wholly immersed." In that sentence French fries means anything fried the French way, the method rather than the potato. For the potato itself, [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/french%20fry) dates the noun french fry, a strip of potato fried in deep fat, to **1886**, so the dictionaries do not agree on when the short name arrived.

## The soldiers in Belgium story is off by seven years

The popular version holds that American troops in Belgium during World War I heard French spoken around them, took the local fried potatoes for a French dish, and named them accordingly. [BBC Travel](https://www.bbc.com/travel/article/20180730-can-belgium-claim-ownership-of-the-french-fry) tells it as the sequel to a Belgian legend, with soldiers who "allegedly dubbed the potatoes" French fries.

The paper trail runs the other way. The **1910** Manual for Army Cooks, issued as War Department Document No. 379 and [digitised in full at the Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/manualforarmyco01statgoog), lists "French fried potatoes" again and again through its month of bills of fare: with Hamburg steak at breakfast, with a ham omelet, with fried fish at dinner.

Its [recipe No. 357](https://archive.org/details/manualforarmyco01statgoog), Potatoes, French fried, for 60 men, cuts 22 pounds of potatoes lengthwise into half-inch slices to "fry in deep lard until nicely browned," and calls the result "a dish much relished by the men." The Army was frying them by name, sixty men at a time, years before anyone shipped out.

Woodrow Wilson did not go before Congress to request a declaration of war until **2 April 1917**, per the [US State Department's own history office](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1914-1920/wwi), and the [first AEF infantry unit to set foot in France](https://abmc.gov/news-events/news/remembering-world-war-i-american-troop-ships-first-arrive-france) came ashore at Saint-Nazaire on 26 June 1917. The Army had the phrase printed in its official menus seven years before the United States entered the war that is supposed to have coined it.

The same manual brackets the arrival of the term. The [1896 edition](https://archive.org/details/manualforarmycoo00unit) has no French fried anything: its potato section runs boiled, baked, steamed, fried cooked and fried raw. Its fried raw potatoes are sliced lengthwise and dropped into enough boiling lard to float them, so the method was already there and only the name was missing. The phrase entered the Army's manual somewhere between those two printings, and it did not come off a troopship.

## The julienne theory has no dictionary behind it

The runner-up explanation says "French" describes the cut, from a verb "to french" meaning to slice into strips. [Etymonline's entry for French](https://www.etymonline.com/word/French) carries no culinary verb sense at all; the only verb sense it records for lowercase "french" is an unrelated slang term dated to about **1917**.

[Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/french) does list the strip sense, "to cut (green beans) in thin lengthwise strips before cooking," but its earliest date for the verb is about **1895**, and that date belongs to a butcher's sense, trimming the meat from the end of a chop bone. Quinion reaches the same verdict from the other side: "the earliest evidence for frenched is much later than that for French fried potatoes," he writes on [World Wide Words](https://worldwidewords.org/qa-fre2.html).

A verb first recorded in the 1890s cannot account for a phrase that was already in print in 1856. This one is assembled backwards: the fries are cut into strips, a verb exists, so the verb gets appointed as the source.

## Jefferson's kitchen had the dish, not the name

Monticello's research librarian at the time, Anna Berkes, told [Quinion](https://worldwidewords.org/qa-fre2.html) of a recipe "probably dating from Jefferson's presidential period" that describes, in French, what Quinion translates as "Potatoes, raw, in small slices, deep-fried." That is the dish, on paper some fifty years before the English phrase.

The better-known version, that an 1802 recipe in Jefferson's own hand records a request to his French chef, Honore Julien, for "potatoes served in the French manner," is an often-repeated story with no known contemporary source: Quinion found that "Nobody associated with the Jefferson archives knows of the request," as he reports on [World Wide Words](https://worldwidewords.org/qa-fre2.html). A dinner at the President's house that a guest did write down that year, in congressman Manasseh Cutler's [diary entry for 6 February 1802](https://archive.org/details/lifejournalscorr02cutl/page/71/), runs to rice soup, turkey, mutton, ham, "fried eggs, fried beef, a pie called macaroni," and ice cream, and names no potatoes.

## Belgium's claim rests on a manuscript a historian could not find

The Belgian case goes back to Jo Gerard and a 1781 manuscript said to be by his ancestor Joseph Gerard, popularised by the magazine Pourquoi Pas? in 1985, according to the Liege historian [Pierre Leclercq](https://culture.uliege.be/jcms/c_13056/fr/la-veritable-histoire-de-la-pomme-de-terre-frite-1re-partie?part=2). As printed there, it says people in Namur, Andenne and Dinant cut potatoes into the shape of little fish and fried them when frost made fishing on the Meuse too risky, a habit more than a hundred years old, which would put it around 1680. Leclercq adds in a footnote that he was never able to find the original text, so he cannot vouch for the transcription.

He also notes that the potato did not reach the Namur region until around **1735** ([Leclercq, page 3](https://culture.uliege.be/jcms/c_13056/fr/la-veritable-histoire-de-la-pomme-de-terre-frite-1re-partie?part=3)), decades after the date the story needs. He doubts the frying too: in the 18th century, he wrote, as quoted by [BBC Travel](https://www.bbc.com/travel/article/20180730-can-belgium-claim-ownership-of-the-french-fry), "fat was a luxury for people of limited means," so peasants were unlikely to have deep-fried anything.

Who first lowered a raw potato into deep fat is unsettled, and Belgium and France both claim the honour; Leclercq himself wrote, as [BBC Travel](https://www.bbc.com/travel/article/20180730-can-belgium-claim-ownership-of-the-french-fry) reports, that "The inventor of the fried potato will probably always remain anonymous." Where the English words came from is a different question, and that one has a paper trail ending in a cookbook instruction.

Nobody chose to honour a country. A cook needed one word for the deep kettle and another for the pan, French frying was the word for the kettle, and potatoes were the food it stuck to hardest. The war story turned up afterwards, fitted the gap beautifully, and has been passed along for decades by people who never opened the 1910 mess manual. Strictly FYI.
