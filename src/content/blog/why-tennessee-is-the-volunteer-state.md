In **September 1813**, Governor Willie Blount of Tennessee put out a call for **3,500 volunteers**. An attack at Fort Mims, near Mobile in what is now Alabama, on **August 30** had outraged the populace, and Blount needed men in the field. Thomas Kanon, writing in the [Tennessee Encyclopedia](https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/war-of-1812/), records that the response to that call "initiated a tradition that gave the state its nickname."

That call is where most versions of the story start. The words "Volunteer State" were not, by the [state archives' own account](https://tslablog.blogspot.com/2015/05/memorial-day-and-volunteer-state.html), in use during the war they are credited to.

## Why is Tennessee called the Volunteer State?

Tennessee is called the Volunteer State for the turnout of its volunteer soldiers. State sources credit the War of 1812, when Governor Willie Blount [called for 3,500 men in September 1813](https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/war-of-1812/). The label was settled in 1846, when [Tennessee was asked for 2,800 volunteers](https://digitaltennessee.tnsos.gov/exhibit/the-volunteer-state-goes-to-war-a-salute-to-tennessee-veterans/mexican-american-war/) for the Mexican-American War and roughly 30,000 offered to serve, a response the state archives say "cinched the nickname."

That is the version the standard state-symbol references carry, and the second half of it holds up on paper. The first half is where the state's own sources part company.

## The archives say the phrase came later

The Tennessee State Library and Archives, the body that keeps the state's records, addressed the dating directly in a [Memorial Day post in 2015](https://tslablog.blogspot.com/2015/05/memorial-day-and-volunteer-state.html).

> Most Tennessee historians now agree that the nickname "Volunteer State" is derived from the War of 1812, although the phrase was never actually used during that conflict. - Tennessee State Library and Archives

What Tennesseans did use during that war, the [same post explains](https://tslablog.blogspot.com/2015/05/memorial-day-and-volunteer-state.html), was the word "volunteer" inside unit names, as in Tennessee Volunteers and Mounted Volunteers. The archives place Tennessee under the name ["at least a decade before the Mexican-American conflict,"](https://tslablog.blogspot.com/2015/05/memorial-day-and-volunteer-state.html) which puts the phrase in circulation by 1836 at the latest, two decades after New Orleans. By the archives' account, then, the reputation and the phrase have different birthdays.

Not every state source agrees. Ann Toplovich's [Tennessee Encyclopedia entry on the nickname](https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/volunteer-state/) says Tennessee became known as the Volunteer State during the War of 1812, though the wartime newspaper phrases it cites are "the Tennessee Volunteers" and "the volunteers from Tennessee."

The [Tennessee Blue Book](https://publications.tnsosfiles.com/pub/blue_book/25-26/25-26_TennesseeState.pdf) gives three answers by itself: its page on state nicknames says the name "originated during the War of 1812." Its [history chapter](https://publications.tnsosfiles.com/pub/blue_book/25-26/25-26_History.pdf) says Tennessee earned it "for its role in America's wars of expansion," listing the War of 1812, the Texas Revolution, the Seminole Wars and the Mexican War, while a picture caption in the same chapter credits "Heavy enlistment for the Mexican War."

By 1847 the name was a headline: Toplovich's [entry](https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/volunteer-state/) quotes the Nashville Daily Union of **May 17, 1847**, under the headline "The Volunteer State": "The proud name which Tennessee earned in the last war is being gloriously sustained in this." The paper treated the name as something won in one war and renewed in the next.

The battle most often attached to the nickname carries a footnote of its own. [State Symbols USA](https://statesymbolsusa.org/symbol-official-item/tennessee/state-nickname/volunteer-state), which names the Tennessee Blue Book as its source, credits Tennessee's volunteers "especially during the Battle of New Orleans." [Kanon's account](https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/war-of-1812/) dates that battle to **January 8, 1815**, fifteen days after the peace treaty was signed on **December 24, 1814**, and answers the obvious objection himself: the treaty was not ratified until **February 1815**. Its [first article](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/treaty-of-ghent) stopped hostilities only once both sides had ratified it.

## Thirty thousand men answered a call for 2,800

The 1846 half of the story is documented and, on its own terms, extraordinary. Timothy Johnson's [encyclopedia entry on the Mexican War](https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/mexican-war/) records that **Secretary of War William Marcy requested 2,800 recruits** from Tennessee, enough to fill two regiments of infantry and one of cavalry, and that **30,000 Tennesseans offered their services**.

That is more than ten to one, and it left the state with a problem nobody plans for: choosing who was allowed to go to war.

After state officials ran **a lottery** to sort the surplus, [approximately 1,000 men from Middle Tennessee counties](https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/mexican-war/) formed the First Tennessee Infantry, volunteers from the western part of the state made up the Second under Colonel William T. Haskell, and a contingent of dragoons came from East Tennessee.

## The official numbers do not agree with each other

Read six state and reference sources and you get the same requisition at two different sizes, credited to three different men and two offices.

| Source | 1846 request | Credited to | Responded |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Tennessee Encyclopedia, "Mexican War"](https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/mexican-war/) | 2,800 | Secretary of War William Marcy | 30,000 |
| [Tennessee Encyclopedia, "Volunteer State"](https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/volunteer-state/) | 2,800 | President James K. Polk | 30,000 |
| [Tennessee State Library and Archives exhibit](https://digitaltennessee.tnsos.gov/exhibit/the-volunteer-state-goes-to-war-a-salute-to-tennessee-veterans/mexican-american-war/) | 2,800 | the Secretary of War | 30,000 |
| [Tennessee Blue Book, "Past Governors"](https://publications.tnsosfiles.com/pub/blue_book/25-26/25-26_TennesseeState.pdf) | 2,600 | Governor Aaron V. Brown | 30,000 |
| [Tennessee National Guard news story](https://www.tn.gov/military/news/2021/11/2/tennessee-national-guard-honors-president-polk.html) | 2,600 | President James K. Polk | 30,000 |
| [State Symbols USA](https://statesymbolsusa.org/symbol-official-item/tennessee/state-nickname/volunteer-state) | 2,800 | the secretary of state | 30,000 |

The two encyclopedia entries share a publisher, the Tennessee Historical Society, and still name different men. Marcy was [Polk's Secretary of War](https://millercenter.org/president/polk/essays/marcy-1845-secretary-of-war) and Brown was the governor whose call the [Blue Book records](https://publications.tnsosfiles.com/pub/blue_book/25-26/25-26_TennesseeState.pdf), so the three names can describe one requisition passing from the president through the War Department to the state. State Symbols USA's "secretary of state" reads like a slip for secretary of war, the office the Mexican War entry and the archives exhibit name.

The 1812 figures scatter further. Blount's call was for 3,500 in [Kanon's account](https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/war-of-1812/) and 2,500 in the [Blue Book's history chapter](https://publications.tnsosfiles.com/pub/blue_book/25-26/25-26_History.pdf). The archives exhibit's [introduction](https://digitaltennessee.tnsos.gov/exhibit/the-volunteer-state-goes-to-war-a-salute-to-tennessee-veterans/) says Tennessee earned the nickname "by sending 2,000 willing soldiers" to that war, while the exhibit's own [War of 1812 page](https://digitaltennessee.tnsos.gov/exhibit/the-volunteer-state-goes-to-war-a-salute-to-tennessee-veterans/the-war-of-1812-and-indian-wars/) judges that "up to 20,000 men served at one time or another during the conflict." Toplovich writes that [some historians estimate that almost 28,000 Tennesseans served](https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/volunteer-state/) by the end of the war in early 1815.

## The Bloody First went over the wall at Monterrey

The men who did go lived up to the name. At the Battle of Monterrey in September 1846, the First Tennessee Infantry under Colonel William Bowen Campbell [stormed Fort Teneria](https://digitaltennessee.tnsos.gov/exhibit/the-volunteer-state-goes-to-war-a-salute-to-tennessee-veterans/mexican-american-war/) and were, in the archives' phrase, **first over the walls and into the fort**. The regiment won the nickname the **Bloody First**.

The First and Second Tennessee had signed on for [twelve-month enlistments](https://tennesseeencyclopedia.net/entries/mexican-war/), and when the terms ran out in the summer of 1847 both regiments, like most volunteers, chose to go home before the fall of Mexico City.

The archives exhibit records that the [Third, Fourth and Fifth Infantries](https://digitaltennessee.tnsos.gov/exhibit/the-volunteer-state-goes-to-war-a-salute-to-tennessee-veterans/mexican-american-war/) were recruited after the major fighting ended and acted mainly as garrison troops and supply line guards, though the Third saw some action in the last days of the war.

## Two true stories, three decades apart

Tennesseans turned out for both wars, and in the second they overshot the quota roughly tenfold, a record documented down to the fort walls at Monterrey. The legislature made the nickname official only in [2020, by Public Chapter 516](https://publications.tnsosfiles.com/pub/blue_book/25-26/25-26_TennesseeState.pdf), more than two centuries after Blount's call.

A state nickname is a compressed argument about the past, and this one folds at least two wars into a single name. Strictly FYI.
