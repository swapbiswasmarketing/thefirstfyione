On 7 October 1959, a Soviet probe called Luna 3 came around the back of the Moon carrying [a photocell whose job was to notice the sunlit Moon](https://web.archive.org/web/20241215002220/https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/nmc/spacecraft/display.action?id=1959-008A). The sunlight was there, which is how the camera knew to start. The spacecraft [took 29 photographs in about 40 minutes, covering 70% of the far side](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/imgcat/html/object_page/lu3_1.html), most of it ground no one had ever seen.

That hemisphere is still routinely called the dark side, and it was in broad daylight the whole time. The nickname [mixes up the far side, turned away from Earth, with the night side, turned away from the Sun](https://web.archive.org/web/20130703162844/http://www.nasa.gov/worldbook/moon_worldbook.html), and it travels with a second mistake: the idea that the Moon does not spin at all.

## Why do we only see one side of the Moon?

Because the Moon rotates once on its axis in exactly the time it takes to orbit Earth. Astronomers call this tidal locking: the spin and the orbit run on the same clock, so the same hemisphere stays pointed at Earth. A Moon that had stopped rotating would show Earth every side of itself once a month.

NASA's own phrasing leaves no wiggle room: it ["takes precisely as long for the Moon to spin on its axis as it does to complete its monthly orbit around Earth"](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/tidal-locking/). That shared period is [27.3 days for one revolution, against 29.5 days from new moon to new moon](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/top-moon-questions/), because Earth and Moon travel around the Sun together and sunlight needs a little over two extra days to strike the Moon at the same angle again.

## A Moon that stopped spinning would show us everything

Walk a full circle around a lamppost while keeping your face pointed at one distant building. The lamppost gets your front, then your shoulder, then your back, then your other shoulder. Now walk the same circle keeping your face on the lamppost the entire way: you have to turn your body through one complete revolution to do it, and that turn is a rotation.

The Moon is doing the second version. NASA's answer to whether the Moon rotates states the consequence outright: ["If the Moon did not rotate on its axis at all, or if it rotated at any other rate, then we would see different parts of the Moon throughout the month."](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/top-moon-questions/) The unchanging face is what a rotating Moon looks like from the body it is locked to.

## Earth's gravity dragged a bulge, and the bulge did the braking

The Moon did not begin synchronised. [NASA's tidal-locking explainer](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/tidal-locking/) describes the newly formed body as a "hot, molten object that coalesced from the ejected material" that "would have been spinning wildly, with its shape changing as it was pulled at by Earth's gravity," and notes that Earth's pull still distorts the Moon into "a slight football shape" today.

That pull raised a bulge in soft rock. Because the young Moon turned faster than it orbited, the bulge kept being carried past the Earth-facing line while Earth's gravity kept hauling it back, and the rock flexed on every cycle. In [NASA's words](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/tidal-locking/), "As the Moon bent and fluxed in this tug of war, energy was released in the form of heat," and the spin slowed "until a single spin on its axis took the same amount of time as one trip around Earth," a state the bigger moons reach "within hundreds of thousands of orbits," which is quick by planetary standards.

Once the bulge stopped shifting relative to Earth, NASA explains, no more energy needed to be dissipated by this process, and the spin rate stopped changing.

## The dark side is not dark

Away from a few polar craters and peaks, every point on the lunar surface runs a day-night cycle of [about 29 and a half Earth days from one sunrise to the next](https://web.archive.org/web/20130703162844/http://www.nasa.gov/worldbook/moon_worldbook.html), and the far side is lit for half of it. NASA's Moon questions page is direct about this: ["The far side of the Moon gets as much sunlight as the near side."](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/top-moon-questions/)

As a name for the far side, dark meant unknown, not unlit. Penn State's account of a 2014 far-side study puts it plainly: the far side ["was called the dark side because it was unknown, not because sunlight does not reach it,"](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/55-year-old-dark-side-moon-mystery-solved) and it stopped being unknown in 1959.

> At new moon, [NASA's description of the phase](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/moon-phases/) is exact: the illuminated side of the Moon faces the Sun and the night side faces Earth. The far side is in full daylight at the precise moment the familiar one goes black.

## We have already seen 59% of it

The Moon does not hold perfectly steady even from Earth's point of view. Its orbit is elliptical, so its steady rotation sometimes runs ahead of its orbital position and sometimes lags behind. Its rotation axis is [tilted 6.68 degrees to its orbit](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/moonfact.html), so it also nods north and south.

That combined wobble is called libration, and from the center of the Earth [it can reach 7 degrees 54 minutes of arc east to west and 6 degrees 50 minutes north to south](https://epod.usra.edu/blog/2014/05/libration-of-the-moon.html). Over time it shows Earth [about 59 percent of the lunar surface](https://web.archive.org/web/20130703162844/http://www.nasa.gov/worldbook/moon_worldbook.html). About **18% of the far side** is [occasionally visible from Earth this way, and the remaining 82%](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far_side_of_the_Moon) went unobserved by anyone until Luna 3.

## The hidden hemisphere is a different world

The hemisphere turned away from us is not a mirror image of the one we know. Dark volcanic plains called maria [cover about 16 percent of the Moon's surface](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/pp1348), and the US Geological Survey's 1987 monograph The Geologic History of the Moon puts them on [about 30 percent of the near side but only about 2 percent of the far side](https://pubs.usgs.gov/pp/1348/report.pdf). Nearly all of the Moon's maria sit on the near side, where they draw the familiar face. They are old lava flows, and the National Air and Space Museum notes that they make the near side look darker, leaving the hidden hemisphere [brighter than the side we do see](https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/is-dark-side-moon-actually-dark).

| | Near side | Far side |
|---|---|---|
| Dark maria | About 30% of its surface | About 2% of its surface |
| Crust | About 40 km thick | About 60 km thick |
| Seen from Earth | Always faces Earth | Only its edges, at times; first photographed 7 October 1959 |
| Direct radio contact with Earth | Continuous | Blocked, needs a relay |

The crust is the obvious suspect. NASA puts it at [about 37 miles (60 km) thick on the far side but only 25 miles (40 km) on the near side](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/composition/), and says scientists are still trying to find out why it is so uneven.

One answer, from [Penn State astrophysicists in 2014](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/55-year-old-dark-side-moon-mystery-solved), starts with tidal locking itself. In their paper, the young Moon ["rapidly achieved synchronous rotation"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2020) beside "the hot, post-Giant-Impact Earth," so aluminium and calcium condensed out of the rock vapour on the cooler far side and built a thicker crust there. Later impacts on the near side, in their account, ["punched through the crust, releasing the vast lakes of basaltic lava"](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/55-year-old-dark-side-moon-mystery-solved) that became the maria, while on the far side the crust was mostly too thick to let any through.

[Ranger 4 crashed into the far side in April 1962](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/ranger-4/), but no spacecraft soft-landed there until China's Chang'e 4 [touched down in Von Karman crater on 3 January 2019](https://lroc.im-ldi.com/images/1087), [within the South Pole-Aitken basin](https://www.planetary.org/space-missions/change-4), which the Smithsonian's National Air and Space Museum calls [the largest confirmed impact crater on the Moon](https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/is-dark-side-moon-actually-dark). The mission depended on [the Queqiao relay satellite, launched in May 2018](https://www.planetary.org/space-missions/change-4), because the far side never faces Earth and the Moon itself blocks the signal.

## Earth is being slowed by the same process

The mechanism that locked the Moon is still running, with the roles reversed. The Moon raises tides in Earth's oceans, and in [James Williams and Dale Boggs's analysis of lunar laser ranging data](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10569-016-9702-3) that tidal friction slows Earth's rotation and pushes the Moon outward by **38.30 millimetres a year**, about an inch and a half. The ranging works by bouncing lasers off reflector arrays on the surface, including one [left on the Sea of Tranquility by Apollo 11 in July 1969](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/lunar/apollo11info.html).

Earth pays for the Moon's retreat in spin. The same study puts the tidal lengthening of the day at [2.395 milliseconds per century](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10569-016-9702-3), and the theoretical end state is [a mutual lock about 50 billion years from now](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/tidal-locking/), a date NASA hedges with "if the Moon and Earth could somehow avoid the eventual death of the Sun."

Luna 3's photocell went hunting for sunlight on the far side and found it at once, glaring off a hemisphere that had been turning once per orbit throughout the whole of human history without ever turning its face to Earth. The single face is the proof of the spin, and the machinery that finished the job on the Moon is still grinding away at Earth. Strictly FYI.
