On 7 October 1959, a Soviet probe called Luna 3 came around the back of the Moon carrying a photocell whose job was to notice sunlight. The sunlight was there, which is how the camera knew to start. In one photographic run the spacecraft exposed 29 frames covering about **70% of a hemisphere** no human being had ever seen.

That hemisphere is still routinely called the dark side, and it was in broad daylight the whole time. The nickname fails for the same reason the popular answer to the question fails: both of them assume the Moon is holding still.

## Why do we only see one side of the Moon?

Because the Moon rotates once on its axis in exactly the time it takes to orbit Earth. NASA calls this tidal locking: the spin and the orbit run on the same clock, roughly 27.3 days, so the same hemisphere stays pointed at Earth. A Moon that had stopped rotating would show Earth every side of itself once a month.

NASA's own phrasing leaves no wiggle room: it ["takes precisely as long for the Moon to spin on its axis as it does to complete its monthly orbit around Earth"](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/tidal-locking/). The period is the sidereal month, [about 27.3 days](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_of_the_Moon), against the 29.5 days of the familiar cycle of phases, which is timed from a different reference point. [Units of time have a long history of being odder than they sound](/blog/how-long-is-a-moment/).

## A Moon that stopped spinning would show us everything

Walk a full circle around a lamppost while keeping your face pointed at one distant building. The lamppost gets your front, then your shoulder, then your back, then your other shoulder. Now walk the same circle keeping your face on the lamppost the entire way: you have to turn your body through one complete revolution to do it, and that turn is a rotation.

The Moon is doing the second version. Wikipedia's account of tidal locking states the consequence outright: "[if the Moon did not rotate then the face would change over the course of an orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_locking)." The unchanging face is what a rotating Moon looks like from the body it is locked to.

## Earth's gravity dragged a bulge, and the bulge did the braking

The Moon did not begin synchronised. NASA describes the newly formed body as a "hot, molten object that coalesced from the ejected material" that "would have been spinning wildly, with its shape changing as it was pulled at by Earth's gravity."

That pull raised a bulge in soft rock. Because the young Moon turned faster than it orbited, the bulge kept being carried past the Earth-facing line while Earth's gravity kept hauling it back, and the rock flexed on every cycle. NASA: "As the Moon bent and fluxed in this tug of war, energy was released in the form of heat," and the spin slowed "until a single spin on its axis took the same amount of time as one trip around Earth."

The braking was quick by planetary standards. NASA puts synchronisation for the larger moons at "within hundreds of thousands of orbits." Earth's gravity still deforms the Moon into what NASA calls "a slight football shape" today.

## The dark side is not dark

Every point on the lunar surface runs a day-night cycle roughly 29.5 Earth days long, and the far side is lit for half of it. Wikipedia's far side article is direct about this: "Each location on the Moon experiences two weeks of sunlight while the opposite location experiences night," and "both the near and far sides receive, on average, almost equal amounts of light directly from the Sun."

The word dark in the phrase never meant unlit. It meant unknown. The same [Wikipedia article on the far side](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far_side_of_the_Moon) records that "dark" means "unseen" instead of "unilluminated," and the far side stopped being unseen on 7 October 1959. Names outlive their accuracy all the time, [as anyone who has looked closely at what birds actually leave on a windscreen will recognise](/blog/why-is-bird-poop-white/).

> At new moon, [NASA's description of the phase](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/moon-phases/) is exact: the illuminated side of the Moon faces the Sun and the night side faces Earth. The hemisphere no one on Earth can ever see is in full daylight at the precise moment the familiar one goes black.

The companion belief is that the Moon does not rotate at all. It does, once per orbit, which is the entire reason for the single face.

## We have already seen 59% of it

The Moon does not hold perfectly steady even from Earth's point of view. Its orbit is elliptical, so its steady rotation sometimes runs ahead of its orbital position and sometimes lags behind. Its rotation axis is [tilted about 6.7 degrees to its orbital plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libration), so it also nods north and south.

That combined wobble is called libration, and it is worth almost 8 degrees of extra view east to west and nearly 7 degrees north to south. About **18% of the far side** is occasionally visible from Earth as a result, which brings the running total to **59% of the lunar surface** over time. The remaining **82% of the far side** went unobserved by anyone until Luna 3.

## The hidden hemisphere is a different world

The 41% we never get is not a mirror image of the part we know. Dark volcanic plains called maria cover **31.2% of the near side** and only **about 1% of the far side**, which makes the hidden hemisphere brighter, rougher, and pocked with craters that were never flooded over.

| | Near side | Far side |
|---|---|---|
| Surface covered by dark maria | 31.2% | About 1% |
| Crust | Thinner | Thicker |
| Seen from Earth | Naked eye, always | Never; first photographed 7 October 1959 |
| Direct radio contact with Earth | Continuous | Blocked, needs a relay |

The standard explanation is crust thickness, and the same Wikipedia account traces it to the aluminium and calcium that combined with silicates to build the lunar crust. The cooler far side "experienced condensation of these elements sooner and so formed a thicker crust," while impacts on the near side "would sometimes penetrate the thinner crust here and release basaltic lava that created the maria." Which layer ends up on top decides how a whole world reads from a distance, and that is also [the whole argument about the red planet](/blog/is-mars-really-red/).

Nobody landed on that side until China's Chang'e 4 [touched down in Von Karman crater on 3 January 2019](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27e_4), inside the South Pole-Aitken basin, one of the largest known impact craters in the Solar System. It needed a relay satellite parked beyond the Moon to speak to Earth at all, because the Moon itself blocks the signal.

## Earth is being slowed by the same process

The mechanism that locked the Moon is still running, with the roles reversed. The Moon raises tides in Earth's oceans, tidal friction bleeds Earth's rotational momentum into the orbit, and the Earth-Moon distance grows by [about 38 millimetres a year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_of_the_Moon), which [NASA's Moon page rounds to about an inch](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/facts/). That number comes from bouncing lasers off reflector arrays on the surface, the first of them [left by the crew of Apollo 11 on 21 July 1969](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Laser_Ranging_experiment).

Earth pays for the Moon's retreat in spin. The day lengthens by [about 24 microseconds every year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_of_the_Moon), and the theoretical end state is [a mutual lock about 50 billion years from now](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_acceleration). The same page adds the catch: rising solar radiation will probably vaporise Earth's oceans within one to one and a half billion years, taking the bulk of the tidal friction with it long before the two clocks ever match.

Luna 3's photocell went hunting for sunlight on the far side and found it at once, glaring off a hemisphere that had been turning once per orbit throughout the whole of human history without ever letting Earth look at it. The single face is the proof of the spin, and the machinery that finished the job on the Moon is still grinding away at Earth. Strictly FYI.
