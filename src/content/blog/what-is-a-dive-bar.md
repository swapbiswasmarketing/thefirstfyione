Edward Crapsey's book [The Nether Side of New York](https://archive.org/details/nethersideofnewy00crap/page/159/mode/2up), published in **1872**, stops in the middle of a night walk through the slums to define a word. His guide, a detective, had just pointed at a row of nine dilapidated tenements and called them one of the worst dives in the city. In the New York detective parlance of 1872, Crapsey explains, every foul place is a dive, ["whether it be a cellar or garret, or neither."](https://archive.org/details/nethersideofnewy00crap/page/159/mode/2up)

Earlier that night the pair had climbed down eight steps into the den of a woman known as Big Sue on Grand Street, a room Crapsey judged [a cellar rather than a basement](https://archive.org/details/nethersideofnewy00crap/page/158/mode/2up), so far was it below the street, and "scant six feet from floor to ceiling." On the dictionary evidence the word was barely five years into print, and it already meant more than a hole in the ground.

## What is a dive bar?

A dive bar is ["an unpretentious and usually inexpensive bar that caters especially to regular clientele"](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/dive%20bar), in Merriam-Webster's definition. The bar sense of dive began as [American slang for an illicit drinking den or a brothel](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/przx2ry), recorded from **1867**; Green's says such places usually sat in a basement, cellar or other hidden spot. It was an insult long before it became praise.

## The written record opens with a moll and a wine hall

Green's Dictionary of Slang opens its list of examples with the National Police Gazette of New York, 12 January 1867, where a woman the paper calls a "moll" keeps a dive on Plumb Street. Ten years later the Chicago Street Gazette reported that ["There is a dive on North LaSalle Street, called a wine hall, kept by an old procuress."](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/przx2ry) Green's marks the sense as originally American, and by 1883 the Sydney Bulletin was describing a San Francisco newspaperman who published analyses of the liquor served in that city's dives and saloons.

That record sinks [the claim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dive_bar) that dive was first used in the American press in the 1880s. Green's has it in print more than a decade before that, and Crapsey's book was already glossing it as detective parlance.

## Why it is called a dive: lowness, by the dictionary's account

Jonathon Green's explanation is that the word implied lowness of two kinds, physical and social. Such places, his entry says, were [usually set in a basement, cellar or "other slightly clandestine place"](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/przx2ry) that customers could duck into without being noticed. None of the 19th-century quotations the entry prints for the drinking sense shows a customer diving anywhere. The cellar image itself is older: to dive for a dinner, Francis Grose's slang dictionary defined in 1788, was ["to go down into a cellar to dinner."](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/t4civ3q) None of these sources records the step from that cellar slang to the American dive.

Crapsey's book backs the physical half. Of a burglars' cellar at the corner of the Bowery and Hester Street, he wrote that it was a true dive because it lay many steps below the street, and that ["nobody but an idiot would expect to enter it and not get robbed."](https://archive.org/details/nethersideofnewy00crap/page/160/mode/2up)

The same book shows the limits of the basement story. Crapsey treats depth as the mark of a real dive, yet his own definition takes in garrets. The cellar origin is plausible, and the link is still an inference.

## Big Sue's dive made it into a 1928 history, and "dive bar" came later

Herbert Asbury's The Gangs of New York, copyrighted in 1927 and [published by Knopf in March 1928](https://archive.org/details/gangsofnewyorkin0000unse/page/n11/mode/2up), places [the famous dive kept by a woman "known variously as Big Sue and the Turtle"](https://archive.org/details/gangsofnewyorkin0000unse/page/46/mode/2up) in the Arch Block, between Broome and Grand streets, and credits a contemporary journalist with describing her as a huge turtle standing on its hind legs. Crapsey had written that he [could not help imagining her that way](https://archive.org/details/nethersideofnewy00crap/page/158/mode/2up).

The two-word phrase is younger than the slang. Dictionary.com dates the [first record of dive bar to **1940-45**](https://web.archive.org/web/20261002110027/https://www.dictionary.com/browse/dive-bar), and Merriam-Webster gives its [first known use as 1974](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/dive%20bar), while Merriam-Webster's entry for plain dive reads ["a shabby, cheap, or disreputable establishment (such as a bar, restaurant, or nightclub)"](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/dive).

## How the insult became a recommendation

Green's dates the word's heyday to US Prohibition (1920-33) and is unimpressed by what followed: the compound dive bar, its entry says, ["has persisted, lending an air of spurious romance to otherwise unexceptional drinking places."](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/przx2ry)

The romance found a market. In **2003** Ig Publishing put out [New York City's Best Dive Bars: Drinking & Diving in the Five Boroughs](https://archive.org/details/newyorkcitysbest00mitc), and in 2009 the same house added [Seattle's Best Dive Bars](https://archive.org/details/seattlesbestdive0000seel). The phrase still fights its own history, and one Eater Boston writer opened his attempt at a definition with that problem:

> "Best Dive Bar? Something about those three words never looks quite right. Like Best Prison." - [Eater Boston, 2014](https://boston.eater.com/2014/10/23/7046703/what-we-talk-about-when-we-talk-about-dive-bars)

## One writer's test: effort, not decor

Checklists fail, argued Knoefel Longest of Eater Boston, because there are dives with and without music, dives with and without food, and old falling-down dives as well as brand new ones, so his single test is effort: a true dive is not making much of one ["beyond the minimum required to service its guests with drinks."](https://boston.eater.com/2014/10/23/7046703/what-we-talk-about-when-we-talk-about-dive-bars)

Under that test, a dive does not have to be falling apart, or even old. Newer dives, he wrote, are built with the least amount of the cheapest materials needed for seated drinking and stay that way until they go out of business or become old dive bars. Whatever happens to be broken in a dive will ["look to have been broken for some time."](https://boston.eater.com/2014/10/23/7046703/what-we-talk-about-when-we-talk-about-dive-bars)

The word has kept the lowness and lost the steps. A dive was once a place a detective warned you about, and now it is a place a guidebook sends you to. Strictly FYI.
