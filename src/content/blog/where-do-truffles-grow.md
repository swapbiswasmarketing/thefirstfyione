Brown hyenas in the southern Kalahari kept catching a smell on the breeze in **April 1975**. **Twenty-one times** they turned upwind, sometimes covering as much as **200 meters**, then dug in the sand for a few seconds and came up with a desert truffle, *Kalaharituber pfeilii*. The finds are recorded in M.G.L. Mills's study of brown hyena foraging.

Brown hyenas, a separate species from the spotted hyenas known for [their giggle](/blog/why-do-hyenas-laugh/), live mainly by scavenging.

A truffle depends on animals like them. It is the spore-bearing fruiting body of a fungus, formed underground, and it needs an animal to smell it, dig it up and eat it, then pass the spores in droppings somewhere new, as a [2022 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9402283/) of fungus-eating mammals explains.

## Where do truffles grow?

Most truffles grow underground on the roots of living trees, a partnership biologists call ectomycorrhizal; desert truffles can pair with rock-roses instead. The black truffle grows with oaks and hazels in France, Italy and Spain, the white mostly in Italy and the Balkans. Both favor limy soil, and black truffles are now farmed in both hemispheres.

The black, or Perigord, truffle, *Tuber melanosporum*, "grows in symbiosis with several oak species and hazelnut trees in France, Italy, and the Iberian Peninsula," says a [2013 study in PLoS One](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3640031/), and has been introduced successfully to **New Zealand, Australia, Israel and North America**. A [2020 paper in Scientific Reports](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7719165/) on truffle farming in central Europe gives the reason for the lime: "truffle growth and maturation require high pH levels," and high-calcium limestone is among the most suitable bedrock.

The white truffle, *Tuber magnatum*, comes from habitats "scattered through the Italian and Balkan peninsulas," according to a [2014 PLoS One study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4275250/). At three Tuscan sites sampled for a [2022 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9149314/), it grew under Turkey oak and hornbeam at one and under poplar and willow at the other two, in calcareous soil with a pH of **6.8 to 8**. The same study puts white truffles **10 to 30 centimeters** down, sometimes as deep as 80.

Desert truffles, mostly of the genera *Terfezia* and *Tirmania*, "grow in arid and semiarid areas," a [2020 review in Molecules](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7765491/) notes; the Kalahari hyenas' *Kalaharituber pfeilii* is one of them. Some pair with plants other than trees: growers in Spain, Israel and Abu Dhabi cultivate *Terfezia claveryi* with *Helianthemum*, the rock-roses, as its host plant.

## A truffle has to be eaten to spread its spores

Many truffle-like fungi "have lost their ability for the forcible discharge of spores," the 2022 review says, and so "rely on the production of volatile olfactory cues to attract animal dispersers."

Truffles and truffle-like fungi have evolved this way **more than 100 times**, in separate fungal lineages, the review says. The reasons are debated, though the authors suggest the trait was likely driven in part by the advantages of being eaten and spread by mammals. They compare the arrangement to plants attracting pollinators with [nectar](/blog/how-do-bees-make-honey/) and seed dispersers with sugary fruits.

The smell may also be timed to the spores. Three of the authors have watched trained truffle dogs at work:

> "On multiple occasions, we have seen highly trained truffle dogs step on partially emergent immature truffles, totally unaware of their presence, while signalling their handlers toward a ripe truffle nearby." - Elliott, Trappe and Vernes, *Fungal Systematics and Evolution*, 2022

They suggest the smell helps keep a truffle from being found "before spores are mature."

## Black truffles clear the ground around their tree

Black truffles leave a sign above ground. The fungus is associated with a patch around its host tree that "looks burned," the 2013 PLoS One study says, and that goes by the French word *brule*: "an area devoid of vegetation, usually circular," where the black truffles "usually are collected."

Why it forms is not settled. Chemicals given off by truffles "can be perceived by plants and potentially inhibit the growth of some," the study notes; one earlier study proposed that the fungus parasitizes the non-host herbaceous plants around it, and another that it outcompetes them for nutrients and water.

## The boar pheromone story is half right

The popular story says a truffle smells to a pig like a boar. It began as a real finding. In November 1981, Claus, Hoppen and Karg reported in *Experientia* that [black truffles contain androstenol](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01989905), a musk-smelling steroid that is "a major constituent of the pheromone of the boar" and "occurs also in axillary sweat of men."

They measured **40 to 60 nanograms per gram** of fresh truffle and wrote that this "offers an explanation for the ability of pigs to detect truffles growing as deep as 1 m under ground." In 1990, Talou and four colleagues tested the idea with domestic dogs and pigs and pointed to a different smell.

| | 1981 paper | 1990 paper |
|---|---|---|
| Authors | Claus, Hoppen and Karg | Talou, Gaset, Delmas, Kulifaj and Montant |
| Title | "The secret of truffles: A steroidal pheromone?" | "Dimethyl sulphide: the secret for black truffle hunting by animals?" |
| Proposed smell | Androstenol, a boar pheromone steroid | Dimethyl sulfide, a sulfur compound |
| Evidence | Measured in black truffles | Experiments with domestic dogs and pigs |

The 2020 Molecules review sums up the result as dogs and pigs being drawn by dimethyl sulfide rather than androstenol, and dimethyl sulfide is among the compounds "common to most white and black truffle species."

The steroid is in truffles, but the experiments found another smell doing the work, and the 2022 review still treats the pigs' motive as open: "it is hard to prove whether wild pigs are so passionately interested in truffles merely because they are tasty and nutritious or as a result of some sexual pheromonal trickery."

## Italy's truffle law asks for a dog and never mentions pigs

Italy wrote the dog into its national truffle law. Article 5 of [Law 752 of 16 December 1985](https://www.normattiva.it/uri-res/N2Ls?urn:nir:stato:legge:1985-12-16;752~art5) says the search, by whoever carries it out, must be made "con l'ausilio del cane," with the help of a trained dog, and that digging, with a small spade, must be limited to the spot where the dog began. Owners searching their own land are exempt.

The law never uses *maiale*, the Italian word for pig, but a rule that the search be made with a dog leaves no role for one. The same article bans general tilling of the ground during the harvest season, picking unripe truffles, leaving holes unfilled and searching from an hour after sunset to an hour before dawn unless a region's rules say otherwise. Hunters must pass an exam and be at least **14**.

The practical case for dogs comes down to appetite. Pigs "tend to eat the truffles," the 2020 Molecules review says, so dogs are preferred because they "have little appetite for mushrooms."

## Farmed truffles take years, and the white one mostly refuses

Black truffle orchards are "located both in the Northern and Southern Hemispheres," the 2014 PLoS One study says. [NC State Extension](https://newcropsorganics.ces.ncsu.edu/specialty-crops/black-truffle-project/) explains that the roots of tree seedlings are inoculated with the fungus before planting, and that growers in **North Carolina, Kentucky, Virginia and Tennessee** have produced truffles.

North Carolina's soils are naturally acidic, so they "must be heavily limed" to reach the pH of about **7.6 to 8.1** that truffles need to fruit, and the first truffles should appear between the **fourth and ninth year** after planting. Trained dogs find them in winter and early spring. Filberts are a common host there, and Eastern Filbert Blight "decimated many of the earliest truffle orchards."

The US has native truffles as well. The pecan truffle, *Tuber lyonii*, is "found in pecan orchards across the Southeastern United States," according to a [2021 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8155716/), though it is not yet widely cultivated.

The white truffle is the hard case. Attempts to cultivate it "have often failed," the 2014 study says, and the high prices of white truffles "reflect their low availability on the market"; in autumn 2012, a poor harvest year, it sold for **4,000 to 5,300 euros a kilogram**. Cultivation is starting to work, though: a French orchard planted with inoculated plants produced white truffles in 2019 and 2020, outside the species' natural range, a [2021 study in Mycorrhiza](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00572-020-01013-2) reported. Still, as with [caviar](/blog/why-is-caviar-so-expensive/), the price follows a supply that cannot be quickly increased.

Truffle smells evolved to get truffles eaten, by animals from wild pigs to Kalahari hyenas. A trained dog follows the same smell and hands the truffle over. Strictly FYI.
