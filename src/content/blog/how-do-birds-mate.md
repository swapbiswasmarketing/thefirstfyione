If you have spent time around city pigeons, you have probably watched a male puff up his chest and strut in circles around a female, [bowing and cooing](https://www.audubon.org/field-guide/bird/rock-pigeon). He is courting her without a penis.

In 2013 [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/how-chickens-lost-their-penises-ducks-kept-theirs) listed pigeons among the birds that have lost the organ completely, along with eagles, owls, gulls, hummingbirds and woodpeckers.

## How do birds mate?

Most birds mate with a "cloacal kiss." The male mounts the female and the two press their cloacas together, the single opening birds use for droppings, eggs and sperm, and sperm passes from him to her in a contact that "may last only a few seconds," [BBC Wildlife](https://www.discoverwildlife.com/animal-facts/birds/how-do-birds-mate-bird-sex) notes. Only about **3%** of bird species have a penis that can be inserted, or in the words of a [2013 paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23746636/) in *Current Biology*, "have retained a phallus capable of intromission."

Those are mainly ducks, geese and swans, plus ostriches, emus and other ratites, many tinamous and the cracids, the groups a [2021 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8316823/) in *Royal Society Open Science* names as using "a true intromittent organ." Chickens and most other land fowl keep a small penis that cannot be inserted, and most other birds have none.

The female takes an active part. When captive female house sparrows initiate mating, they adopt "a crouching position with their wings quivering and their posterior end held upright," and both birds bend their tails so the cloacas meet, a [2018 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5976671/) in *Scientific Reports* describes. In house sparrows, mating comes in bouts of repeated rapid mounts, and not every mount includes cloacal contact.

## The sperm still ends up inside the female

Mating through the cloaca still ends in internal fertilization. "All birds reproduce by internal fertilization," the *Current Biology* paper [states in its abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23746636/).

Inside a hen, sperm move up through the vagina, which opens into the cloaca. Only about **1%** of them enter the sperm storage tubules at the junction of the vagina and the uterus, a [2025 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12537510/) in *The Journal of Poultry Science* reports, and sperm can survive there for extended periods. That is why a hen keeps laying fertile eggs long after mating: [the reported duration](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12537510/) is **2 to 3 weeks** in chickens, **10 to 15 weeks** in turkeys and 1 to 2 weeks in Japanese quail.

Fertilization happens at the upper end of the oviduct, [in the infundibulum](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12537510/), where sperm meet the ovum before its white, membranes and shell are added further along. Laying itself does not depend on mating: grocery store eggs "are from hens raised without roosters," notes a [2020 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7582499/) in *Sensors*, and an unfertilized ovum still travels the oviduct and is covered in egg white like any other.

## Only a few groups near the base of the bird tree kept a penis

Biologists often call the bird organ a phallus. [BBC Wildlife's Stuart Blackman](https://www.discoverwildlife.com/animal-facts/birds/how-do-birds-mate-bird-sex) says the stricter word fits because it "evolved independently from the mammalian penis." That claim is disputed: adult tuataras have no intromittent phallus, yet their embryos start to form genitals, which "suggests a single evolutionary origin of amniote external genitalia," Martin Cohn's lab reported in [Biology Letters in 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26510679/). The bird species that have a phallus all belong to "basal lineages of extant birds," Patricia Brennan and colleagues wrote in [PLOS ONE in 2007](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0000418), meaning branches that split off near the base of the bird family tree.

| Group | What the males have | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Neoaves, the vast majority of species | No penis | Pigeons, owls, eagles, gulls, wrens, hummingbirds |
| Land fowl (galliforms) | A small penis that cannot be inserted | Chickens, quail, pheasants |
| Waterfowl (anseriforms) | A penis that can be inserted, spiralled counter-clockwise | Ducks, geese, swans |
| Large flightless birds (ratites) | A penis that can be inserted | Ostriches, emus |
| Tinamous | In many species a penis that can be inserted; one that cannot in the genus Crypturellus | Ground birds of Mexico, Central and South America |

Exceptions run through the groups, especially the land fowl. The cracids, a family from Central and South America, have penises that can be inserted, while the malleefowl, an Australian mound-builder distantly related to the chicken, [has lost its penis entirely](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0908-8857.2008.04610.x).

In ducks, the female's anatomy has evolved along with the male's. Across **16 waterfowl species**, [Brennan's team found](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0000418) that every male penis spiralled counter-clockwise, and that where males had long penises, females had longer, more elaborate vaginas, with dead-end sacs and coils that turn clockwise, the opposite way.

In a [2010 follow-up](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20031991/), high-speed video caught the Muscovy duck's 20 cm penis everting in an average of **0.36 seconds**. It everted less successfully into glass tubes that coiled clockwise or bent at 135 degrees, shapes that mimicked the female's, which the authors took as support for the idea that her anatomy helps exclude males during forced matings.

In 2001 Kevin McCracken's team described [in Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/35093160) an Argentine lake duck whose corkscrew-shaped penis measured **42.5 cm**, as long as the bird's body, according to [ABC Science](https://www.abc.net.au/science/articles/2001/09/14/366856.htm). The earlier estimate for the species had been 20 cm.

## A chicken embryo starts to grow a penis, then its cells die

In a study led by Ana Herrera, a graduate student working with Cohn at the University of Florida, Pekin duck and chicken embryos both began with a stub called the genital tubercle, but the chicken's stopped growing around **day 9**, while the duck's went on to become a coiled penis, Ed Yong reported for [*National Geographic*](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/how-chickens-lost-their-penises-ducks-kept-theirs).

The cause was cell death. A gene called **Bmp4** switches on at the tip of the tubercle and, in the words of the [university's announcement](https://ufhealth.org/news/2013/activation-cell-death-gene-triggered-loss-phallus-bird-evolution), "turns on a cell suicide program that causes the tubercle to regress." In ducks and emus, Bmp4 is not activated there.

The team [implanted tiny beads](https://ufhealth.org/news/2013/activation-cell-death-gene-triggered-loss-phallus-bird-evolution) carrying a Bmp-blocking protein into the tubercles of chick embryos, and where the beads sat, the cells survived and growth continued. Activating Bmp signaling in duck genitals did the reverse, inducing "a galliform-like pattern of apoptosis," or cell death, [the paper's abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23746636/) reports.

> "It was surprising to learn that outgrowth fails not due to absence of a critical growth factor, but due to presence of a cell death factor," Cohn told *National Geographic*.

What is left is small: Yong [described chickens' penises](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/how-chickens-lost-their-penises-ducks-kept-theirs) as "tiny nubs that are no good for penetrating anything," and a [2015 paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25011524/) from Herrera, Brennan and Cohn calls the chicken's a "non-intromittent penis."

## The gene result covers chickens, not every bird

The University of Florida [announced the paper](https://ufhealth.org/news/2013/activation-cell-death-gene-triggered-loss-phallus-bird-evolution) under the headline "Activation of cell death gene triggered loss of phallus in bird evolution," which reads as if one gene explained the whole class. The paper is narrower.

It compared land fowl with waterfowl, using emus and alligators as outgroups, and [its abstract concludes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23746636/) that Bmp4-driven cell death explains the reduction "in galliform birds," the order that includes chickens and quail. Yong [noted the limit](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/how-chickens-lost-their-penises-ducks-kept-theirs) at the time: Cohn's team had "only compared two groups," leaving out the Neoaves, which "includes the vast majority of bird species."

None of the Neoaves has a penis. A [2008 survey](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0908-8857.2008.04610.x) in the *Journal of Avian Biology* by Brennan and colleagues counted two reductions already known, the land fowl's switch to a penis that cannot be inserted and "a complete loss of phallic structures in the ancestor of Neoaves," and reported three more: penises that cannot be inserted in a genus of tinamous and in the Australian brush-turkey, and a complete loss in the malleefowl.

Why birds lost the organ was uncertain, [the 2013 Florida release](https://ufhealth.org/news/2013/activation-cell-death-gene-triggered-loss-phallus-bird-evolution) said. For land fowl, it set out two of the hypotheses Herrera described: a byproduct of change in the beak, feathers or limbs, which the same gene helps build, or female choice for males with smaller genitalia. "The females want to have more control over copulation, reproduction and mate choice," she said.

## Most matings take seconds, but not the aquatic warbler's

In most birds the contact is brief. The aquatic warbler, a songbird whose [major breeding populations are in Belarus, Ukraine and Poland](https://aquatic-warbler.cms.int/), takes far longer. In a [1995 study in Ibis](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1474-919X.1995.tb03223.x), Schulze-Hagen and colleagues timed mounts in hand-reared, captive birds at an average of **23.7 minutes**, with about six cloacal contacts in each copulation and the male staying on top of or directly behind the female in between.

[The authors suggested](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1474-919X.1995.tb03223.x) the long mount may be "contact mate guarding," which they called "a behaviour not previously recorded in birds."

The pigeon bowing on the pavement and the rooster in the farmyard mate the same way, though they got there by different routes: the pigeon's ancestors lost the penis outright, while the rooster's embryo starts to grow one until Bmp4 sets its cells dying. Strictly FYI.
