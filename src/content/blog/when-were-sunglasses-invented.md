Ask when sunglasses were invented and you expect a date for a dark lens. Hunters on the Bering Sea were cutting the sun's glare with a slit instead. The [Smithsonian's catalogue entry](https://collections.si.edu/search/detail/edanmdm:nmnhanthropology_8382085) for a pair of St. Lawrence Island Yupik snow goggles notes that goggles carved from walrus ivory "have been found in Old Bering Sea archaeological sites nearly 2000 years old."

The pair itself, which [reached the Smithsonian in 1913](https://collections.si.edu/search/detail/edanmdm:nmnhanthropology_8382085), lets light in through **nine narrow horizontal slits** and nowhere else. If sunglasses need dark glass, goggles like these do not qualify.

## When were sunglasses invented?

The oldest known eyewear against the sun's glare is Arctic [slit goggles](https://collections.si.edu/search/detail/edanmdm:nmnhanthropology_8382085), **nearly 2,000 years** old and without a lens. Ming China made dark spectacles of tinted quartz. American [newspapers advertised](https://www.college-optometrists.org/the-british-optical-association-museum/history-fashion-sunglasses) "sun glasses" by **1895**, Foster Grant was [selling plastic ones](https://newengland.com/yankee/magazine/shades-of-greatness-foster-grant-sunglasses/) by **1929**, and polarizing sunglasses date from a **1935** [contract with American Optical](https://www.library.hbs.edu/hc/polaroid/emergence-of-a-new-technology/invention-of-the-polarizer/).

Two famous candidates are weaker than they look. Nero's emerald was probably a mirror, and the smoky-quartz lenses of Chinese judges are unverified, vouched for by a historian who faulted his own source.

## Inuit and Yupik snow goggles cut the glare with a slit

Snow goggles were made from "a strip of bone, wood or other material, with a slit cut into it," in the words of [*Smithsonian* magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smithsonian-institution/snow-goggles-demonstrate-indigenous-ingenuity-180973738/), and pairs in the national collections are made of baleen, caribou bone, driftwood and walrus ivory. Their narrow slits "allow only a small amount of sunlight to reach the eyes but still allow the person wearing them to see," says the Inuvialuit Cultural Resource Centre's description, quoted in the [catalogue entry](https://collections.si.edu/search/detail/edanmdm:nmnhanthropology_8362006) for a wooden pair from Canada's Northwest Territories. On that pair "the indented area has been blackened to further reduce the glare of sunlight."

They were made against [snow blindness](https://collections.si.edu/search/detail/edanmdm:nmnhanthropology_8362006), caused by sunlight reflected from snow and ice. The nine-slit pair's entry [quotes Estelle Oozevaseuk](https://collections.si.edu/search/detail/edanmdm:nmnhanthropology_8382085), speaking in 2001: "When spring comes, the sun is very bright."

Pairs from archaeological sites carry dates of their own. A carved wooden pair at the National Museum of the American Indian, collected at an ancient village site at Point Agiak on Coronation Gulf, in Nunavut, is [catalogued](https://collections.si.edu/search/detail/edanmdm:NMAI_120710) as "probably **AD 1300-1600**" and attributed, probably, to the Thule tradition.

## Nero's emerald was probably a mirror

The Science Museum in London starts its list of [surprising moments in the history of sunglasses](https://blog.sciencemuseum.org.uk/9-surprising-moments-in-the-history-of-sunglasses/) with the emperor Nero. The source is Pliny the Elder, whose word for Nero's stone is smaragdus, a name that, his translators note, covered emeralds and other green stones.

"When the surface of the smaragdus is flat, it reflects the image of objects in the same manner as a mirror," Pliny writes, in [Bostock and Riley's 1855 translation](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.02.0137:book=37:chapter=16). "The Emperor Nero used to view the combats of the gladiators upon a smaragdus." The translators' note takes up the theory that a shortsighted Nero used the stone as a concave lens, and answers that the passage "will hardly support such a construction."

## Needham believed the Chinese judges story and faulted its source

The other early claim is that judges in medieval China wore smoky quartz so that no one could read their faces. The Science Museum's [list repeats it](https://blog.sciencemuseum.org.uk/9-surprising-moments-in-the-history-of-sunglasses/) with a hedge: "Some historical accounts describe them being worn by court judges to conceal their facial expressions during trials."

A scholarly version appears in Joseph Needham's [*Science and Civilisation in China*](https://archive.org/details/bwb_S0-ASX-482_26). In its **1962** volume on physics, Needham writes that Song-dynasty judges wore "dark glasses made of smoky quartz," not "for driving against the sun, but so as to disguise from litigants their reactions to the evidence."

Just before that, he [cites the Song writer Liu Chhi](https://archive.org/details/bwb_S0-ASX-482_26), who died in 1117, for judges using rock-crystal magnifying lenses on illegible documents. For the dark glasses, his footnote names no medieval text:

> "This piece of information, which I fully believe to be true, comes from a paper on fire-pearls and spectacles by Pi (1), which, though interesting, is full of serious and misleading mistakes." - Joseph Needham, *Science and Civilisation in China*, vol. 4, part 1

Needham's [bibliography](https://archive.org/details/bwb_S0-ASX-482_26) identifies the paper as H.T. Pi's "The History of Spectacles in China," in the *China Medical Journal* for **1928**. With no medieval text behind it, the judges story stays unverified.

What Needham does document is later. The earliest Chinese books that refer to spectacles were written in the Ming dynasty, he writes, and a mention in Chao Hsi-Ku's 13th-century *Tung Thien Chhing Lu* is missing from the best and oldest versions of the book, an addition by a Ming hand. Two kinds of tinted quartz, "tea-crystal" and "black crystal," [were used in the Ming](https://archive.org/details/bwb_S0-ASX-482_26) "for making dark spectacles, just as we use dark sunglasses today."

## Ayscough tinted his glass against the glare of the page

James Ayscough, a London optician at the Great Golden Spectacles in Ludgate Street, is the "mastermind behind tinted lenses" in the sunglasses history of the [*International Directory of Company Histories*](https://www.encyclopedia.com/books/politics-and-business-magazines/fostergrant-inc), which dates his green or blue lenses to 1752. His **1750** [pamphlet](https://archive.org/details/b30377584) shows that others got there first, and that the glare he meant came off the page.

The common white glass, by which he meant clear, "gives an offensive glaring Light, very painful and prejudicial to the Eyes; insomuch, that some advise green or blue Glass," he [wrote](https://archive.org/details/b30377584). His own glass was "a little ting'd with Blue," and so, he [promised](https://archive.org/details/b30377584), "it takes off the glaring Light from the Paper."

By **1913**, when William Crookes read his paper on [eye-preserving glass](https://archive.org/details/philtrans09510120) to the Royal Society, tinted spectacles came in "tints of yellow, green, blue, and neutral" to "suit the public demand." His own target was the glass furnace: working with the Society's Glass Workers' Cataract Committee, he found that cerium "is of value in cutting off the ultra-violet rays" while leaving the glass "very slightly coloured."

## Foster Grant's first sunglasses were sold as a toy

Foster Grant came out of the plastics trade. In 1919 Sam Foster, an Austrian immigrant, [left his job](https://newengland.com/yankee/magazine/shades-of-greatness-foster-grant-sunglasses/) at the Viscoloid comb factory in Leominster, Massachusetts, to start the business that became Foster Grant.

In **1929**, the [directory says](https://www.encyclopedia.com/books/politics-and-business-magazines/fostergrant-inc), Foster Grant began selling sunglasses at the Woolworth's on the Atlantic City Boardwalk, where they were displayed with other medical aids. The directory says these were "the first sunglasses sold" over the counter, and that buyers before then "had to have them made to order by an optometrist," but that goes too far. Advertisements in American newspapers began listing "sun glasses" as distinct items for sale in 1895, according to the [British Optical Association Museum](https://www.college-optometrists.org/the-british-optical-association-museum/history-fashion-sunglasses).

A history of the firm published in **1972**, Glenn D. Kittler's [*More Than Meets the Eye*](https://archive.org/details/morethanmeetseye0000glen), is less sure of the date. Nobody could remember exactly when the first sunglasses were made, Kittler [writes](https://archive.org/details/morethanmeetseye0000glen), though it "probably was between 1927 and 1929." Those first pairs sold for [ten cents](https://archive.org/details/morethanmeetseye0000glen) each and were "marketed as a kiddie item."

What Foster Grant pioneered, [*Yankee* magazine says](https://newengland.com/yankee/magazine/shades-of-greatness-foster-grant-sunglasses/), was sunglasses "as a fashion item," at a time when "the few American companies that were making sunglasses touted them solely as eye protection." A [business directory](https://www.encyclopedia.com/books/politics-and-business-magazines/fostergrant-inc) agrees that "the glamour we now associate with sunglasses came from" the pairs Foster Grant began selling in Atlantic City. The optical museum [dates that turn](https://www.college-optometrists.org/the-british-optical-association-museum/history-fashion-sunglasses) to the 1930s, when "Hollywood celebrities helped popularise sunglasses, making them fashionable beyond their functional purpose."

The early pairs were made by hand, with frames [jigsawed from sheets of plastic](https://newengland.com/yankee/magazine/shades-of-greatness-foster-grant-sunglasses/), until Foster Grant adopted injection molding in the 1930s. In 1937, sunglasses sold "an unprecedented 20 million pairs," the Science History Institute's [*Distillations* magazine](https://www.sciencehistory.org/distillations/magazine/plastic-town) reports.

## Polarized lenses began with a 1929 patent application

In 1929, Edwin Land applied for his first patent for a light-polarizing sheet, according to the [Baker Library at Harvard Business School](https://www.library.hbs.edu/hc/polaroid/emergence-of-a-new-technology/invention-of-the-polarizer/). Eastman Kodak contracted for polarizing camera filters in 1934, and the following year Land's firm, Land-Wheelwright Laboratories, signed with the American Optical Company to produce sunglasses, marketed as Polaroid Day Glasses.

Land wanted his filters on cars. Polarizers against headlight glare "had occupied Land's thinking since the 1920s," the library's [history of the company](https://www.library.hbs.edu/hc/polaroid/a-research-and-manufacturing-company/commercialization-of-the-polarizer/) says, but the plan needed every carmaker to fit new headlights and windshield glass, and it never launched. In 1937 the firm was reincorporated as the Polaroid Corporation, selling Day Glasses and camera filters instead of headlights.

## The history of sunglasses has six starting points

Each definition crowns a different first:

| If sunglasses means | Earliest date | On the evidence of |
|---|---|---|
| Eyewear that cuts glare, lens or not | Nearly 2,000 years ago | Ivory goggles from Old Bering Sea sites |
| Dark lenses worn against light | Ming dynasty | Needham; the Song judges are unverified |
| Tinted glass to spare the eyes | By 1750 | Ayscough, who says others already advised it |
| Sun glasses sold as a store item | 1895 | American newspaper advertisements |
| Ten-cent plastic sunglasses | By 1929 | Foster Grant; its history says probably 1927 to 1929 |
| Polarizing sunglasses | 1935 | Land-Wheelwright's contract with American Optical |

Snow goggles had one more use. Elders have pointed out, according to [the Smithsonian's entry](https://collections.si.edu/search/detail/edanmdm:nmnhanthropology_8417902) for a Yup'ik pair, that narrow slits also "sharpen and focus vision, like a pinhole camera." Phillip Moses, [quoted in the same entry](https://collections.si.edu/search/detail/edanmdm:nmnhanthropology_8417902), said people "used them before sunglasses became available," and that "those who had bad eyes used them when they rowed to make their eyesight better." The slit goggles that open this story doubled as glasses. Strictly FYI.
