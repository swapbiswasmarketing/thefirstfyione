Venus is wrapped in cloud, the cloud works like a blanket, the blanket holds in the Sun's heat, and that is why the second planet from the Sun runs hotter than the first. Many popular explanations of Venus arrive at that sentence. The temperature at the end of it is right. The mechanism at the front of it is pointed the wrong way.

In a 2018 review of the Venus cloud system for Space Science Reviews, [Dmitrij Titov and four co-authors](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11214-018-0552-z) write that the sulfuric acid droplets making up those clouds "almost conservatively scatter the solar light in the range from UV to near-IR", so that "more than 75% of the incoming solar flux is returned back to space" and, on average, **only about 3 percent** of the sunlight arriving at the top of the atmosphere reaches the ground.

The review calls what follows a surprising fact, and it is: in [the authors' words](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11214-018-0552-z), "even though Venus is 30% closer to the sun, it receives less solar energy than the Earth". The cloud deck turns most of the light around before it gets anywhere near the surface.

## Why is Venus hotter than Mercury?

Venus is hotter than Mercury because it traps heat on the way out, even though its clouds [turn most sunlight away](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11214-018-0552-z) on the way in. [Mercury has almost no atmosphere](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/mercuryfact.html), so the sunlight its rock absorbs radiates straight back to space. Venus sits under [92 bars of an atmosphere that is 96.5% carbon dioxide](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/venusfact.html), which absorbs outgoing infrared and holds the surface at a mean of **464C** against Mercury's **167C** on [NASA's table of planetary temperatures](https://science.nasa.gov/resource/solar-system-temperatures/).

## Mercury gets the sunlight and loses it every night

NASA places Mercury [0.4 astronomical units](https://science.nasa.gov/mercury/facts/) from the Sun and [Venus at 0.72](https://science.nasa.gov/venus/venus-facts/), so Mercury takes by far the harder beating. Its daytime surface [reaches 430C](https://science.nasa.gov/mercury/facts/), and with nothing to hold that heat its night side falls to about **minus 180C**, a swing from 800F to minus 290F.

What passes for air on Mercury is a thin exosphere of atoms [blasted off the surface by the solar wind and striking meteoroids](https://science.nasa.gov/mercury/facts/), mostly oxygen, sodium, hydrogen, helium and potassium. [NASA's Mercury fact sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/mercuryfact.html) calls it "a surface bounded exosphere, essentially a vacuum". Sunlight lands, the ground warms, the ground radiates, and the energy leaves about as fast as it arrived.

## Mercury runs at its sunlight budget and Venus does not

Every planet has a black-body temperature, the surface temperature it [would have "if it were in radiative equilibrium and had no atmosphere"](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/fact_notes.html), keeping the same reflectivity. That number is the honest baseline for asking whether a world is running hot, and NASA's planetary fact sheets list one for each planet.

Mercury's fact sheet gives a [black-body temperature of 439.6 K and an average temperature of 440 K](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/mercuryfact.html). The planet sits almost exactly where sunlight alone puts it, hot by day and frozen by night, because there is nothing above the rock to change the sum.

By sunlight alone Venus should be colder than Earth: [NASA's Venus fact sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/venusfact.html) lists a black-body temperature of 226.6 K against Earth's 254.0 K, yet a surface averaging 737 K. Venus is closer in and receives [2601.3 watts of sunlight per square metre to Earth's 1361.0](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/venusfact.html), but its Bond albedo, [the fraction of incident sunlight reflected back into space](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/fact_notes.html) without being absorbed, is 0.77 against Earth's 0.294.

| Measure | Mercury | Venus |
|---|---|---|
| Sunlight arriving (watts per square metre) | 9082.7 | 2601.3 |
| Bond albedo | 0.068 | 0.77 |
| Black-body temperature | 439.6 K | 226.6 K |
| Average surface temperature | 440 K (167C) | 737 K (464C) |
| Surface pressure | under 0.005 picobar | 92 bars |

[Cedric Gillmann and colleagues](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11214-022-00924-0), in a 2022 Space Science Reviews paper on the long-term evolution of Venus's atmosphere, put the surface "approximately 500 K higher than its equilibrium temperature" and credit the gap to the greenhouse effect of carbon dioxide. Earth's own natural greenhouse effect, by [NASA's account of Earth's energy budget](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/climate-and-earths-energy-budget/), keeps the surface "more than 30 degrees warmer than it would be if it didn't have an atmosphere".

## The clouds work as a mirror before they work as a blanket

The blanket picture was not invented out of nothing. [Titov's review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11214-018-0552-z) also records that "strong absorption at longer wavelengths makes sulfuric acid clouds a powerful greenhouse agent", trapping thermal radiation beyond about 2.5 micrometres in the lower atmosphere. Both descriptions are true at once, at different wavelengths.

What the popular version reverses is the order. The clouds meet sunlight first and reject more than three quarters of it, and only afterwards do they help hold in the infrared the surface gives off. Calling them a blanket names the second job and hides the first.

> Venus is closer to the Sun than Earth and still absorbs less sunlight, because its clouds send more than three quarters of the light reaching it back to space. Everything after that is a question of how slowly the heat gets back out.

## Carbon dioxide blocks the exit

The sliver of sunlight that does reach the Venusian surface warms the rock, and the rock sheds that energy as infrared. On Earth, the oxygen and nitrogen that make up most of the air let that infrared through, but [NASA's Earth Observatory](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/climate-and-earths-energy-budget/) explains that water vapor, carbon dioxide and methane are "opaque to many wavelengths of thermal infrared energy".

On Venus, carbon dioxide is nearly the whole atmosphere, and [NASA's Venus page](https://science.nasa.gov/venus/venus-facts/) calls it "the same gas" that drives the greenhouse effect on Earth, and says the surface pressure squeezes like being **more than 3,000 feet underwater**.

A planet holds a steady temperature only when the energy it receives from sunlight [is balanced by an equal amount of energy radiating into space](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/climate-and-earths-energy-budget/), so the Venusian surface keeps warming until enough infrared finally gets out. NASA's Venus page puts the result at about 467C, hot enough to melt lead and a few degrees above the fact sheet's 464C mean, and says Venus has [the hottest surface in the solar system, apart from the Sun itself](https://science.nasa.gov/venus/venus-facts/). Of the [Soviet probes sent to Venus between 1961 and 1984](https://science.nasa.gov/venus/venus-facts/), ten reached the ground, and the longest survivor lasted two hours before the landers cooked.

## The ocean ending is still an open question

Many write-ups finish with Venus as a cautionary tale: an Earth-like world with oceans, cooked by a runaway greenhouse. A 2019 review led by [Stephen Kane](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.02783), accepted for publication in JGR Planets, calls the runaway greenhouse ["the accepted explanation for how Venus lost its water"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.02783). The oceans are the contested part, and [the same review](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.02783) states plainly that "It is unclear whether the oceans condensed, then later evaporated, or never condensed after accretion".

In **December 2024**, Tereza Constantinou, Oliver Shorttle and Paul Rimmer [published a Nature Astronomy paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-024-02414-5) backing the second option. From the rate at which Venus's atmosphere destroys water, carbon dioxide and carbonyl sulfide, which volcanism must keep resupplying, they infer that [Venusian volcanic gases](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-024-02414-5) carry "at most a 6% water mole fraction", substantially drier than terrestrial magmas degassed under similar conditions. [Their conclusion](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-024-02414-5) is that Venus "has never been liquid-water habitable".

That does not settle the argument. The paper's own [main text](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-024-02414-5) hedges, saying Venus "probably never experienced conditions conducive to ocean condensation", and its volcanic argument assumes the atmosphere is in a steady state. It does mean the tidy version, wet Venus with oceans lost to its own atmosphere, is a live hypothesis with a live competitor rather than a finding.

Mercury takes the full blast of the Sun and hands it back before morning. Venus turns most of a weaker beam away in its clouds, keeps a sliver, and then declines to let go of it. The planet that rejects more than three quarters of the sunlight reaching it holds the hottest surface of any planet, and both facts come from the same thick atmosphere. Strictly FYI.
