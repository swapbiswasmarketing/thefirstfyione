Spring tides do not wait for spring. The one under the North Sea flood of 1953 arrived on the night of 31 January, when a storm tide swept across the North Sea and flooded the low-lying coasts around it, killing **1,836** people in the Netherlands and **307** in the United Kingdom, according to a [2005 retrospective](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16191650/) by Herman Gerritsen of Delft Hydraulics for the Royal Society.

The peak high waters came during the night, and the storm "surprised many people in their sleep," [Gerritsen wrote](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16191650/). It also, [his paper notes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16191650/), "occurred in combination with spring tide."

Two nights earlier the Moon had been full: [NASA's table of moon phases](https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/phase/phases1901.html) lists a total lunar eclipse at the full moon of 29 January, 23:44 Universal Time, which means the Sun, Earth and Moon were almost exactly in line.

## What is a spring tide?

A spring tide is the larger-than-average tide that comes **twice a month**, around every new moon and full moon, when the Sun, Earth and Moon line up and the Sun's tidal pull [adds to the Moon's](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides06_variations.html). High tides run higher and low tides lower than average. It happens in every season, and the name has nothing to do with spring.

NOAA's National Ocean Service says spring tides ["occur twice each lunar month all year long, without regard to the season."](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/springtide.html) A lunar month, new moon to new moon, averages **29 days, 12 hours and 44 minutes** in [NASA's figures](https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/phase/phases1901.html), so one arrives roughly every two weeks.

## The Sun pulls harder but raises a tide half the Moon's

The Sun is about **27 million times** more massive than the Moon, according to NOAA's [tides tutorial](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides02_cause.html).

> "Based on its mass, the sun's gravitational attraction to the Earth is more than 177 times greater than that of the moon to the Earth," NOAA's tides tutorial says.

A tide comes from the difference in pull between the side of Earth nearest the Moon or Sun and the side farthest away. That difference shrinks with the cube of distance, while gravity itself shrinks only with the square, NOAA's [tutorial explains](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides02_cause.html).

The Sun is **390 times** farther away than the Moon, and 390 cubed is about 59 million, as the [same tutorial](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides02_cause.html) works out. Divide the Sun's 27-million-fold mass advantage by that and roughly half is left. The Sun's tide-generating force comes out at "about half that of the moon," [NOAA says](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides02_cause.html). The UK's [National Tidal and Sea Level Facility](https://ntslf.org/tides/about-tides/tides-faq) (NTSLF) puts it at **0.46** of the Moon's.

## Neap tides fall a week later, at the half moon

About seven days after a spring tide, the Moon reaches first or third quarter and sits at a right angle to the Sun, [NOAA explains](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/springtide.html). The solar tide then "partially cancels out the lunar tide," its [page on tidal variations](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides06_variations.html) says, leaving a neap tide, with high tides a little lower and low tides a little higher than average.

| | Spring tide | Neap tide |
|---|---|---|
| Moon phase | New moon and full moon | First and third quarter |
| Sun, Earth and Moon | In a line | At a right angle |
| Solar tide | Adds to the lunar tide | Partly cancels it |
| Tidal range | Largest of the month | Smallest of the month |
| Peak timing | 1-2 days after new or full moon | About 7 days after a spring tide |

## The biggest tide arrives a day or two late

The geometry and friction of the real oceans hold back the peak, so spring tides arrive **one to two days** after new or full moon, [NTSLF explains](https://ntslf.org/tides/about-tides/tides-faq). NOAA's [tide glossary](https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/glossary.html) calls the delay the age of phase inequality, or the age of tide. The 1953 storm struck about two days after the full moon of 29 January in [NASA's table](https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/phase/phases1901.html).

Pliny the Elder, the Roman encyclopedist [born in AD 23](https://archive.org/details/naturalhistoryof11855plin), had already recorded the delay. Tides swell, his *Natural History* says, "not exactly at the full nor at the new moon, but after them," in John Bostock and Henry Riley's [translation of book 2, chapter 99](https://archive.org/details/naturalhistoryof11855plin/page/126/mode/1up).

## Nobody is sure where the name came from

[NOAA says](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/springtide.html) the term comes from the tide "springing forth," and Parks Canada's [Fundy National Park](https://parks.canada.ca/pn-np/nb/fundy/nature/environment/marees-tides/vives-mortes-spring-neap) derives it from the Old English "springan," which it says "means to well up." NTSLF is more cautious: the origins "are not known for certain," [its FAQ says](https://ntslf.org/tides/about-tides/tides-faq), and its candidates are "the Scandinavian word for 'leap up'" and "a place where water wells up from the ground."

[Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/spring) supplies the root, Old English *springan*, "to leap, leap up, jump," also said of a fountain that would "burst forth." In Middle English, [Etymonline adds](https://www.etymonline.com/word/spring), the noun spring was used of sunrise, the waxing of the moon and "rising tides."

The word [springtide](https://www.etymonline.com/word/springtide) did double duty, meaning springtime in the 1520s and, from the 1540s, a tide higher than usual.

## Equinox or solstice, depending on the coast

Around most of the UK there are two high tides and two low tides a day, and there spring tides are "particularly large close to the equinoxes (21 March and 21 September) when the sun is overhead at the equator," [NTSLF says](https://ntslf.org/tides/about-tides/tides-faq). Pliny recorded the equinox effect too, writing that tides rise "higher at the equinoxes, and more so at the autumnal than at the vernal," in the [same chapter](https://archive.org/details/naturalhistoryof11855plin/page/126/mode/1up).

Coasts with one tide a day, like many in the [Gulf of Mexico](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides07_cycles.html), barely register the spring and neap cycle; for that kind of tide NOAA's [glossary](https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/glossary.html) calls the spring range "of no practical significance." Their range grows and shrinks instead with the Moon's swing north and south of the equator, its declination: when that swing is greatest, at the tropic tides, the [glossary](https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/glossary.html) notes "a tendency for an increase in the diurnal range."

Those coasts, and mixed-tide coasts like the [US West Coast](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_tides/tides07_cycles.html), peak nearer the solstices, when the tropic range "may be expected to be especially large," in the words of the [same glossary](https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/glossary.html). California's [Coastal Commission](https://www.coastal.ca.gov/kingtides/learn.html) says its king tides, "the highest high tides of the year," come in winter, and that around the New Year Earth is closest to the Sun, "further increasing the gravitational pull on the ocean."

## Perigee can add a foot, and a popular name

Often between **6 and 8 times a year**, a new or full moon also coincides closely with perigee, the Moon's closest approach to Earth, producing what NOAA calls a [perigean spring tide](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/perigean-spring-tide.html). The extra height depends on the place, but [NOAA says](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/perigean-spring-tide.html) it is "not uncommon" for high tides then to run **more than a foot** above those of spring tides near apogee. In Anchorage, Alaska, which has a tidal range over 30 feet, the gap can reach **3 feet or more** at high tide, according to the [same NOAA page](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/perigean-spring-tide.html). People often call such extremes king tides, a "non-scientific term" in [NOAA's words](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/kingtide.html).

The spring tide under that winter flood came two nights after a full moon, on the same schedule the Moon and Sun keep every month. The storm on top of it was the part no tide table could show. Strictly FYI.
