If cilantro has ever made your salsa taste like soap, you have company. When 23andMe asked its customers whether fresh cilantro tasted like soap, **12.4%** of the [18,495 who answered yes or no](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/2044-7248-1-22/tables/3) said yes.

The [same 2012 study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1209.2096) found a variant linked to that answer near OR6A2, a smell-receptor gene. The authors' own estimate of how much it explains is far smaller than a gene-for-soap story suggests.

## Why does cilantro taste like soap?

Cilantro tastes like soap to some people because its soapy aroma is largely attributed to aldehydes, and aroma chemists describe one group of them, mostly (E)-2-decenal and (E)-2-dodecenal, as soapy, fatty or pungent. Not everyone notices that note, and a gene variant in a cluster of smell-receptor genes accounts for a small, measurable share of the difference.

## The soap is in the leaf's aldehydes

The 23andMe paper, published in *Flavour*, cites aroma studies for the chemistry: the key aroma components of cilantro are aldehydes, and the (E)-2-alkenals among them are described as ["soapy, fatty, 'like cilantro,' or pungent."](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/2044-7248-1-22)

The paper describes two other aldehydes in cilantro, **decanal and dodecanal**, as fruity, green and pungent; the soapy label goes to the (E)-2-alkenals. Both are perfumery chemicals: EPA chemical-use reports compiled by PubChem list fragrance as an industrial use of both [decanal](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/8175) and [dodecanal](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/8194), and list **soap, cleaning compound and toilet preparation manufacturing** among the sectors that process them.

The authors left open whether the problem is smell or taste: ["It is suspected, although not proven, that cilantro dislike is largely driven by the odor rather than the taste."](https://arxiv.org/html/1209.2096)

## One variant held up, and it explains little

Nicholas Eriksson and seven 23andMe colleagues scanned the genomes of **14,604** customers of European ancestry who answered the soap question, then checked the strongest signals in **11,851** who answered only the second question the site had asked, ["Do you like the taste of fresh (not dried) cilantro?"](https://arxiv.org/html/1209.2096)

The one that held up, **rs72921001**, sits on chromosome 11 within a cluster of eight smell-receptor genes, and the authors wrote that one of them, OR6A2, ["appears to be the most promising candidate"](https://arxiv.org/html/1209.2096): the rat version of its receptor, I7, responds to a range of aldehydes, including (E)-2-decenal. They said a neighbor, OR10A2, may also be a reasonable candidate.

The variant's C form goes with tasting soap, and in Europeans it is the [more common of the two](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/2044-7248-1-22/tables/2); each copy of the other form, A, was linked to an [odds ratio of 0.81](https://arxiv.org/abs/1209.2096) for tasting soap, odds about a fifth lower.

> Despite "the strength of the association," the authors wrote, the variant ["explains only about 0.5% of the variance in perceiving that cilantro tastes soapy."](https://arxiv.org/html/1209.2096)

Their estimate of how much all the common variants their chips measured could explain was low too, a heritability of [about 0.087](https://arxiv.org/abs/1209.2096) on a scale where 1 would mean genetic differences account for all of the variation, a figure they could not statistically separate from zero and treated as a lower bound.

A single gene makes a tidy answer. The paper's own conclusion is more cautious: cilantro dislike ["may stem from genetic variants in olfactory receptors."](https://arxiv.org/abs/1209.2096)

## The 3 to 21% figure measured dislike, not soap

The other figure that circulates, **3 to 21%**, comes from [another 2012 paper in *Flavour*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/2044-7248-1-8), by Lilli Mauer and Ahmed El-Sohemy of the University of Toronto. [Their analysis covered **1,381** young adults aged 20 to 29](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/2044-7248-1-8), recruited on campus, who rated cilantro on a 9-point scale from "dislike extremely" to "like extremely" or said they had never tried it or would not, and it counted ratings of 1 to 4 as dislike.

The percentages cover only the **1,108** who had tried it; [27% of the East Asian participants and 16% of the Caucasian participants](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/2044-7248-1-8/tables/2) never had. The two surveys rank the groups differently:

| Group | Disliked it, of those who had tried it (Toronto) | Said it tastes like soap (23andMe) |
|---|---|---|
| East Asian | 20.8% (81 of 390) | 8.4% of 463 |
| European or Caucasian | 17.6% (85 of 484) | 13.0% of 15,186 |
| South Asian | 7.0% (11 of 157) | 3.9% of 335 |
| Hispanic or Latino | 3.8% (1 of 26) | 8.7% of 898 |

The people differ too, campus volunteers against genetics-company customers, and neither set describes a whole ethnic group.

Mauer and El-Sohemy judged differences in flavor perception, ["possibly attributable to genetic differences,"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/2044-7248-1-8) the likely cause, but said they "cannot rule out" exposure, since cilantro "is most popular" in the cuisines of the groups with the fewest dislikers. Testing its soap-linked variant group by group, the 23andMe team found ["the differences in allele frequency across populations do not explain the differences in soapy-taste detection,"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/2044-7248-1-22) and allowed for experience too: "many people might initially dislike cilantro yet later come to appreciate it."

## A twin study put the genetic share higher

A [twin study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3589946/) from the Monell Chemical Senses Center, published in *Chemical Senses* in 2012, tested twins at the Twins Days Festival in Twinsburg, Ohio, and put the heritability of how pleasant people found cilantro's smell at **0.38**, and of its flavor at **0.52**.

Those figures assume, as the paper flags, that the environment twins share adds nothing, and the cilantro ratings came from only [15 pairs of non-identical twins](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3589946/) against 113 or 114 identical pairs. The 23andMe authors suggested the gap ["could be due to the differences in phenotypes measured between the two studies,"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/2044-7248-1-22) or to rare variants their chips missed.

Testing [44 variants](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3589946/) in taste and smell genes, the Monell team linked cilantro ratings to three genes: TAS2R50, a bitter-taste receptor; GNAT3, which encodes part of gustducin, a protein that [carries both bitter and sweet taste signals](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8657284/); and TRPA1, an ion channel [activated by pungent compounds](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15046718/) in mustard oil, cinnamon and ginger. Those links cleared only nominal thresholds, out of [1,188 possible associations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3589946/) the team tested, and when the 23andMe team checked the same three variants against its soap question, it was ["unable to replicate"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/2044-7248-1-22) any of them.

## Bugs are the other complaint, and an old one

Mauer and El-Sohemy sum up the descriptions: people who like cilantro may call it "fresh, fragrant or citrusy," while those who dislike it ["report that it tastes like soap, mold, dirt or bugs, among other descriptors."](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/2044-7248-1-8)

The bugs have a chemical match. (E)-2-decenal "is a major component of the alarm pheromone" of the brown marmorated stink bug, a [2020 study in *Frontiers in Physiology*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7494974/) notes. Mauer and El-Sohemy report the suggestion that it is one of the compounds people find unpleasant in cilantro, and note that the idea has not yet been tested.

John Gerard's *Herball*, printed in London in **1597**, opens its chapter on coriander, the plant whose leaves are cilantro, by calling the common kind ["a very stinking herbe, smelling like the stinking worme called in Latine Cimex"](https://archive.org/details/herballorgeneral00gera_0/page/n888/mode/1up). *Cimex* is still the scientific name of the [bed bug genus](https://www.cdc.gov/bed-bugs/about/index.html). Gerard condemned ["the greene and stinking leaues"](https://archive.org/details/herballorgeneral00gera_0/page/n888/mode/1up) and praised "the drie and pleasant well sauoring seede."

The plant's name may carry the same complaint. [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/coriander) traces coriander through Latin to Greek *koriannon*, "often said by botanists to be related to *koris* 'bedbug' from the bad smell of the unripe fruit," though it allows that the word may instead be "a non-Indo-European word conformed to the Greek insect name."

Gerard faulted the green leaf in 1597, and 23andMe's liking question still specified fresh, not dried, cilantro four centuries later. The aldehydes are in that fresh leaf for whoever eats it; the split lies in the eaters, and most of it has not been traced to any gene. Strictly FYI.
