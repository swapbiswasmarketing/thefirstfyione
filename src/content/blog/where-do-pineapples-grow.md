For many people the pineapple "evokes the 50th state," as *Smithsonian* magazine [put it in 2013](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/its-pineapple-season-but-does-your-fruit-come-from-hawaii-5211854/). Yet on Lanai, where Dole's pineapple fields peaked at nearly **15,000 acres** in the early 1980s, an Associated Press story in the [Los Angeles Times](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1992-10-04-me-553-story.html) reported in 1992 that only **300** acres had been planted that year. An executive of Castle and Cooke Properties, the Dole subsidiary that owned nearly all of the island, [told the AP](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1992-10-04-me-553-story.html) that the cost of producing pineapple in Hawaii was "uncompetitive."

In 2024, Costa Rica supplied **89%** of the fresh pineapples the United States imported, by weight, according to the [detailed trade matrix](https://www.fao.org/faostat/en/#data/TM) of the UN Food and Agriculture Organization, and the variety behind that trade was bred in Hawaii.

## Where do pineapples grow?

Pineapples grow in the tropics and warm subtropics, mostly at low elevations, on spiky-leaved plants rooted in the soil, not on trees. Each plant normally bears [a single fruit](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/ananas-comosus/) at its center. **Costa Rica, the Philippines and Indonesia** grew the most in 2024, by [FAO's production figures](https://www.fao.org/faostat/en/#data/QCL).

Julia Morton, in [Fruits of Warm Climates](https://hort.purdue.edu/newcrop/morton/pineapple.html) (1987), called it "a tropical or near tropical plant" confined, outside greenhouses, to low elevations between 30 degrees north and 25 degrees south, though she also recorded crops grown up to 7,545 feet in Ecuador. In colder climates it needs a greenhouse: fruit was being produced in Holland in 1686, Morton records, and when she wrote, the Azores had grown pineapples under glass "for many years."

In the continental US, the [University of Florida](https://gardeningsolutions.ifas.ufl.edu/plants/edibles/fruits/pineapple/) says, pineapples are grown in protected spots in landscapes throughout Florida, and a freeze below 28 degrees Fahrenheit will kill the plant.

## The plant is a bromeliad rooted in the soil

A common picture puts pineapples on trees, but the pineapple is a bromeliad, a relative of Spanish moss, and unlike most bromeliads it grows in soil at ground level, the [University of Florida notes](https://gardeningsolutions.ifas.ufl.edu/plants/edibles/fruits/pineapple/). In [Morton's account](https://hort.purdue.edu/newcrop/morton/pineapple.html) the plant stands two and a half to five feet tall, a rosette of waxy, long-pointed leaves 20 to 72 inches long around a very short, stout stem. That stem later runs up through the fruit as its "fibrous but fairly succulent" core, she wrote, and keeps growing above it into the leafy crown.

When the plant is big enough, the stem sends up a head of **100 to 200 flowers** arranged in a spiral, each of which develops into a berrylike fruitlet, and the fruitlets fuse into one fruit, by the account in a [University of Hawaii growers' guide](https://www3.ctahr.hawaii.edu/oc/freepubs/pdf/F_N-7.pdf); North Carolina State's extension service counts [50 to 200](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/ananas-comosus/) flowers. Each flower stays open for only a day, the [University of Florida](https://gardeningsolutions.ifas.ufl.edu/plants/edibles/fruits/pineapple/) says.

## The first fruit takes about 18 months

On Hawaii's plantations around 1990, a field planted with crowns needed about **18 months** before its first harvest, the [University of Hawaii guide](https://www3.ctahr.hawaii.edu/oc/freepubs/pdf/F_N-7.pdf) says, and in hotter equatorial climates the crop can take **11 to 14 months**. The same field could then give two more crops, called ratoon crops, from suckers, side shoots on the same plant, with the last harvested at about [month 46](https://www3.ctahr.hawaii.edu/oc/freepubs/pdf/F_N-7.pdf).

Home growers wait longer. The [University of Florida](https://gardeningsolutions.ifas.ufl.edu/plants/edibles/fruits/pineapple/) tells gardeners to expect fruit 18 to 32 months after planting, and [North Carolina State](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/ananas-comosus/) warns that a plant started from a crown "can take at least 24 months to flower."

Commercial growers set the date themselves. They "force" flowering, most commonly with ethephon, a compound that releases the plant hormone ethylene, so that each harvest can be [timed to anticipated market demand](https://www3.ctahr.hawaii.edu/oc/freepubs/pdf/F_N-7.pdf). Morton traces the method to 1874, when it was [discovered by accident](https://hort.purdue.edu/newcrop/morton/pineapple.html) in the Azores that smoke would bring pineapple plants into bloom in six weeks; ethylene turned out to be the active ingredient.

## Hawaii led the world, then canning moved abroad

Pineapples were growing in Hawaii by 1813, when their presence was first recorded, and James Dole's Hawaiian Pineapple Company produced its first profitable lot of canned pineapple in 1903, according to a [2012 history of the industry](https://doi.org/10.21273/HORTSCI.47.10.1390) in *HortScience* by Duane Bartholomew and two colleagues. By 1930, the authors write, Hawaii led the world in canned pineapple and had the world's largest canneries.

Canned production peaked in **1957**, but the stage for the decline had been set in the 1930s, when Del Monte established its Philippine Packing Corporation, and the slide sped up after Castle and Cooke's Dole division opened plantations and canneries in the Philippines in 1964 and in Thailand in 1972, the [HortScience history](https://doi.org/10.21273/HORTSCI.47.10.1390) records. Del Monte's Philippine company dates itself a few years earlier, to **1926**, in [its own history](https://www.delmontephil.com/about-us/our-history).

The decline came mainly because foreign canneries had labor costs about **one-tenth** of Hawaii's, Bartholomew and his co-authors [conclude](https://doi.org/10.21273/HORTSCI.47.10.1390), and when they wrote in 2012 every Hawaiian cannery had closed, the last on Maui in 2007, while Dole and Maui Gold still grew fresh pineapples there, mostly for the local market. Del Monte shut the last of its fresh produce operation on Oahu in 2008, and Maui Land & Pineapple harvested its last crop in December 2009, the [Honolulu Star-Bulletin](https://archives.starbulletin.com/content/20091224_maui_pineapple_harvests_final_crop) reported.

By [FAO's figures](https://www.fao.org/faostat/en/#data/QCL), the United States grew more pineapples than any other country in 1961, but by 1975 Thailand grew twice as much.

## Costa Rica grows a pineapple bred in Hawaii

As the canneries closed, the Pineapple Research Institute of Hawaii produced the MD-2 hybrid, "now the world's pre-eminent fresh fruit cultivar," whose "first and major beneficiary" was Costa Rica, where Del Monte had started a fresh-fruit plantation in the late 1970s, the [2012 history](https://doi.org/10.21273/HORTSCI.47.10.1390) says. The [2015 paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4867222/) that sequenced its genome calls the MD-2 "the dominant pineapple variety for the global fresh fruit market."

> The MD-2, the variety that now leads the world's fresh-pineapple trade, came out of Hawaii's own breeding program while Hawaii's canneries were closing.

Costa Rica grew **3,118,093 tonnes** of pineapples in 2024, by [FAOSTAT's count](https://www.fao.org/faostat/en/#data/QCL), more than any other country and ahead of the Philippines and Indonesia; in 2023 Indonesia had led. Its hold on the trade is far larger: in 2023 it shipped 2.11 million tonnes of fresh pineapples abroad, **58%** of world exports, by [FAO's trade figures](https://www.fao.org/faostat/en/#data/TCL). US fresh-pineapple imports grew more than elevenfold between 1990 and 2024, and Costa Rica's share of them rose from 48% to 89%, by the same [trade matrix](https://www.fao.org/faostat/en/#data/TM).

## The wild pineapple came from South America

Morton placed the pineapple's native home in southern Brazil and Paraguay, "perhaps especially the Parana-Paraguay River" area, where its wild relatives grow, and [wrote](https://hort.purdue.edu/newcrop/morton/pineapple.html) that it was apparently domesticated by Indigenous peoples and carried north to Mexico and the West Indies long before Europeans arrived. The [genome paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4867222/) instead puts its "initial domestication in northern South America, possibly more than 6000 BP," meaning over 6,000 years before the present, so the two accounts disagree on where people first farmed it.

Either way, it was "already cultivated on a continental-wide scale" when Columbus reached Guadeloupe on November 4, 1493, on his second voyage, and he and his sailors met pineapple plants and fruit at a Carib village, the [same paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4867222/) records. By the end of the 16th century, [the authors write](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4867222/), the pineapple was "pantropical."

On the US mainland, the fruit that evokes Hawaii is usually Costa Rican, and the variety in the box was most likely bred in Hawaii as the canneries there closed. Strictly FYI.
