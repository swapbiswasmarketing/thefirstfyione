Charles Batchelor, Thomas Edison's closest associate, was writing up a run of experiments with carbon filaments made from a variety of materials at the Menlo Park laboratory in New Jersey in October 1879. One of them, a carbonized filament of uncoated cotton thread, glowed for a total of **14 and a half hours** over 22-23 October, and it [led the Menlo Park team to believe that they were on the right track](https://americanhistory.si.edu/lighting/19thcent/invent19.htm), the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History says.

Eight months earlier, in Newcastle upon Tyne, the English chemist Joseph Swan had already shown a working electric lamp to [an audience of over seven hundred people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Swan).

## When was the light bulb invented?

The practical light bulb dates to **1879**, when two men working separately made carbon lamps that worked. Joseph Swan [showed his lamp at lectures in Newcastle, England, between December 1878 and February 1879](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Swan), and Edison's team in New Jersey kept a cotton-thread filament glowing [from the early morning hours of October 22](https://edison.rutgers.edu/life-of-edison/biographical-essays/lighting/the-carbon-filament-lamp). Edison's lamp patent, [number 223,898, was granted on January 27, 1880](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/thomas-edisons-patent-application-for-the-light-bulb).

## Edison was one runner in a crowded field

["Edison was neither the first nor the only person trying to invent an incandescent electric lamp,"](https://americanhistory.si.edu/lighting/19thcent/comp19.htm) the museum's Lighting a Revolution exhibition says. It names Moses Farmer, William Sawyer and Albon Man, and Hiram Maxim in the United States, and St. George Lane-Fox and Swan in England. The National Archives puts the state of play in 1878 plainly: a practical long-burning electric light [had eluded scientists for decades](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/thomas-edisons-patent-application-for-the-light-bulb).

Electric light itself was older than all of them. The Smithsonian says Humphry Davy [demonstrated to the Royal Society in 1806](https://americanhistory.si.edu/lighting/19thcent/prec19.htm) that an electric arc between two charcoal rods gave a powerful light, and arc lamps from the 1870s gave many cities their first electric streetlights. Edison's goal, in the exhibition's phrase, lay in "subdividing the light" of an arc lamp into small lamps suitable for use indoors.

## Swan's lamp worked first, in Newcastle

Swan had been at the problem for decades. By 1860 he [was able to demonstrate a working device](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Joseph_Wilson_Swan), Grace's Guide records, but the vacuum in his bulb was poor and his source of electricity inadequate, so the bulb was inefficient and short-lived.

Swan first showed his carbon lamp in public at a lecture for the Newcastle upon Tyne Chemical Society on **December 18, 1878**. It was not yet reliable: in his laboratory, after burning brightly for some minutes, [the lamp broke down owing to excessive current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Swan). He repeated the lecture on January 17, 1879, with the lamp shown in actual operation, and on **February 3, 1879**, he showed the lamp in the lecture theatre of the Literary and Philosophical Society of Newcastle, with [Sir William Armstrong of Cragside presiding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Swan).

That early lamp had a weakness. Like those of the other contenders, it [used a carbon rod of relatively low electrical resistance](https://americanhistory.si.edu/lighting/19thcent/comp19.htm), so it was practical only wired in series, where several lamps turned on and off together, or close to the power supply. Swan had experimented with carbonized paper filaments for some years, the museum adds, and once he learned that a high-resistance filament was needed, he quickly adapted it to his lamps. He patented the lamp on [November 27, 1880, as British patent number 4933](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co8405148/light-bulb). The Institution of Mechanical Engineers' 1914 obituary of Swan records that the first public lighting of a hall by his lamps [took place in Newcastle-on-Tyne on 20th October 1880](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Joseph_Wilson_Swan).

## Edison announced an answer a year before he had one

"When, in 1878, Edison announced that he had the answer and knew how to make an incandescent light, gas stocks around the world fell," [the Smithsonian's account of the lamp records](https://americanhistory.si.edu/lighting/19thcent/invent19.htm). "The only problem was that his answer was wrong." The answer he had then was a platinum filament, with a regulator that shunted the current away every few minutes so the filament would not overheat and burn out, and a bulb that kept switching itself off was hardly practical, the museum says.

Once the team was back on carbon, Edison filed for a patent on [November 4, 1879](https://patents.google.com/patent/US223898A/en), and its first claim is for a lamp made with a filament of "carbon of high resistance." He listed the materials he had already carbonized as ["cotton and linen thread, wood splints, papers coiled in various ways"](https://patents.google.com/patent/US223898A/en), and the patent describes the carbon placed in a glass bulb exhausted to "one-millionth of an atmosphere."

Both details mattered. Edison saw that lighting a whole town needed lamps wired in parallel, which was possible only with [what was then a very high pressure, about 100 volts](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Joseph_Wilson_Swan), and a small current for each lamp, and that carbon is permanent in a vacuum, the Institution of Electrical Engineers' 1914 obituary of Swan explains.

> "Edison's patent was an improvement on electric lamps, not the invention of them ..." - [National Archives, Milestone Documents](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/thomas-edisons-patent-application-for-the-light-bulb)

Edison also understood publicity. The New Year's Eve demonstration at Menlo Park on December 31, 1879, [became the first in a series of promotional events](https://americanhistory.si.edu/lighting/19thcent/promo19.htm), all designed to link Edison's name with the new lighting technology in the public's mind. Henry Villard, president of the Oregon Railway and Navigation Company, attended it, and his company's new steamship, the S.S. Columbia, [became the first commercial order for Edison's light bulb](https://americanhistory.si.edu/lighting/19thcent/promo19.htm).

## The 40-hour bulb is the legend version

The cotton-thread test has grown in the retelling. "Much mythology surrounds these experiments," [the Smithsonian notes](https://americanhistory.si.edu/lighting/19thcent/invent19.htm), adding that the filament in the notebooks burned for a time "not the 40 hours of legend."

The bigger figure still circulates. In 2022 the Government of Japan's Highlighting Japan magazine quoted the managing director of the Japan Electric Association saying Edison's cotton filament was improved ["to allow for 40 hours continuous burning"](https://www.gov-online.go.jp/eng/publicity/book/hlj/html/202208/202208_08_en.html). The same article tells the bamboo story too. A bamboo growing wild in Kyoto Prefecture, found by one of Edison's researchers in 1880, gave a filament that lasted **1,200 hours**, and bulbs made with it were [manufactured from around 1881](https://www.gov-online.go.jp/eng/publicity/book/hlj/html/202208/202208_08_en.html) until 1894, it says.

## Carbon gave way to metal filaments

Edison's carbon did not last either. Austria's Carl Auer von Welsbach [developed the first commercially practical metal filament lamp in 1898](https://americanhistory.si.edu/lighting/20thcent/prec20.htm), using osmium. Tantalum lamps replaced osmium in 1902, and in 1904 three different tungsten lamps appeared on the European market almost simultaneously. Their tungsten was too brittle to bend. In 1910 General Electric began selling lamps made with William Coolidge's bendable, or "ductile," tungsten wire, which [gave 10 lumens per watt](https://americanhistory.si.edu/lighting/20thcent/prec20.htm). The Smithsonian says that this, combined with the growing level of electrification in the United States, [effectively eliminated competition from gas lighting](https://americanhistory.si.edu/lighting/19thcent/comp19.htm).

## The rivals settled by merging

In Britain, the Edison Electric Light Company threatened Swan's company with an infringement suit, and [instead of going to trial, the two companies agreed to merge](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co8405148/light-bulb), the Science Museum Group says. The Smithsonian says Swan's patent position in England was strong enough that the merger was [arranged in mid-1882](https://americanhistory.si.edu/lighting/19thcent/comp19.htm). The Science Museum Group dates [the founding of the merged company to October 26, 1883](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co8405148/light-bulb), and its Ediswan bulbs dominated the British market.

The Institution of Electrical Engineers' obituarist saw two different inventions. In 1914 the obituary said ["Edison and Swan were hardly racing, as they were on different roads"](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/Joseph_Wilson_Swan): Edison was solving the distribution of light across a town, and Swan was making a practical carbon lamp.

So the practical light bulb has two working dates, eight months apart, one in a Newcastle lecture theatre and one in a New Jersey laboratory. By 1883 the two rivals were [one company, selling Ediswan bulbs](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co8405148/light-bulb). Strictly FYI.
