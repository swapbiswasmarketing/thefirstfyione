The story is always told the same way. Erik the Red, [outlawed in Iceland around **982**](https://archive.org/details/booksettlementi00ellwgoog) after a feud that left men dead, spends three winters exploring an unknown coast to the west. He sails home and recruits settlers by giving the place a name it has not earned. Men will desire much the more to go there, he tells them, if the land has a good name. An outlaw, an ice sheet, and a real-estate pitch a thousand years old.

Most of that holds up. The quotation does not, and neither does the idea that the name was unearned.

## Why is Greenland called Greenland?

Because Erik the Red chose a name that would sell the place. In the account the Icelandic priest Ari Thorgilsson wrote in the twelfth century, Erik called the land Greenland and ["said that people would be more eager to go there because the land had a good name"](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Translation:%C3%8Dslendingab%C3%B3k). No older account of Erik's naming survives, and nothing at all from his own century.

An older explanation of the name does exist: the Saxon churchman Adam of Bremen, writing about fifty years before Ari, said the land took its name from its people, [tinged green by the salt sea](https://eaglehill.us/JONAonline/articles/JONA-Sp-2/08-Grove.shtml) they lived beside.

## The line everyone quotes has a chain of custody

Ari Thorgilsson, known as Ari the Learned, was [born in **1067 or 1068**](https://vsnr.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/IslKr.pdf), and his Book of the Icelanders, Islendingabok, is dated to between **1122 and 1133** from the two bishops its prologue names, as the introduction to Sian Gronlie's 2006 Viking Society translation explains. Its Greenland chapter dates the settlement to ["fourteen or fifteen winters before Christianity came here to Iceland"](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Translation:%C3%8Dslendingab%C3%B3k), and the same book dates the summer of the conversion, when Olaf Tryggvason fell, to "a thousand after the birth of Christ". That lands the voyage around **985**, the [date modern research still uses](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4673052/) for the Norse settlement of Greenland.

So the account is about fourteen decades late, set down by a man born roughly eighty years after the ships sailed. Ari does not hide where his date came from. In the Old Norse text, the count rests on what [a man who had himself sailed out with Erik told Thorkell Gellisson in Greenland](https://heimskringla.no/wiki/%C3%8Dslendingab%C3%B3k), and Thorkell was Ari's own uncle, his father's brother.

Ari also asked to be corrected. His prologue tells readers that ["whatever is incorrect in this scholarship, one should consider better what is proven to be truer"](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Translation:%C3%8Dslendingab%C3%B3k).

The wording quoted at the top is later still. "Men will desire much the more to go there if the land has a good name" is [Eiriks saga rauda, chapter 2](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/17946/17946-h/17946-h.htm), where J. Sephton's 1880 translation has Erik say it in his own voice, and Sephton took that chapter from the Hauksbok version of the saga. The saga itself is [thought to have been written in the 13th century](https://archive.org/details/bibliographyofic01hermuoft/page/n35/mode/1up), generations after Ari was in his grave.

Landnamabok, the Book of Settlements, tells the naming in nearly the same words and adds the sequel: men of lore say that ["twenty-five ships went to Greenland from Broadfirth and Borgfirth, and that fourteen got through to the west"](https://archive.org/details/booksettlementi00ellwgoog), the rest driven back or lost. A fleet followed. The sea took a share of it.

> The popular story puts that sentence in Erik's mouth. Ari, writing generations later, gives only a paraphrase, and the one thing he sources is the date: a man who had sailed with Erik, talking to Ari's own uncle.

## The green was real and it is still there

About **80 percent** of the island lies under the ice sheet, which the National Snow and Ice Data Center measures at [1.7 million square kilometres, or 656,000 square miles](https://nsidc.org/learn/parts-cryosphere/ice-sheets/ice-sheet-quick-facts). The fifth that is not includes sheltered fjord pastures in the south, where Norse farmers raised livestock [from the 10th to the 15th century](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1536/).

UNESCO inscribed that landscape in **2017** as [Kujataa](https://slks.dk/english/work-areas/cultural-heritage/sites-and-monuments/world-heritage-sites/kujataa-greenland), described in the listing as "the earliest introduction of farming to the Arctic, and the Norse expansion of settlement beyond Europe". The protected area takes in Qassiarsuk, the site of Erik's own farm at [Brattahlid](https://visitgreenland.com/destinations/qassiarsuk/), where the same hillsides are still used to graze animals. A sales pitch can be true and still be a sales pitch.

## The glaciers up north peaked, but the farms show no chill

The standard defence of the name is that the whole region really was greener in the tenth century, during the Medieval Warm Period. The record is patchier than that.

Nicolas Young, Avriel Schweinsberg, Jason Briner and Joerg Schaefer published [beryllium-10 dating of moraines](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4673052/) in Science Advances on **4 December 2015**, from boulders in two Baffin Island valleys and in the Uigordleq Lake valley in western Greenland. The glaciers were ["at or near their maximum LIA configurations"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4673052/) during the Medieval Warm Period, and the chronology keeps them near that maximum "between ~975 and 1275 CE". Little Ice Age extent, centuries before the Little Ice Age.

The valleys sit at about 70 degrees north, so they speak for the Baffin Bay region rather than for the farms at Kujataa. Pairing the moraines with other climate records, the authors write that the proxy data ["suggest that the western North Atlantic region remained cool, whereas the eastern North Atlantic region was comparatively warmer during the MWP"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4673052/).

Records from beside the southern farms tell a different story. Oxygen isotopes in midge remains from lake mud near Narsaq, studied by G. Everett Lasher and Yarrow Axford of Northwestern University, show southern Greenland was [relatively warm when the Norse lived there](https://news.northwestern.edu/stories/2019/02/study-shows-that-vikings-enjoyed-a-warm-greenland), about **1.5 degrees Celsius** above the cooler centuries around it. A 2022 record from a lake beside a former Norse farm found [no substantial temperature changes during the settlement period](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8942370/). Whatever Baffin Bay was doing, the farms themselves show no medieval chill.

## The ice-core forest is more than a hundred millennia too old

The second defence cites a real paper and loses its date. In **2007**, Eske Willerslev and twenty-nine co-authors published [DNA recovered from the base of the Dye 3 ice core](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2694912/) in Science: alder, spruce, pine and the yew family, from silty ice in southern Greenland now lying below "more than two kilometers of ice". The invertebrate DNA matched butterflies and moths most strongly, with weaker matches to beetles, flies and spiders. A boreal forest on Greenland, recovered as molecules.

The age is the part the myth drops. The paper reports that ["all four dating methods give overlapping dates for the silty ice between 450Ka and 800Ka"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2694912/), or **450,000 to 800,000 years ago**. The authors add that they ["cannot rule out the possibility of a LIG age"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2694912/) for the basal ice, meaning the Last Interglacial, which the paper puts at "~130-116Ka". Even that youngest reading puts the forest more than a hundred thousand years before any Norse ship.

## Nobody swapped the names

The tidiest version of the story has the Norse naming Iceland ugly to repel settlers and Greenland pretty to attract them, one trick with two matching halves. The names came from different men, generations apart.

| Name | Who named it | When | What the source says |
|---|---|---|---|
| Iceland | Floki Vilgerdarson's party | before c. **870** | Named after a fjord full of drift ice, per Landnamabok |
| Greenland | Erik the Red | c. **985** | Named so people would be "more eager to go there", per Islendingabok |

Landnamabok tells the Iceland story without a year. Floki's party wintered at Vatnsfjordur on Bardastrond, were so taken up with the fishing that they never gathered hay, and lost all their livestock over the winter. In a cold spring Floki climbed a high mountain and saw, north beyond it, ["a firth full of drift ice"](https://archive.org/details/booksettlementi00ellwgoog), and the party named the whole island for it. The book does not name the fjord, and it puts the voyage before the settlement that Ari dates to ["eight hundred and seventy winters after the birth of Christ"](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Translation:%C3%8Dslendingab%C3%B3k). Erik's voyage was more than a century off.

In Greenlandic the country has a name of its own, **Kalaallit Nunaat**, with no green in it. The name [means the country of the Greenlanders](https://um.dk/kina/en/about-denmark/the-realm/greenland/), after the people rather than the land. Underneath the branding anecdote sits a real pasture at Kujataa, a remembered sales line, one priest's paraphrase of it fourteen decades on, and a forest that was gone more than a hundred thousand years, and probably far longer, before Erik sighted the coast.
