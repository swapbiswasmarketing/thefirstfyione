Herod the Great, the king in the Christmas story, had been dead for nearly four years when AD 1 began. [Matthew's Gospel](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+2&version=KJV) says Jesus was born "in the days of Herod the king," and Herod died in **4 BC**, by the reckoning [most scholars follow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Date_of_the_birth_of_Jesus).

A child born in Herod's reign was born in 4 BC or earlier: "before Christ," by the calendar that counts its years from his birth. The Gospels themselves give no date, "not even the time of year," as Andrew McGowan puts it in [Bible History Daily](https://www.biblicalarchaeology.org/daily/biblical-topics/new-testament/how-december-25-became-christmas/). The familiar answer, December 25 of AD 1, was put together from later documents.

## When was Jesus actually born?

Jesus was [most likely born between about **6 and 4 BC**](https://theconversation.com/what-was-the-christmas-star-astronomy-might-hold-the-answer-270358), the range most biblical scholars accept. Matthew and Luke set the birth under Herod the Great, who died in 4 BC, and [Matthew](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+2&version=KJV) has Herod kill children aged two and under, allowing a birth as early as 6 BC. No Gospel gives a day, and the AD count came five centuries later, in 525, from [a monk's Easter tables](https://henk-reints.nl/cal/audette/denys.html).

The chapter on calendars in the *Explanatory Supplement to the Astronomical Almanac*, which [NASA reprints](https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/SEhelp/calendars.html) on its eclipse website, credits the count to the sixth-century scholar Dionysius Exiguus and puts the problem plainly:

> "What Dionysius did not do is establish an accurate date for the birth of Christ. Although scholars generally believe that Christ was born some years before A.D. 1, the historical evidence is too sketchy to allow a definitive dating."

## Herod's death sets the latest likely year

Josephus, the Jewish historian, put an astronomical marker in his account of Herod's final illness. In [*Antiquities of the Jews*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2848), he writes that on the night Herod had a rebel teacher named Matthias burned alive, "there was an eclipse of the moon," and he places the king's death before the Passover that followed. His English translator, [William Whiston](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2848), dated the eclipse in a footnote: it "happened March 13th, in the year of the Julian period 4710, and the 4th year before the Christian era." NASA's [Five Millennium Catalog of Lunar Eclipses](https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/LEcat5/LE-0099-0000.html) does list a partial eclipse of the Moon on March 13 of the astronomical year -3, which is 4 BC, because astronomers count a year 0.

The astronomer Johannes Kepler had already [put Herod's death in 4 BC](https://bibleinterp.arizona.edu/articles/not368010) early in the seventeenth century. [Most twentieth-century scholarship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herod_the_Great) followed Emil Schurer's case for that date, though a minority still argue for 1 BC and a later eclipse.

Matthew's story then stretches the window backward. Herod, [Matthew writes](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+2&version=KJV), killed the children of Bethlehem "from two years old and under, according to the time which he had diligently inquired of the wise men," so the birth could have come up to two years before the order, around **6 BC**.

## Luke's census points a decade later

[Luke's Gospel](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+2&version=KJV) sends Joseph and Mary to Bethlehem because "there went out a decree from Caesar Augustus that all the world should be taxed," and it adds that "this taxing was first made when Cyrenius was governor of Syria." Josephus also wrote about Cyrenius, the governor better known as Quirinius. In [his account](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2848), Cyrenius came into Judea "to take an account of their substance" after Augustus banished Herod's son Archelaus, and the taxings were "made in the thirty-seventh year of Caesar's victory over Antony at Actium."

That puts the census in [**AD 6 or 7**](https://bibleinterp.arizona.edu/articles/not368010), the date the historian of chronology Philipp Nothaft gives from sources outside the Bible, roughly ten years after Herod died. Yet Luke's first chapter sets its story "in the days of Herod, the king of Judaea" ([Luke 1:5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+1&version=KJV)), so one Gospel points at both dates. The many attempts to reconcile Luke with an earlier birth, Nothaft writes, "[have done little but to further erode the credibility of the Lucan author in this respect](https://bibleinterp.arizona.edu/articles/not368010)."

Luke's last clue is an age. Jesus "began to be about thirty years of age" ([Luke 3:23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+3&version=KJV)) after John started preaching in "the fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius Caesar." That year fell in AD 28 or 29, and early writers such as Tertullian, Eusebius and Epiphanius [counted back thirty years](https://bibleinterp.arizona.edu/articles/not368010) to about **3 or 2 BC**. Nothaft thinks their answer still deserves a hearing: he doubts that the infancy stories' tie to Herod is historical, which leaves him with Luke 3 to go on.

| Clue | Where it comes from | Year it points to |
|---|---|---|
| Born "in the days of Herod the king" | Matthew 2:1 | 4 BC or earlier |
| Children killed "from two years old and under" | Matthew 2:16 | As early as about 6 BC |
| A census "when Cyrenius was governor of Syria" | Luke 2:2, dated by Josephus | AD 6 or 7 |
| "About thirty years of age" after Tiberius's 15th year | Luke 3:1 and 3:23 | About 3 or 2 BC |
| Born under the consuls Caesar and Paullus | A Roman almanac, the Chronograph of 354 | AD 1 |

## The monk behind AD 1 never said when Jesus was born

The usual telling says a sixth-century monk got the year wrong. Dionysius Exiguus, who was compiling tables of [Easter dates](/blog/why-does-easter-change-every-year/), did begin numbering years AD, short for *anno Domini*, in the year of the Lord. His table, the *Explanatory Supplement* chapter records, began with [*Anni Domini Nostri Jesu Christi* 532](https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/SEhelp/calendars.html), the year after an older table counted in years of the emperor Diocletian ran out. Pope Benedict XVI wrote in 2012 that Dionysius had miscalculated by several years, [Time reported](https://web.archive.org/web/20260422081745/https://newsfeed.time.com/2012/11/22/pope-benedict-disputes-jesus-date-of-birth/).

Dionysius's rule for finding the year *ab incarnatione Domini nostri Jesu Christi* is [a plain sum](https://henk-reints.nl/cal/audette/denys.html): 15 times 34, plus 12, plus the year's indiction, its place in the fifteen-year cycle that the same chapter calls [a Roman tax cycle](https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/SEhelp/calendars.html). For the consulship of Probus the Younger, with an indiction of 3, the total in [Dionysius's worked example](https://henk-reints.nl/cal/audette/denys.html) was **525**. Yet nowhere in explaining his table does he tie its starting point, the year of the incarnation, to any other dating system, Bonnie Blackburn and Leofranc Holford-Strevens point out in [*The Oxford Companion to the Year*](https://archive.org/details/oxfordcompaniont00blac): "much less does he explain or justify the underlying date."

The [Chronograph of 354](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/chronography_of_354_00_eintro.htm), a Roman almanac made 171 years before the Easter tables, already lists, in the copies that survive, the birth under the consuls of **AD 1**, Caesar and Paullus: "[Hoc cons. dominus Iesus Christus natus est VIII kal. Ian.](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/chronography_of_354_08_fasti.htm)" The Latin says the Lord Jesus Christ was born in that consulship, on December 25. Counting backward came later still: Bede, the English historian, began numbering the years before AD 1 in the eighth century, with no year 0, so 1 BC runs straight into AD 1, as [the NASA chapter](https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/SEhelp/calendars.html) explains.

## The star matches several skies

The star in Matthew's story cannot settle the year either: the magi arrive in Jerusalem saying "we have seen his star in the east" ([Matthew 2:2](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+2&version=KJV)), and astronomers have matched it to several skies. The astrophysicist Jonti Horner and the physicist Duane Hamacher [survey the candidates](https://theconversation.com/what-was-the-christmas-star-astronomy-might-hold-the-answer-270358) and list the triple conjunction of Jupiter and Saturn in 7 BC that Kepler linked to the star; a comet that Chinese records place in 5 BC, visible for more than 70 days; a probable supernova reported from Korea to Palestine in 4 BC; and a series of conjunctions of Jupiter and Venus in 2 BC. Many biblical scholars, they add, read the star as [pious fiction](https://theconversation.com/what-was-the-christmas-star-astronomy-might-hold-the-answer-270358), a symbolic story rather than a real event.

## December 25 surfaces in a Roman almanac of 354

Around AD 200, Clement of Alexandria reported proposed birthdays of May 20 and of [April 19 or 20](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/03724b.htm), and he "doesn't mention December 25 at all," [McGowan writes](https://www.biblicalarchaeology.org/daily/biblical-topics/new-testament/how-december-25-became-christmas/). A passage giving December 25 in one manuscript of Hippolytus's commentary on Daniel [would date the feast to about 205](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/03724b.htm), but writers before the Byzantine chronicler George Syncellus, who [died after 810](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/06463a.htm), quote the passage without the date. "[Interpolation is certain](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/03724b.htm)," Cyril Martindale concluded in the 1908 *Catholic Encyclopedia*.

At Rome, the earliest evidence of a December 25 birthday is the Chronograph of 354 again: its list of martyrs' commemorations, which Martindale thought [probably dates from **336**](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/03724b.htm), opens "[VIII kal. Ian. natus Christus in Betleem Iudeae](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/chronography_of_354_12_depositions_martyrs.htm)." Christ was born in Bethlehem of Judea, it says, on the eighth day before the Kalends of January, a [Roman way of counting days](/blog/why-does-february-have-28-days/) that lands on December 25. Martindale suspected that this line was inserted later, though, so the record may be no older than the almanac itself.

Two explanations for the date still compete. One holds that the date was [borrowed from a pagan feast of the sun](https://www.biblicalarchaeology.org/daily/biblical-topics/new-testament/how-december-25-became-christmas/): the emperor Aurelian is credited with setting a feast of the birth of Sol Invictus, the Unconquered Sun, on December 25 in 274, and the almanac's own [festival calendar](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/chronography_of_354_06_calendar.htm) marks that day N INVICTI, the birthday of the Unconquered. The other, set out by McGowan, [counts nine months from March 25](https://www.biblicalarchaeology.org/daily/biblical-topics/new-testament/how-december-25-became-christmas/), a date some early Christians gave for both the conception and the crucifixion.

So the calendar that counts from the birth of Jesus most likely starts its count four to six years too late. The monk who numbered those years never stated a birth year at all; he left a sum that came to 525. Strictly FYI.
