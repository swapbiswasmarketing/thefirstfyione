On 4 August 2014, Jianing Wu and three colleagues in the mechanical engineering department of Tsinghua University published the results of [an experiment](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0103823) on honey bee stings. Working in the university's State Key Laboratory of Tribology, the team collected 30 fresh stings, fixed 20 of them upright on droplets of polyvinyl alcohol and drove each one, at about six millimetres per second, into small cubes of agar, silica gel, soft rubber and paraffin wax.

The stings went in. They also turned. Under an electron microscope, the team found that a honey bee's sting carries [two rows of about ten barbs](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0103823) that wrap the shaft in a right-handed helix, set at **8 to 9 degrees** to its axis, and cross-sections photographed before and after each push showed that it turns clockwise as it goes in. The average rotation across every sample was **8.364 degrees**, smaller in the harder materials, which, the authors noted, agrees with the angle between the line of barbs and the shaft. They compared the twist to the spiral flight of a bullet and suggested that it makes the sting harder to remove.

Those barbs are why she usually cannot get the sting back out of your arm, and the material they catch in is why she can get it back out of another insect.

## Why do bees die after stinging?

A worker honey bee that stings a person or another vertebrate usually dies, because her barbed sting [lodges in elastic skin](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11923620/) and she tears free without it, leaving the end of her own abdomen behind. Death can take hours or days to come. Honey bees that sting other insects can withdraw the sting and keep it.

## Eight species out of almost 21,000

James Dorey, Amy-Marie Gilpin and Rosalyn Gloag laid out the arithmetic in [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/most-bees-dont-die-after-stinging-and-other-surprising-bee-facts-227162) in April 2024: "Only eight out of almost 21,000 bee species in the world die when they sting." Those eight are the honey bees, genus Apis. For **99.96% of bee species**, dying is not what stinging does.

The rest of the roll call is odder than the headline. Because their stings lack the honey bee's barbs, [the authors write](https://theconversation.com/most-bees-dont-die-after-stinging-and-other-surprising-bee-facts-227162), "the majority of bees can sting as often as they want". Hundreds of species have lost the ability to sting at all, among them **537 species**, about 2.6% of all bees, of [stingless bees](https://theconversation.com/most-bees-dont-die-after-stinging-and-other-surprising-bee-facts-227162) in the tribe Meliponini, which defend their nests by biting. The vast majority of bees are solitary rather than social, so most of the world's bees have no colony to die for.

| Bee | Can it sting you? | Does it die? |
|---|---|---|
| Worker honey bee, the 8 Apis species | Yes | Usually, the sting stays in you |
| Most other bee species | Yes, often more than once | No |
| Stingless bees, 537 Meliponini species | No, they bite | Not applicable |
| Any male bee, any species | No | Not applicable |

## The barb only bites into skin that stretches

The sting is a stylet flanked by two barbed lancets which, in the words of a [2023 iScience study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10359947/), "protract and retract past the tip of the stylet in an alternating fashion"; the barbs on a retracting lancet are thought to anchor it while the rest of the sting pushes deeper. A 2025 review of honey bee colony defence in Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B [puts the fatal step plainly](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11923620/): "The elastic skin of vertebrates traps the barbed stinger of honeybees", which then tears away from the abdomen.

The pull is lopsided. Measurements cited in [the iScience study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10359947/) found that pulling an Asian honey bee's sting out took about **20 times** the force of pushing it in. A trapped bee is not always lost: to get free with her sting, [the study notes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10359947/), she has to rotate her body around the sting site to pry it loose, which can work but "can be quite laborious".

Comparing the stingers of 51 species of vespid wasps in the journal [Insects](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8397143/) in 2021, Bissessarsingh and Starr describe what heavy barbing buys: it "increases the likelihood of the stinger remaining fatally embedded in the skin of a vertebrate adversary". The same paper credits Jurgen Tautz with the observation that a honey bee can withdraw the stinger after stinging an arthropod, and with the suggestion that failing against a vertebrate may be "an evolutionary 'mistake' on the bees' part". The paper itself reports that the [prevailing opinion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8397143/) runs the other way.

## The sting takes a piece of her abdomen with it

Kirk Visscher and Richard Vetter of UC Riverside, with Scott Camazine of Penn State, set out the [inventory](https://bees.ucr.edu/bee-sting-removal): the detached sting carries away "the entire distal segment of the bee's abdomen, along with a nerve ganglion, various muscles, a venom sac" and the end of her digestive tract.

Three of those parts, the ganglion, the muscles and the venom sac, are still on duty in your skin.

## The sting keeps working after the bee is gone

> The piece left in your skin is a [functioning machine](https://bees.ucr.edu/bee-sting-removal). The attached nerve ganglion coordinates the muscles so the lancets move alternately, a valve and piston on the lancets pump venom "like an old-fashioned water pump", and the barbs give one-way traction "so that the sting continues to work itself deeper into the flesh". The bee is elsewhere. The sting has not finished.

It also marks you. The lone sting keeps releasing alarm pheromone, "effectively tagging the intruder", in the words of the [2025 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11923620/), which draws more bees to the same target. The pheromone comes from the Koschevnikov gland associated with the sting, and its major component is **isopentyl acetate**, according to a [2025 study in Communications Biology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12848076/). Isopentyl acetate is also known as banana oil, and the [US National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/npg/npgd0347.html) describes it as a "colorless liquid with a banana-like odor".

Losing the sting does not end her shift. Cunard and Breed, writing in the [Annals of the Entomological Society of America](https://doi.org/10.1093/aesa/91.5.754) in 1998, found that "following sting autotomy, honey bee workers continue to participate in colony defense by following and harassing potential predators". Death usually follows, but earlier observers, the [iScience study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10359947/) notes, found it may not come for 18 to 114 hours, time in which she may go on defending the hive.

## Darwin saw a cost the colony can carry

The popular version says a honey bee chooses to sacrifice herself for the hive, and that every bee does the same. The arithmetic above disposes of the second half. A mechanism that anchors in one kind of tissue and slides out of another is not an act of will, so the choosing is a story people added.

The sacrifice itself is the mainstream reading, though Darwin never called the sting perfect. In [On the Origin of Species](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1228/1228-h/1228-h.htm) in 1859 he described the bee's sting as an instrument "modified but not perfected for its present purpose", and argued that "if on the whole the power of stinging be useful to the community, it will fulfil all the requirements of natural selection, though it may cause the death of some few members." The prevailing opinion today, [Bissessarsingh and Starr](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8397143/) report, follows Darwin in treating sting autotomy as favoured by natural selection in the interest of the colony as a whole, "and therefore in no way maladaptive".

## The scrape-do-not-pinch rule got tested on a real forearm

Visscher and Vetter ran the experiment on their own forearms. They caught workers leaving a hive, held each one by the wings, and, in [their own write-up](https://bees.ucr.edu/bee-sting-removal), "pressed her against the skin of the inside of the subject's forearm until she stung". One series left stings in for 0.5, 1, 2, 4 or 8 seconds before scraping them out, in five-sting series on each of Visscher's arms; the other removed stings after 2 seconds by scraping or pinching, 10 of each on each man.

Scraped stings produced a mean weal of [80 square millimetres](https://bees.ucr.edu/bee-sting-removal), pinched stings **74**, a difference their analysis found not significant. Delay was the variable that moved the number: weal area grew with the time from sting to removal, even within a few seconds. Their short report ran in [The Lancet in August 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8709689/), and what it found was about speed rather than technique.

The trial rested on two men's arms. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/insect-bites-and-stings/) still tells people to "Brush or scrape the stinger sideways with your fingernail or the edge of a bank card", and not to use tweezers, because "you could squeeze poison out of it".

## No male bee has ever stung anybody

A sting is a modified ovipositor. [Dorey, Gilpin and Gloag](https://theconversation.com/most-bees-dont-die-after-stinging-and-other-surprising-bee-facts-227162) put it as "a tube for laying eggs (ovipositor) that has also been adapted for violent defence", which settles the question of the males: **male bees of all species lack stingers**, having nothing to convert. Some will bluff the motion anyway if you try to grab them.

The honey bee at the end of your arm is not choosing to die. She is driving a barbed, slightly twisting shaft into the one material it usually cannot back out of, and the machine keeps running after she has gone. Strictly FYI.
