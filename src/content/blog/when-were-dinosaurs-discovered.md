"There happily came to Oxford while I was writing of this, a living Elephant," Robert Plot [wrote](https://archive.org/details/naturalhistoryof00plotuoft/page/n165/mode/1up) in his natural history of Oxfordshire, and he used the animal to size up a stone bone. Plot's stone, from a quarry in the Oxfordshire parish of Cornwell, [had](https://archive.org/details/naturalhistoryof00plotuoft/page/n160/mode/1up) "exactly the figure of the lowermost part of the thigh-bone of a Man, or at least of some other Animal." It [measured **two feet** around](https://archive.org/details/naturalhistoryof00plotuoft/page/n160/mode/1up) at the knee end and [weighed almost 20 pounds](https://archive.org/details/naturalhistoryof00plotuoft/page/n161/mode/1up).

Almost every other author, Plot [noted](https://archive.org/details/naturalhistoryof00plotuoft/page/n162/mode/1up), would put a bone that size down to an elephant brought over by the Romans. He compared his fossil with the bones and teeth of the young elephant, shown in Oxford in **1676**, and [found the elephant's](https://archive.org/details/naturalhistoryof00plotuoft/page/n165/mode/1up) "not only of a different shape, but also incomparably bigger," though the beast was "very young and not half grown." With horses, oxen and elephants ruled out, he settled on a giant.

The bone was a dinosaur's, as far as anyone can tell from the engraving. Plot's picture of it is "the first known illustration of a dinosaur bone," according to the [Oxford University Museum of Natural History](https://web.archive.org/web/20061001094736/http://www.oum.ox.ac.uk/learning/pdfs/plot.pdf).

## When were dinosaurs discovered?

Dinosaur bones were found long before anyone knew what they were, so the answer comes in three dates. In **1677** Robert Plot [published](https://archive.org/details/naturalhistoryof00plotuoft/page/n6/mode/1up) a dinosaur bone from an Oxfordshire quarry and took it for a giant's. In **1824** William Buckland formally named and described **Megalosaurus**, which the [Natural History Museum](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/how-dinosaurs-got-their-name.html) says "is thought to be the first dinosaur described in scientific literature." Richard Owen named the group, **Dinosauria**, in a report [printed in 1842](https://archive.org/details/reportofeleventh42lond).

People have probably been "stumbling upon dinosaur bones for millennia without knowing what they were," the [museum says](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/how-dinosaurs-got-their-name.html), and it suggests such finds may have been one of the inspirations for dragons.

## Plot matched his bone to a giant nine feet nine inches tall

Plot got one thing right. Writers such as Agricola and Kircher thought stones like his formed in hollows of rock or by a "sportive plastic power of the Earth," but his had a hollow for the marrow, so it ["must have been a real bone, now petrified."](https://archive.org/details/naturalhistoryof00plotuoft/page/n161/mode/1up)

> "It remains, that (notwithstanding their extravagant magnitude) they must have been the bones of Men or Women." - Robert Plot, *The Natural History of Oxford-shire*

Then he looked for an owner. Pliny, the Roman encyclopedist, had written of Gabbara, a man brought from Arabia in the reign of Claudius, who was, in Plot's words, "exactly of the height of Goliath, viz. nine foot nine inches high," a size ["very proportionable to our bone found at Cornwell."](https://archive.org/details/naturalhistoryof00plotuoft/page/n166/mode/1up) Plot was "rather inclined to believe" that Claudius had brought him to Britain, where he might have died.

The bone itself is gone: "none of Plot's specimens survive in the collections," the [Oxford museum says](https://web.archive.org/web/20061001094736/http://www.oum.ox.ac.uk/learning/pdfs/plot.pdf). Its key to his plates identifies plate 8, figure 4, as the ["Femur of Megalosaurus"](https://web.archive.org/web/20061001094736/http://www.oum.ox.ac.uk/learning/pdfs/plot.pdf) from Cornwell.

## Plot's book carries both 1676 and 1677

Published dates for the find split between **1676** and **1677**, and Plot gives no date for the dig. The book itself carries both years. Its imprimatur, the licence to print signed by Oxford's vice-chancellor, Ralph Bathurst, is dated [April 13, 1676](https://archive.org/details/naturalhistoryof00plotuoft/page/n5/mode/1up). The title page says [1677](https://archive.org/details/naturalhistoryof00plotuoft/page/n6/mode/1up).

## Scrotum humanum began as a caption, possibly a misplaced one

Scrotum humanum is sometimes billed as a dinosaur name older than Megalosaurus. The words come from [a plate](https://archive.org/details/newaccuratesyste05broo/page/n409/mode/1up) in volume 5 of Richard Brookes's natural history, where a drawing of Plot's bone sits under the engraved label "Scrotum Humanum," next to a "Kidney Stone" and a heart-shaped "Anthropocardites." The volume's title page is dated [1763](https://archive.org/details/newaccuratesyste05broo/page/n4/mode/1up).

Brookes's [own text](https://archive.org/details/newaccuratesyste05broo/page/n406/mode/1up) calls the fossil a stone "exactly resembling the lowermost part of the thigh-bone of a man." A separate sentence higher on the [same page](https://archive.org/details/newaccuratesyste05broo/page/n406/mode/1up) mentions stones "exactly representing the private parts of a man." Plot had described stones of that kind [just before his bone](https://archive.org/details/naturalhistoryof00plotuoft/page/n160/mode/1up), and "out of modesty" printed no picture of them.

The Natural History Museum reads the label as Brookes's descriptive name for Plot's fossil, with that line about a man's private parts as his only explanation, and says that, given Plot's fossil, Megalosaurus ["could have been known by a different name."](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/how-dinosaurs-got-their-name.html) Olivier Rieppel, in a [2021 paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/08912963.2021.1954176) in *Historical Biology*, concludes that pinning the name on the fossil "is most likely due to an error on the part of the illustrator."

Either way, the label never became a scientific name. Plot's fossil is lost, so scientists "can't confirm whether it was from a Megalosaurus," and in the 1990s the International Commission on Zoological Nomenclature determined that Scrotum humanum "didn't constitute a valid scientific name," the [museum says](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/how-dinosaurs-got-their-name.html).

## Megalosaurus reached print in 1822 and was described in 1824

On **February 20, 1824**, William Buckland, president of the Geological Society of London, read his "[Notice on the Megalosaurus or great Fossil Lizard of Stonesfield](https://archive.org/details/biostor-126641)" to the society. The bones came from Stonesfield, about twelve miles northwest of Oxford, and the teeth, he wrote, showed the creature "to have belonged to the order of Saurians or Lizards."

The name was not new. Buckland [chose it](https://archive.org/details/biostor-126641) "in concurrence with my friend and fellow-labourer, the Rev. W. Conybeare," and it had already reached print in James Parkinson's [*Outlines of Oryctology*](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_Fc8QAAAAIAAJ/page/n311/mode/1up) of 1822, which lists Megalosaurus as an animal "not yet described." Parkinson [added](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_Fc8QAAAAIAAJ/page/n311/mode/1up) that "it is hoped a description may shortly be given to the public," and Buckland's paper supplied it.

The Natural History Museum's [Dino Directory](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/dino-directory/megalosaurus.html) dates the name to 1824 and calls Megalosaurus "the first dinosaur in history given a name," one it got "before the word 'dinosaur' had even been invented." Megalosaurus lived about [166 million years ago](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/dino-directory/megalosaurus.html).

Gideon Mantell followed in **1825**, describing Iguanodon in the Royal Society's [Philosophical Transactions](https://archive.org/details/jstor-107739) from teeth found in Tilgate Forest, Sussex. The name, he wrote, was "derived from the form of the teeth," which he compared with an iguana's.

## Owen's 1841 speech apparently never mentioned dinosaurs

Richard Owen spoke on British fossil reptiles to the British Association at Plymouth on **August 2, 1841**: *The Athenaeum* [places his paper](https://archive.org/details/sim_athenaeum-uk_1841-08-21_721/page/n16/mode/1up) in its report of that [day's sessions](https://archive.org/details/sim_athenaeum-uk_1841-08-21_721/page/n12/mode/1up). The meeting itself had opened on [July 29](https://archive.org/details/sim_athenaeum-uk_1841-08-07_719/page/n9/mode/1up), and the printed report appeared in **1842**, in a volume whose title page reads ["held at Plymouth in July 1841."](https://archive.org/details/reportofeleventh42lond)

It "has always been assumed that Owen used the term 'dinosaur' when he delivered his paper," the Linda Hall Library notes in its [Paper Dinosaurs exhibition](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/paper-dinosaurs/03-early-restorations/04-the-word-dinosaur-is-coined-1842/), and in 1991 England held "numerous celebrations" of the dinosaur's 150th anniversary. In fact, "Owen made no mention of dinosaurs in his presented paper"; he invented the group while writing it up for print. Sources that give 1841 are dating the meeting, and the word first appeared in the 1842 report.

## Dinosaur means "fearfully great lizard"

On [page 103](https://archive.org/details/reportofeleventh42lond/page/n142/mode/1up) of that report, Owen argued for "a distinct tribe or sub-order of Saurian Reptiles, for which I would propose the name of Dinosauria." A footnote glosses the Greek deinos as "fearfully great" and sauros as "a lizard."

The name is often translated "terrible lizard," but Owen was pointing at size: his animals were ["far surpassing in size the largest of existing reptiles."](https://archive.org/details/reportofeleventh42lond/page/n142/mode/1up) The group rested on three genera, Megalosaurus, Hylaeosaurus and Iguanodon, which he called "the gigantic Crocodile-lizards of the dry land."

One reason Plot gave up on the elephant was that even a young one, "not half grown," had bigger bones than his fossil. In 1824 the elephant came back: Cuvier, Buckland [reported](https://archive.org/details/biostor-126641), had worked out from a Megalosaurus thigh bone "two feet nine inches long" an animal of "a length exceeding 40 feet" with the bulk of an elephant seven feet high.
