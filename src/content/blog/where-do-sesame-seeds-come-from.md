You can eat sesame on bagels for years without seeing the plant or the grooved pods that hold its seeds. [NC State Extension's 2026 fact sheet for growers](https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/sesame-production-fact-sheet) sums up their defining habit: "In nature, sesame capsules shatter once seed are mature."

Machines could not harvest a crop like that without losing much of it. [A 2002 history of the US breeding effort](https://hort.purdue.edu/newcrop/ncnu02/v5-157.html) traces how a mutant with pods that stayed shut turned up in 1943 and proved a dead end, and how one company, Sesaco, released its first mechanized varieties in 1982.

## Where do sesame seeds come from?

Sesame seeds come from *Sesamum indicum*, a flowering herb farmed for its oil for thousands of years, mostly in Africa and Asia. Each bell-shaped flower can become [a grooved capsule](https://hort.purdue.edu/newcrop/afcm/sesame.html), the plant's fruit, 1 to 3 inches long and holding 50 to 100 or more seeds. Left to itself, the ripe pod splits open and spills them.

## Each flower lasts a day and can leave a pod

The plant grows 2 to 8 feet tall and is, in [NC State's](https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/sesame-production-fact-sheet) words, "the only agronomic crop from the family Pedaliaceae." It flowers from as early as 30 days after planting until about day 75 to 80, but each flower is open for one day and then drops. The seeds are small: NC State counts roughly 150,000 in a pound.

Growth is indeterminate: new flowers keep opening at the top while older pods mature below, so the lower pods ripen before the upper ones. [NC State tells growers](https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/sesame-production-fact-sheet) the goal is mature seed at the top of the plant, "however, this is not always possible," and that waiting longer risks "seed loss from opened capsules."

## Ripe pods spill, so the crop was cut by hand

A 1990 [crop manual](https://hort.purdue.edu/newcrop/afcm/sesame.html) from the Universities of Wisconsin and Minnesota spells out the consequence: "Because of this shattering characteristic, sesame has been grown primarily on small plots that are harvested by hand."

Shattering varieties, [the manual explains](https://hort.purdue.edu/newcrop/afcm/sesame.html), are usually "swathed green", stood upright in small shocks of about eight bundles, and threshed around two weeks later. A [2024 review in the journal Plants](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11434663/) notes that farmers "tend to harvest manually with partial maturity" to limit the losses, which a [2022 review in Frontiers in Genetics](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9763032/) puts at **up to 50% of the yield**.

## The 1943 mutant held its seed too well

In **1943**, [in Venezuela](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9763032/), D.G. Langham found a mutant sesame plant whose pods did not split, a trait controlled by a single recessive gene, *id*, and in the initial *id/id* lines "the capsules were so tough that it took a hammer to open the capsules," [the 2002 paper](https://hort.purdue.edu/newcrop/ncnu02/v5-157.html) records. Combines modified to break such pods open damaged the seed.

Because of side effects that included "reduced yields due to poor seed set and susceptibility to diseases," the gene "was not incorporated successfully into any commercial variety, despite intensive efforts," A. Ashri wrote in a [2001 International Atomic Energy Agency report](https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/Publications/PDF/te_1195_prn.pdf). A US company, Sesaco, did release an *id* variety, Sesaco 01, in **1982**, but D.R. Langham's chapter of [the same report](https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/Publications/PDF/te_1195_prn.pdf) says its seed "deteriorated quickly due to combine damage."

The sesame that suited machines came from separate breeding that did not use the gene. Its first variety, Sesaco 02, came out that same year and could be swathed, left to dry in the field and picked up by a combine with "No manual labor" required, [the chapter](https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/Publications/PDF/te_1195_prn.pdf) records.

It still leaked. [Langham and Wiemers](https://hort.purdue.edu/newcrop/ncnu02/v5-157.html) write that Sesaco 02 lost about **30%** of its seed with no rain and as much as **60%** with a little rain on the drying windrow, while later varieties lose less than 5% in most fields under normal conditions. The fix combined six capsule traits, starting with a pod built to open at the tip: too open and seed falls out, too tight and mold can form inside. The open tip also lets moisture escape, so the seed dries down faster.

| | Shattering sesame | Shatter-resistant (modern US) |
|---|---|---|
| Ripe pods | Split and release the seed | Stay mostly shut |
| Harvest | Cut green, stood in shocks, threshed | Combined directly |
| The catch | Seed losses of up to 50% | A late harvest still loses seed |

> The sesame bred for combines was meant to open at the tip, because a pod that barely opens can hold back its seed or grow mold. NC State warns that these lines "are not truly shatter resistant: capsules will open slightly at the top along the seams."

## Texas and Oklahoma grow most of America's sesame

The [Agricultural Marketing Resource Center](https://www.agmrc.org/commodities-products/grains-oilseeds/sesame-profile) (AgMRC) notes that Thomas Jefferson grew sesame in test plots more than 200 years ago, and that he called it beni or benne, which AgMRC describes as "the name used in Africa."

[AgMRC says](https://www.agmrc.org/commodities-products/grains-oilseeds/sesame-profile) US commercial production began in the 1950s and credits the 1943 mutant for it, but D.R. Langham's [chapter](https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/Publications/PDF/te_1195_prn.pdf) says "few commercial acres were grown with ID varieties" and that shattering lines bred by M.L. Kinman "were the majority of varieties grown in Texas and New Mexico in the late 1950s and early 1960s." [Langham and Wiemers](https://hort.purdue.edu/newcrop/ncnu02/v5-157.html) write that US sesame in the 1950s and 1960s was harvested by "cutting, binding, shocking, and combining," and that "when the hand labor from Mexico became unavailable, the sesame crop disappeared."

Drawing on the 2017 Census of Agriculture, [AgMRC](https://www.agmrc.org/commodities-products/grains-oilseeds/sesame-profile) puts almost all US commercial production in Texas and Oklahoma, with limited acreage in Kansas and Florida; the US grew 31 million pounds in 2017 and imported about twice that.

## Scholars still argue over where it was first farmed

A [July 2026 genome study in Plant Biotechnology Journal](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13398907/) sets out the traditional case for South Asia: the earliest carbonised seeds come from Harappan sites in Pakistan and [northwestern India](https://www.currentscience.ac.in/Volumes/100/12/1833.pdf), and a wild subspecies native to South Asia, malabaricum, closely resembles modern sesame.

The study's own case for Africa rests on wild relatives and genes. [It notes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13398907/) that Africa has 18 unique wild *Sesamum* species to South Asia's eight, and its analysis of 927 accessions from 14 regions supports "East Africa as the center of origin."

The authors stop short of a verdict: Africa "may have been a critical center of diversification, if not the primary domestication site," and "the exact timing and location of domestication events remain ambiguous without ancient DNA evidence," [they write](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13398907/).

[NC State](https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/sesame-production-fact-sheet) splits the difference, saying sesame "likely originated in sub-Saharan Africa but was domesticated in India." Wherever it began, Africa and Asia grew **96.3%** of the world's sesame in 2021, by the FAOSTAT figures [the genome study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13398907/) cites, and [AgMRC's 2022 profile](https://www.agmrc.org/commodities-products/grains-oilseeds/sesame-profile) names Sudan, India, Myanmar and Tanzania as the largest producers.

## The name is built on a Mesopotamian word for oil

[Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/sesame) traces "sesame" probably to Latin and from there to Greek, which borrowed it very early, via Phoenician, from a reconstructed Late Babylonian form; it compares the Assyrian *shamash-shammu*, literally "oil-seed". At [about 50% oil](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11434663/), the seed still earns the name.

## The pod story behind open sesame is unverified

The popular explanation for "open sesame" is that the password fits because ripe pods open by themselves. The explanation reached [the 1990 crop manual](https://hort.purdue.edu/newcrop/afcm/sesame.html), which says that "upon ripening, sesame capsules split, releasing the seed (hence the phrase, 'open sesame')."

The password belongs to the tale of Ali Baba, and [the earliest known text of the tale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_Baba_and_the_Forty_Thieves) is Antoine Galland's French *Les Mille et Une Nuits* (1704-1717). In English, [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/sesame) dates the magic word to a 1722 translation of Galland.

[An 1806 Paris printing of Galland's text](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Les_Mille_et_Une_Nuits/Histoire_d%E2%80%99Ali_Baba) never mentions a pod. Cassim, Ali Baba's brother, forgets the word inside the cave. He tries "Orge, ouvre-toi" ("Barley, open up") and then, in Galland's phrase, "plusieurs autres noms de grains", several other names of grains. The cave stays shut, and the thieves kill him.

Inside the story, sesame is one grain name among several. The pod explanation may be right, but nothing in the tale supports it and the manual cites no source, which leaves it a plausible guess.

In Galland's tale the cave opens only for the right word. A sesame pod opens when it ripens, so the harvest, by hand or by combine, is still a race to cut the plant before its pods let go. Strictly FYI.
