You can eat sesame on bagels for years without seeing the plant or the grooved pods that hold its seeds. [NC State Extension's 2026 fact sheet for growers](https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/sesame-production-fact-sheet) sums up their defining habit: "In nature, sesame capsules shatter once seed are mature."

Machines could not harvest a crop like that without losing much of it. A mutant with pods that stayed shut turned up in **1943** and proved a dead end. The first US sesame that could be harvested with no hand labor came in **1982**.

## Where do sesame seeds come from?

Sesame seeds come from *Sesamum indicum*, a flowering herb farmed for its oil for thousands of years, mostly in Africa and Asia. Each bell-shaped flower can become a grooved pod 1 to 3 inches long holding **50 to 100 or more seeds**. Left to itself, the ripe pod splits open and spills them.

## Each flower lasts a day and can leave a pod

The plant grows **2 to 8 feet** tall and is, in NC State's words, "the only agronomic crop from the family Pedaliaceae." It flowers from as early as 30 days after planting until about day 75 to 80, but each flower is open for **one day** and then drops. The seeds are small: NC State counts roughly **150,000 in a pound**.

Growth is indeterminate: new flowers keep opening at the top while older pods mature below, so **the lowest pods ripen first**. NC State tells growers the goal is mature seed at the top of the plant, "however, this is not always possible," and that waiting longer risks "seed loss from opened capsules."

## Ripe pods spill, so the crop was cut by hand

A 1990 [crop manual](https://hort.purdue.edu/newcrop/afcm/sesame.html) from the Universities of Wisconsin and Minnesota spells out the consequence: "Because of this shattering characteristic, sesame has been grown primarily on small plots that are harvested by hand." Ceylon cinnamon is harvested by hand as well, [peeled from the stem in Sri Lanka](/blog/how-is-cinnamon-made/) after a brass rod loosens the inner bark from the wood.

Shattering varieties are usually "swathed green", stood upright in small shocks of about eight bundles, and threshed around two weeks later. A [2024 review in the journal Plants](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11434663/) notes that farmers "tend to harvest manually with partial maturity" to limit the losses, which a [2022 review in Frontiers in Genetics](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9763032/) puts at **up to 50% of the yield**.

## The 1943 mutant held its seed too well

In **1943**, in Venezuela, D.G. Langham found a mutant sesame plant whose pods did not split, a trait controlled by a single recessive gene, *id*. In the first *id* lines, "the capsules were so tough that it took a hammer to open the capsules," a [2002 paper on mechanizing US sesame](https://hort.purdue.edu/newcrop/ncnu02/v5-157.html) records. Combines modified to break such pods open damaged the seed.

With its low yields, the gene "was not incorporated successfully into any commercial variety, despite intensive efforts," A. Ashri wrote in a [2001 International Atomic Energy Agency report](https://www-pub.iaea.org/MTCD/Publications/PDF/te_1195_prn.pdf). The sesame that suited machines came from separate breeding that did not use the gene. Its first variety, in **1982**, could be swathed, left to dry in the field and picked up by a combine with "no manual labor," the IAEA report records.

| | Shattering sesame | Shatter-resistant (modern US) |
|---|---|---|
| Ripe pods | Split and release the seed | Stay mostly shut |
| Harvest | Cut green, stood in shocks, threshed | Combined directly |
| The catch | Seed losses of up to 50% | A late harvest still loses seed |

> The sesame bred for combines was meant to open at the tip, because a pod that barely opens can hold back its seed or grow mold. NC State warns that these lines "are not truly shatter resistant: capsules will open slightly at the top along the seams."

## Texas and Oklahoma grow most of America's sesame

The [Agricultural Marketing Resource Center](https://www.agmrc.org/commodities-products/grains-oilseeds/sesame-profile) (AgMRC) notes that Thomas Jefferson grew sesame in test plots more than 200 years ago, calling it "beni or benne, the name used in Africa." AgMRC dates the first US commercial production to the 1950s, but that crop was mostly shattering sesame shocked by hand, and "when the hand labor from Mexico became unavailable, the sesame crop disappeared," the 2002 paper says.

Drawing on the 2017 Census of Agriculture, AgMRC puts almost all US commercial production in **Texas and Oklahoma**, and NC State adds parts of Kansas. The US grew **31 million pounds** in 2017 and imported about twice that.

## Scholars still argue over where it was first farmed

A [July 2026 genome study in Plant Biotechnology Journal](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13398907/) sets out the traditional case for South Asia: the earliest charred seeds come from Harappan-era sites in Pakistan and northwestern India, and a wild subspecies native to South Asia, malabaricum, closely resembles modern sesame.

The study's own case for Africa rests on wild relatives and genes. It notes that Africa has **18** wild *Sesamum* species to South Asia's eight, and its analysis of **927** samples from 14 regions supports "East Africa as the center of origin".

The authors do not claim to have settled it: "the exact timing and location of domestication events remain ambiguous without ancient DNA evidence."

NC State splits the difference, saying sesame "likely originated in sub-Saharan Africa but was domesticated in India." Wherever it began, Africa and Asia grew **96.3%** of the world's sesame in 2021, by the FAOSTAT figures the genome study cites. AgMRC's 2022 profile names Sudan, India, Myanmar and Tanzania as the largest producers.

## The name is built on a Mesopotamian word for oil

[Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/sesame) traces "sesame" through Latin, Greek and Phoenician to a reconstructed Late Babylonian form, and compares the Assyrian *shamash-shammu*, literally "oil-seed". [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/sesame) reads the Akkadian source word as a compound of "oil" and "plant". At about 50% oil, the seed still earns the name, as does flax, the oilseed pressed for [the linseed oil in homemade barn finishes](/blog/why-barns-are-red/).

## The pod story behind open sesame is unverified

The popular explanation for "open sesame" is that the password fits because ripe pods open by themselves. Food words attract tidy origin stories, like [the American soldiers in Belgium who supposedly named french fries](/blog/why-are-french-fries-called-french-fries/), and this one reached a university crop manual, which says that "upon ripening, sesame capsules split, releasing the seed (hence the phrase, 'open sesame')."

The phrase first appears in Antoine Galland's French *Mille et une nuits* (1704-1717), and [the earliest known text of the tale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_Baba_and_the_Forty_Thieves) is his.

[That text](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Les_Mille_et_Une_Nuits/Histoire_d%E2%80%99Ali_Baba) never mentions a pod. Cassim, Ali Baba's brother, forgets the word inside the cave. He tries "Orge, ouvre-toi" ("Barley, open up") and then, in Galland's phrase, "plusieurs autres noms de grains", several other names of grains. The cave stays shut, and the thieves kill him.

Inside the story, **sesame is one grain name among several**. The pod explanation may be right, but nothing in the tale supports it and the manual cites no source, which leaves it a plausible guess.

In Galland's tale the cave opens only for the right word. A sesame pod opens when it ripens, so the harvest, by hand or by combine, is still a race to cut the plant before its pods let go. Strictly FYI.
