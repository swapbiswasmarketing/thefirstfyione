C.V. Raman carried a Nicol prism [in his pocket](https://www.optica-opn.org/home/articles/volume_20/issue_3/features/c_v_raman_and_the_raman_effect/) when he sailed home from Europe in September 1921, and from the deck of the [SS Narkunda](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/raman-sea) he turned it on the sea. Held at the correct angle to the water and turned the right way, the prism [quenches the sky's reflection](https://www.optica-opn.org/home/articles/volume_20/issue_3/features/c_v_raman_and_the_raman_effect/) on the surface.

Lord Rayleigh, the physicist who had [explained why the sky is blue](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/raman-sea), had [written in 1910](https://archive.org/details/scientificpapers05rayliala/page/540/) that the deep sea's dark blue "has nothing to do with the colour of water, but is simply the blue of the sky seen by reflection". On that account, removing the reflection should have removed the blue.

Looking through the prism at ["the deeper waters of the Mediterranean and Red Seas"](https://archive.org/details/sim_nature-uk_1921-11-17_108_2716), Raman found the colour not dimmed but "wonderfully improved". It was, he told [Nature in a letter dated 26 September from Bombay Harbour and printed on **17 November 1921**](https://archive.org/details/sim_nature-uk_1921-11-17_108_2716), "a distinct phenomenon in itself, and not merely an effect due to reflected sky-light".

## Why is the ocean blue?

[The ocean is blue because water absorbs red light](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/oceanblue.html). Water molecules vibrate, and high overtones of those vibrations absorb the red end of sunlight, so every metre of water removes more red than blue. Scattering, by the water itself and by particles in it, turns some of that filtered light back toward the surface, and it arrives blue.

Reflected sky adds to the view from above, but it cannot explain the blue underwater, where, as [Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution](https://www.whoi.edu/ocean-learning-hub/ocean-facts/why-is-the-ocean-blue/) notes, the colour remains with no sky to reflect.

## Red light is spent in the first few metres

Pure water absorbs so little blue light that measuring it is hard: near the minimum, light "can travel more than 100 meters with little absorption", [Physics Today](https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/shedding-new-light-on-light-in-the-ocean) noted in 2011, crediting Edward Fry's group at Texas A&M University with the most accurate measurements there to date.

In a [1997 paper](https://omlc.org/spectra/water/data/pope97.txt), Pope and Fry reported pure water's absorption from 380 to 700 nm. Within that range they put the minimum at **418 nm**, near the violet end of the spectrum, at [0.0044 per metre](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18264420/). At 700 nm, deep red, [their data](https://omlc.org/spectra/water/data/pope97.txt) give 0.624 per metre, **about 140 times** stronger. In 2016 Mason, Cone and Fry [reported an even lower minimum, in the ultraviolet at 344 nm](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27607297/).

| Light | Wavelength | Absorbed in 1 m | Left after 10 m |
|---|---|---|---|
| Violet | 418 nm | 0.4% | 96% |
| Blue | 450 nm | 0.9% | 91% |
| Green | 550 nm | 5.5% | 57% |
| Red | 650 nm | 29% | 3% |
| Deep red | 700 nm | 46% | 0.2% |

Percentages are calculated from Pope and Fry's coefficients for pure water.

A glass of water looks clear because its path is too short: by [the same data](https://omlc.org/spectra/water/data/pope97.txt), a 10 cm glass loses only about 3 percent of its red light at 650 nm.

## Water's blue comes from vibration, a rare source of colour

Water's O-H bonds vibrate at infrared wavelengths near 3 micrometres, [Physics Today](https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/shedding-new-light-on-light-in-the-ocean) says, far too low in energy to absorb visible light one quantum at a time. Absorbing red takes an overtone, several quanta of stretching at once.

In [their 1993 paper, "Why is water blue?"](https://www.webexhibits.org/causesofcolor/ref/Braun-Smirnov.html), Charles Braun and Sergei Smirnov, chemists at Dartmouth College, placed one four-quantum overtone in water vapour at **698 nm**, "just at the red edge of the visible spectrum", and noted that each extra quantum makes a transition 10 to 20 times weaker, so only overtones of four and five quanta matter for the colour. Hydrogen bonding in the liquid shifts the bands to lower energy and longer wavelengths, so [in their spectrum](https://www.webexhibits.org/causesofcolor/ref/Braun-Smirnov.html) liquid water's visible absorption is the short-wavelength tail of a band centred at **760 nm**, plus weaker bands at 660 and 605 nm. That faintness is why the colour needs depth to show, and why bluer light, which would take still more quanta, is barely absorbed at all.

> "To our knowledge the intrinsic blueness of water is the only example from nature in which color originates from vibrational transitions," Braun and Smirnov wrote.

[Physics Today](https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/shedding-new-light-on-light-in-the-ocean) is more guarded, calling electron interactions "the primary determinants of color in almost all other substances".

## Heavier hydrogen takes the colour out of water

Swap ordinary hydrogen for deuterium, its heavier isotope, and you have heavy water. In Braun and Smirnov's [spectrum of it](https://www.webexhibits.org/causesofcolor/ref/Braun-Smirnov.html), the 760 nm band moves to **about 1,000 nm**, in the infrared, where the eye cannot see it: heavy water is colourless, they wrote, "because all of its corresponding vibrational transitions are shifted to lower energy by the increase in isotope mass", at least over paths on the order of metres.

Their eyeball test, a [**3 m** tube](https://www.webexhibits.org/causesofcolor/ref/Braun-Smirnov.html) of purified ordinary water hung over sunlit white paper, had ten or more observers reporting blue; they never filled it with heavy water, because "the large tube volume and a limited budget precluded checking".

## Green, turquoise and brown come from what the water carries

Chlorophyll in phytoplankton absorbs blue and red, so as their numbers rise the sea sends back less blue relative to green, Physics Today explains, and [NASA's Earth Observatory](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/what-are-phytoplankton/) says blooms can turn water "greenish, reddish, or brownish". Silt and sand turn it brown, and clear shallows over sand look turquoise because light reaches the bottom and bounces back, according to [Woods Hole](https://www.whoi.edu/ocean-learning-hub/ocean-facts/why-is-the-ocean-blue/).

The clearest water leans toward violet, the visible colour pure water absorbs least: [Physics Today](https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/shedding-new-light-on-light-in-the-ocean) reports that "in some very clear, clean locations, most notably near Easter Island in the South Pacific Gyre, the water appears almost purple".

## Rayleigh's own version was subtler than the myth

As the whole answer, reflected sky is a myth, but the slogan flattens what Rayleigh actually wrote. His [1910 piece](https://archive.org/details/scientificpapers05rayliala/page/540/), written after a "voyage round Africa", granted water an "absorption, or proper, colour", but argued that in clear, deep ocean "there is often nothing to send the light back to the observer", and that under cloud the sea looks "grey and leaden".

He met the obvious objection, that the deep sea "often looks purer and fuller than that of the sky", by saying [we compare it](https://archive.org/details/scientificpapers05rayliala/page/540/) with the sky near the horizon, "whereas the best blue comes from near the zenith". He even conceded that at Aden and Suez the sea had looked blue ["in a manner not explicable by reflection of the sky"](https://archive.org/details/scientificpapers05rayliala/page/541/).

Raman answered both points with his prism. Under a completely overcast sky, [he reported](https://archive.org/details/sim_nature-uk_1921-11-17_108_2716), the water was "a deeper and fuller blue than ever, but of greatly enfeebled intensity", so a leaden sea meant clouds screening the sun rather than missing sky-light. With the reflections gone, the hue was ["of such fullness and saturation that the bluest sky in comparison with it seems a dull grey"](https://archive.org/details/sim_nature-uk_1921-11-17_108_2716).

Rayleigh never read the reply: he had died on [30 June 1919](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1904/strutt/facts/).

## Raman's scattering was half the answer

The other common answer, that the sea is blue for the same reason as the sky, gets one ingredient right. That was Raman's own explanation: it "seemed not unlikely", he told [his Nobel audience](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/raman-lecture.pdf), "that the phenomenon owed its origin to the scattering of sunlight by the molecules of the water".

Molecular scattering does favour blue, in water as in air, but on its own it is not the answer. Physics Today credits a complete explanation to Vasily Shuleikin, who combined Raman's molecular scattering with older ideas; his [**1923** paper](https://journals.aps.org/pr/abstract/10.1103/PhysRev.22.85) set the absorption Robert Bunsen had argued for beside scattering by fine particles and bubbles, reflection from larger particles, and reflected sky.

The scattering work still led Raman, in **1928**, to the effect named after him, in which a small fraction of scattered light emerges at a changed wavelength, and to the [1930 Nobel Prize in Physics](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1930/raman/facts/). The first section of [his Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/raman-lecture.pdf) is headed "The colour of the sea".

In water, the effect that carries his name comes from vibrations of the water molecule, Physics Today notes, the same motions whose overtones take the red out of sunlight. Raman went looking for a scattering explanation of the blue and found a scattering effect driven by the vibrations that cause it. Strictly FYI.
