Boston Public Schools, School Document No. 16, printed in 1916. Among the suggestions in that year's third-grade syllabus sits a line about how a sentence may open: "Teach the elimination of 'but,' 'so,' 'and,' 'because,' at the beginning of a sentence, or in stringing sentences together as one long sentence" ([the scanned syllabus](https://archive.org/details/schooldocument1915bost)).

That is a city school board handing a writing rule to its third-grade teachers, and the second half of the line aims the same four words at run-on sentences. Strunk's *Elements of Style* and the Constitution both settle the question against the rule.

## Can you start a sentence with but?

Yes. [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/words-to-not-begin-sentences-with) answers that it has never been wrong to begin a sentence with and or but, that English writers have done it from the 9th-century Old English Chronicle to the present day, and it collects six schoolroom bans on sentence-opening words dated from 1826 to 1929. The usage writer [Bryan A. Garner](https://www.lawprose.org/lawprose-lesson-180-conjunctions-as-sentence-starters-2/) puts a number on the habit: "From at least the time of Chaucer, expert writers have tended to begin 10-20% of their sentences with conjunctions."

Shakespeare did it too. In [Romeo and Juliet](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1513), Romeo's second line in the garden scene is "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks?"

## The ban has a paper trail, and it is made of schoolbooks

Five of those six bans, each naming words a sentence may not lead with, are below, linked to the scanned original where one is public:

| Year | Source | The instruction |
|---|---|---|
| 1826 | James Brown, *The American System of English Grammar*, [quoted by Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/words-to-not-begin-sentences-with) | "Or never begins a sentence, paragraph, or chapter." |
| 1854 | George Payn Quackenbos, [*An Advanced Course of Composition and Rhetoric*](https://archive.org/details/advancedcourseof0000quac) | "A sentence should not commence with the conjunctions and, for, or however; but may do so with but, now, and moreover." |
| 1908 | George Hitchcock, [*Sermon Composition*](https://archive.org/details/sermoncompositio00hitc) | "Do not begin a sentence with 'also' or 'likewise,' and do not end one with a preposition." |
| 1916 | Boston Public Schools, [*Syllabus for the Elementary Schools, Third Grade*](https://archive.org/details/schooldocument1915bost) | "Teach the elimination of 'but,' 'so,' 'and,' 'because,' at the beginning of a sentence, or in stringing sentences together as one long sentence." |
| 1929 | J. C. Tressler, [*English in Action*, Book Two](https://archive.org/details/englishinactionb0002jctr) | "Do not begin a sentence with however or a similar unimportant word." |

Only the Boston syllabus bans **but**; Quackenbos names it only to allow it. The others police *or*, *and*, *for*, *however*, *also* and *likewise*, in whatever combination the author disliked, and [Hitchcock's 1908 handbook for student preachers](https://archive.org/details/sermoncompositio00hitc) bans two of them in the same breath as the preposition at the end of a sentence.

## The 1854 rule against and made room for but

Quackenbos is the surprise in the list. The full sentence in [his Advanced Course of Composition and Rhetoric](https://archive.org/details/advancedcourseof0000quac), whose preface is dated 1854, is offered as a general rule with occasional exceptions, and it ends by saying a sentence "may do so with but, now, and moreover." **Quackenbos's 1854 schoolbook banned and, for and however at the head of a sentence, and explicitly allowed but.**

His rule came out of [a lesson on the period](https://archive.org/details/advancedcourseof0000quac). A conjunction that closely ties a sentence to the one before, he wrote, should not stand after a period, and he named the translation of the Scriptures, where verse after verse begins with and, as the place this is frequently done. A word that only carries the narrative on, he added, may open a new sentence without impropriety.

## The rule is not in The Elements of Style

Strunk's book carries his copyright dates of **1918 and 1919**, and [Project Gutenberg ebook 37134](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/37134) is the full public-domain text of the 1920 Harcourt, Brace edition. It contains **no rule against opening a sentence with but** or with and.

There is a "But" entry, and [the Gutenberg text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37134/pg37134-images.html) shows it covering three other things: but is "Unnecessary after doubt and help," its "too frequent use" produces the loose sentences of Rule 14, and "Particularly awkward is the following of one but by another, making a contrast to a contrast or a reservation to a reservation." Sentence position never comes up.

The one opening word Strunk does police is **however**. In its [usage entry](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37134/pg37134-images.html), however in the meaning nevertheless is "not to come first in its sentence or clause," and his model rewrites "However, we at last succeeded in reaching camp" as "At last, however, we succeeded in reaching camp."

> Strunk's Elements of Style tells writers to move a sentence-opening however, and leaves a sentence-opening but alone.

## Strunk opens 15 of his own sentences with But

Count the sentences that open with *But* in [the Gutenberg ebook](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/37134), leave out the transcriber's notes, one example sentence and two exercise items, and **Strunk's own instructional prose begins 15 sentences with the word**.

Two of them, from [Strunk's text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37134/pg37134-images.html): "But a writer should be careful not to construct too many of his sentences after this pattern (see Rule 14)," and, on how paragraphs open, "But this device, if too often used, would become a mannerism." The example sentence is his specimen of the doubled but, whose second but opens a sentence; his repair deletes it, because the fault he names is the contrast to a contrast.

The 1959 Strunk and White keeps the habit, running two consecutive sentences this way in [the Macmillan text](https://archive.org/details/elementsofstylestru00stru): "But since writing is communication, clarity can only be a virtue. And although there is no substitute for merit in writing, clarity comes closest to being one."

## The Constitution opens three sentences with But, and its amendments six more

The [National Archives transcript](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript) of the original text has **three**. One is in Article I, Section 7, on overriding a veto, which begins "But in all such Cases the Votes of both Houses shall be determined by yeas and Nays," and goes on to require that the names of those voting for and against the bill be entered in each House's journal. The other two sit in Article II, Section 1, inside the original machinery for choosing a President.

The [amendments transcript](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/amendments-11-27) adds six: two in the Twelfth, three in the Fourteenth, and one in the Twenty-second, whose two-term limit carries an exception that opens "But this Article shall not apply to any person holding the office of President when this Article was proposed by the Congress," and goes on to let whoever held the office when it took effect serve out that term. [Wayne Schiess](https://sites.utexas.edu/legalwriting/about/), who directs the legal-writing program at the University of Texas School of Law, says seven [on his blog](https://sites.utexas.edu/legalwriting/2020/05/01/beginning-with-but/), and [Garner](https://www.lawprose.org/lawprose-lesson-180-conjunctions-as-sentence-starters-2/) says the Constitution has ten sentence-starting conjunctions; neither lists them.

## Boston's syllabi show the run-on worry

The familiar origin story says teachers banned sentence-initial and to stop children producing endless chains of clauses. [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/words-to-not-begin-sentences-with) reports that its *Dictionary of English Usage* treats this as speculation, and adds that changing how people naturally use the language seems an unlikely fix for run-on writing.

Boston's syllabi never say why, but they put the worry on the page. The second-grade syllabus printed the same year, School Document No. 15, says in its section on spoken English that "Little children have a tendency to talk in a rambling, meaningless fashion," connecting statement after statement with and and but, and adds: "These are bugbears which the second grade teacher must strive to overcome" ([the same archive.org volume](https://archive.org/details/schooldocument1915bost)). The third-grade line names those four words "in stringing sentences together as one long sentence" in [the 1916 document](https://archive.org/details/schooldocument1915bost) itself, though it never says that is why the opening ban exists.

Garner's [lesson on conjunctions](https://www.lawprose.org/lawprose-lesson-180-conjunctions-as-sentence-starters-2/) says your third-grade teacher "wanted to break you of the habit of starting every sentence with" the word and, adding: "That was a bad habit." [Schiess](https://sites.utexas.edu/legalwriting/2020/05/01/beginning-with-but/), whose post opens by saying there is no rule against beginning a sentence with but, allows that it is "a wise admonition from middle-school English teachers that novice writers avoid beginning a series of sentences with but."

## Overuse is the real fault, and Strunk named it

The caution in Strunk that comes nearest to a rule is about frequency. **Rule 14, "Avoid a succession of loose sentences,"** describes the writer who builds a whole paragraph from two-clause sentences joined by *and*, *but* and *so*, and warns in [the Gutenberg text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37134/pg37134-images.html) that a series of them "soon becomes monotonous and tedious."

Formality is the other live question. [Schiess](https://sites.utexas.edu/legalwriting/2016/11/11/want-to-begin-with-but-no-comma-needed/) tells lawyers who find a sentence-initial but too informal that they are "missing out on a vigorous, forceful transition word." He quotes *The Chicago Manual of Style* calling but "a perfectly proper word to open a sentence," and [lists Supreme Court opinions](https://sites.utexas.edu/legalwriting/2020/05/01/beginning-with-but/) from 1793 to 2013 with sentences that open with it, among them "But this case has nothing to do with federalism."

Where a supervisor, a journal or a house style sheet forbids it, that is a local instruction of the 1916 Boston kind, worth obeying in that building without mistaking it for the language.

## No comma after it, and however is no safer

[Schiess](https://sites.utexas.edu/legalwriting/2016/11/11/want-to-begin-with-but-no-comma-needed/) is blunt about the punctuation: if you begin a sentence with but, "you need no comma after it," and the one exception is a comma required for some other reason, as in "But, the plaintiff later realized, the money never went to her sister."

The usual escape route has its own history. [Tressler's 1929 schoolbook](https://archive.org/details/englishinactionb0002jctr) banned an opening however as an unimportant word, and [Schiess](https://sites.utexas.edu/legalwriting/2020/05/01/beginning-with-but/), who says a sentence may begin with however too, finds that "However, this case has nothing to do with federalism" "contrasts but moves more slowly."

Boston's line was an instruction about what to teach third-graders, and the rule outlived it by being mistaken for a fact about English. Strunk never wrote the rule down, began fifteen of his own sentences with But, and saved his only ban on a single opening word for however. Strictly FYI.
