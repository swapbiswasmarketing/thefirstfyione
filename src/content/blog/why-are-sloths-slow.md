Wild brown-throated three-toed sloths sleep **9.63 hours a day**, according to [miniaturised EEG recorders fitted to sloths living free in a rainforest canopy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18482903/). That leaves a sloth awake for roughly fourteen hours of every day, and it still moves at the speed of a sloth.

The slowness therefore has to be explained by something other than sleep. Measurements point to a tiny energy budget, a stomach that holds each meal for days, and forelimb muscle built for hanging. Eyes that lack working cones may add a constraint of their own.

## Why are sloths slow?

Sloths are slow because leaves pay badly. A three-toed sloth has [the lowest FMR, or field metabolic rate, recorded for any mammal](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27420784/), and a captive brown-throated sloth's gut [holds food particles for a mean of 181 to 261 hours](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35468240/), depending on the diet. Slow movement is what that budget allows, and the eyes may add a limit of their own.

## Three-toed sloths have the lowest field metabolic rate on record

Field metabolic rate is the energy an animal actually spends living its life, rather than resting in a lab. Jonathan Pauli and colleagues measured it in two-toed and three-toed sloths living side by side and, writing in The American Naturalist in 2016, [put three-toed sloths at **162 kJ per day per kg^0.734**](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27420784/), the lowest FMR recorded for any mammal. The odd exponent is allometric scaling, a correction that lets animals of very different body sizes be compared fairly.

They then [compared field and basal metabolic rates across 19 species of arboreal folivores](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27420784/) along a spectrum of specialisation. The whole group ran lower basal and field rates than other mammals, and the more specialised the species, the lower its field rate went, while basal rate held steady. The saving came from thermoregulation and behaviour rather than from a smaller resting engine.

Temperature does part of that. Three-toed sloths [were more heterothermic and moved less than two-toed sloths](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27420784/), meaning core temperature drifts with conditions instead of being defended at a mammal's usual fixed setting. For the southern two-toed sloth, the [Smithsonian's National Zoo gives a body temperature of 30 to 34 C](https://nationalzoo.si.edu/animals/southern-two-toed-sloth) that falls during cooler hours of the night, in wet weather and whenever the animal is inactive. With muscles about half the relative weight of most land mammals', the zoo adds, [sloths cannot afford to keep warm by shivering](https://nationalzoo.si.edu/animals/southern-two-toed-sloth).

## A stomach that ferments for days

Leaves are easy to find and expensive to process, and sloths eat very little of them. Rebecca Cliffe and colleagues followed three captive adult female *Bradypus variegatus* for 158 straight days and [reported a mean intake of **17 g of dry-weight food per kg of sloth per day**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4389270/) in PeerJ in 2015.

Whatever does go in stays. Sloths are foregut fermenters, and Cliffe's paper gives [published passage times for sloths of 157 h to 50 days](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4389270/), against 42, 30 and 54 h for the Javan langur, the red kangaroo and colobine monkeys, which it groups as other foregut fermenters of similar size. A 2022 trial in the [Journal of Animal Physiology and Animal Nutrition](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35468240/) fed four captive brown-throated sloths two leaf-based diets and timed how long a liquid marker and a solid-particle marker took to pass through.

| Diet | Liquid marker | Particle marker |
|---|---|---|
| Mixed leaves, higher intake | 133 h, 0.75% per hour | 181 h, 0.55% per hour |
| Cecropia only, lower intake | 204 h, 0.49% per hour | 261 h, 0.38% per hour |

A [mean particle retention time of 261 h](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35468240/) is close to eleven days. Cliffe's group also found that intake rose with ambient temperature, which [contrasts with other mammals, which tend to show increased levels of food intake in colder conditions](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4389270/). Their proposed mechanism is that a sloth whose core temperature tracks the air runs its gut microbes warmer, so fermentation speeds up and room appears for the next meal.

## Sloth eyes lost their cones long ago

Christopher Emerling and Mark Springer reported in Proceedings of the Royal Society B in 2015 that in the genome of Hoffmann's two-toed sloth, [SWS1, PDE6C, PDE6H, GNGT2, GRK7 and GUCY2F are pseudogenic](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4298209/), inactivated and no longer coding for working proteins, and that PDE6C is broken in the pale-throated three-toed sloth too. The one rod-specific gene with a defect, PDE6B, has a small deletion near its end that the authors think probably leaves it working.

That makes sloths rod monochromats, like armadillos, and a retina with rods and no cones produces what their paper describes as "[colourblindness and low acuity vision in dim-light and blindness in bright-light conditions](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4298209/)." Whether that eyesight slows a sloth down is a hypothesis, not a measurement. The authors suggest that rod monochromacy, together with a skeleton reshaped for digging, "[probably prevented diversification into various locomotory types](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4298209/)" in xenarthrans and "canalized tree sloths to convergently adopt a suspensory posture".

The damage is ancient. Emerling and Springer [estimate PDE6C was inactivated in the common ancestor of sloths and anteaters shortly after that lineage diverged from the armadillos, near the Cretaceous-Palaeogene boundary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4298209/), and argue the loss fits an ancestor that lived underground. If they are right, modern sloths climb trees with a retina shaped by life in a burrow.

## The muscle is built for hanging

[Skeletal muscle mass in sloths is reduced](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29722617/), and a 2018 Journal of Applied Physiology study by Kyle Spainhower and colleagues examined what remains. Across forelimb muscles in five *Bradypus variegatus* and four *Choloepus hoffmanni*, [the main fibre type was the slow MHC-1 isoform](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29722617/), with moderate fast MHC-2A and few hybrid fibres.

The enzyme results went against the team's prediction that the fibres would rely on oxidative pathways. [Creatine kinase and lactate dehydrogenase, both anaerobic enzymes, showed elevated activity, while the aerobic markers citrate synthase and 3-hydroxyacetyl CoA dehydrogenase came out low](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29722617/). The authors concluded that sloth forelimbs [may rely heavily on rapid ATP resynthesis pathways](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29722617/), and that lactate accumulation may even help.

None of that makes a sloth's grip feeble. A [2026 Journal of Anatomy analysis of Hoffmann's two-toed sloth forelimbs](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13398789/) estimated the maximum force of the digital flexors at roughly **2.2 times body weight**, which the authors read as exceptional grip strength. A sloth arm is a clamp with a very long duty cycle.

## Wild three-toed sloths sleep six hours less than captive ones

The [Smithsonian's National Zoo page on the southern two-toed sloth](https://nationalzoo.si.edu/animals/southern-two-toed-sloth) states that sloths are "primarily nocturnal, sleeping for 15 hours during the day and waking up at night to eat." The zoo cites no study, and its page describes a two-toed sloth, a different genus from the three-toed sloths in the studies below. Brown-throated three-toed sloths have been measured: in [the only earlier electrophysiological study of their sleep](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2610152/), captive sloths slept **15.85 hours a day**.

Niels Rattenborg and colleagues took the measurement into the forest. In Biology Letters in 2008 they published [the first EEG recordings of sleep on unrestricted animals in the wild](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18482903/), and the [three adult female brown-throated three-toed sloths they caught in the canopy at Barro Colorado Island, Panama](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2610152/), slept over six hours less than the captive ones.

> The almost-sixteen-hour figure came from captive sloths. When Rattenborg's team fitted wild sloths with recorders and released them at the foot of their trees, six of those hours went away.

The authors declined to overclaim. Juvenile mammals typically sleep longer than adults, they noted, and [the 10 sloths studied in captivity included an unspecified mix of adults and juveniles](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2610152/), which may have pushed the captive average up. A larger study in 2014 recorded nine wild brown-throated sloths on the Panamanian mainland and six pygmy sloths on an island, and found that [both species spent between 9 and 10 h per day sleeping](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24899764/). Whatever the cause, the higher figure describes captive three-toed sloths, not sloths in a forest.

A sloth crossing a branch is doing it on a retina that probably lost its cones in an ancestor shared with anteaters, powered by a stomach still fermenting leaves eaten days earlier. Moving slowly is the strategy that equipment supports, through the fourteen or so hours a day a wild sloth spends awake. Strictly FYI.
