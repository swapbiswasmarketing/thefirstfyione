The astronomers had to wait [ten hours for the sun to go down](https://news.ucsc.edu/2017/10/neutron-star-merger.html). Gravitational waves from two neutron stars spiralling into each other had reached the two LIGO detectors and [Virgo, near Pisa in Italy](https://www.ligo.caltech.edu/news/ligo20171016), earlier that day, 17 August 2017.

After dark, a [UC Santa Cruz team](https://news.ucsc.edu/2017/10/neutron-star-merger.html) turned the one-metre Swope telescope at Las Campanas Observatory in Chile on the likeliest galaxies in the search area. The [ninth field](https://news.ucsc.edu/2017/10/neutron-star-merger.html) held NGC 4993, a galaxy [about 130 million light years away](https://news.berkeley.edu/2017/10/16/astronomers-strike-cosmic-gold/), with a point of light that had not been there four months earlier.

Over the next few days the light turned from blue to red, the colour that [theorists at UC Berkeley](https://news.berkeley.edu/2017/10/16/astronomers-strike-cosmic-gold/) had predicted for freshly made heavy elements. From that glow, [Daniel Kasen and colleagues](https://news.berkeley.edu/2017/10/16/astronomers-strike-cosmic-gold/) estimated the collision's yield at **around 200 Earth masses of gold**.

## How is gold formed?

Gold is formed in space, not in the ground, mostly when rare cataclysms like neutron-star collisions flood atomic nuclei with neutrons faster than they can decay. Almost all Earth's original gold sank into the core. The gold we mine is thought to have come later on meteorites; hot water and erosion gathered it into quartz veins and river gravels.

## Stars cannot fuse their way to gold

The Big Bang left hydrogen, helium and "a scant amount of lithium", in [NASA's words](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/stars/neutron-stars/magnetars/where-does-gold-come-from-nasa-data-has-clues/). Stars forged heavier elements by fusion, but fusion "continues in red supergiants until iron is formed", says [NASA's guide to the elements](https://imagine.gsfc.nasa.gov/educators/elements/imagine/05.html), because "iron releases no energy when fused."

Getting further means adding neutrons. When a nucleus captures one and turns unstable, [a decay](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/stars/neutron-stars/magnetars/where-does-gold-come-from-nasa-data-has-clues/) can convert a neutron into a proton and move the atom one place along the periodic table. Iron has **26 protons** and gold has **79**, as a [2021 PNAS feature](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7848598/) on gold's origins notes, so the climb takes dozens of decays and even more neutron captures.

The [same feature](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7848598/) says most gold forms when "a rapid flux of neutrons bombards iron nuclei", so fast that new neutrons pile in before earlier ones decay. That is the r-process, r for rapid, and it needs a flood of free neutrons.

## The supernova story is out of date

For decades, astronomers thought supernova explosions forged the universe's gold and platinum, yet, as the [PNAS feature](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7848598/) points out, "no one has ever seen a supernova create r-process elements." If supernovae make them at all, only a rare breed does, **under 1 percent** of them, the [feature reports](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7848598/); the Milky Way has about two supernovae a century, and r-process events roughly a thousand times less often.

[Berkeley News](https://news.berkeley.edu/2017/10/16/astronomers-strike-cosmic-gold/) reported in 2017 that, according to co-author Enrico Ramirez-Ruiz, the observations supported the theory that mergers "can account for all the gold in the universe". Then a 2020 model of the galaxy's chemical history by [Chiaki Kobayashi and colleagues](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/09/200915110010.htm) "found enough silver but not enough gold". The team favoured fast-spinning, strongly magnetic supernovae as the main source, yet even with them, Kobayashi said in the [team's release](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/09/200915110010.htm), "gold is under-produced in the model compared with observations."

Mergers also "happen too late in the universe's history to explain the earliest gold", [NASA notes](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/stars/neutron-stars/magnetars/where-does-gold-come-from-nasa-data-has-clues/). In 2025 a [study led by Anirudh Patel](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.09181) added magnetar giant flares, estimating they supply at least about 1 to 10 percent of the galaxy's r-process elements. The evidence was a [previously unexplained gamma-ray signal](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.09181) seen in the aftermath of a December 2004 giant flare. Patel told [NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/stars/neutron-stars/magnetars/where-does-gold-come-from-nasa-data-has-clues/) the question is "a fun puzzle that hasn't actually been solved."

## Almost all of Earth's own gold is in the core

As the planet formed, molten iron sank to its centre to make the core and "took with it the vast majority of the planet's precious metals", says the [University of Bristol](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2011/7885.html): enough precious metal to cover the entire surface in a layer **four metres thick**. Gold alone, geologist Bernard Wood told [ABC Science](https://www.abc.net.au/science/articles/2006/06/15/1663566.htm) in 2006, would cover the land surface half a metre deep.

> More than 99% of Earth's gold lies in the core, under about 3,000 km of solid rock. Wood put the share at "more than 99%" to [ABC Science](https://www.abc.net.au/science/articles/2006/06/15/1663566.htm), and the [University of Goettingen](https://www.uni-goettingen.de/en/3240.html?id=7808) gives the depth as 3,000 km. Every gold ring, coin and circuit board ever made came out of the sliver outside it.

Losing that much should have left the outer Earth "bereft of bling", yet the [Bristol release](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2011/7885.html) says the mantle holds precious metals "tens to thousands of times more abundant" than anticipated.

## Greenland rocks point to a late meteorite shower

[Bristol's team](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2011/7885.html) tested one explanation, a meteorite shower after the core formed, which would leave "a diagnostic mark" on Earth's tungsten isotopes. In 2011 Matthias Willbold, Tim Elliott and Stephen Moorbath compared modern rocks with [Greenland rocks some 3.8 billion years old](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21901010/), whose sources still carry "an isotopic memory" of the time before the proposed meteorite shower.

The old rocks held about 13 parts per million more tungsten-182, relative to tungsten-184, than modern ones, a small difference [their Nature paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21901010/) calls "in good agreement" with the expected influence of a late meteorite shower. Willbold put the delivery at **about 20 billion billion tonnes of asteroidal material**, which the [Bristol release](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2011/7885.html) dates to more than 200 million years after Earth formed.

The core may also be leaking. In 2025 a [Goettingen team](https://www.uni-goettingen.de/en/3240.html?id=7808) found a ruthenium signature from the core in Hawaiian lavas; the data, says Goettingen geochemist Nils Messling, "confirmed that material from the core, including gold and other precious metals, is leaking into the Earth's mantle above." Some of the gold we rely on, the [university says](https://www.uni-goettingen.de/en/3240.html?id=7808), "may have come from the Earth's core", though Messling adds that whether the leak also ran in the past "remains to be proven."

## Earth gathers gold and never makes it

Changing an element means changing its nucleus, and as [CERN](https://home.cern/news/news/physics/alice-detects-conversion-lead-gold-lhc) puts it, "chemical methods are powerless to transmute" lead into gold. Physics can do it: [CERN's ALICE experiment](https://home.cern/news/news/physics/alice-detects-conversion-lead-gold-lhc) found the Large Hadron Collider made about 86 billion gold nuclei from 2015 to 2018, **29 picograms** in all, each lasting a tiny fraction of a second.

Gold is "relatively scarce in the earth", says the [US Geological Survey](https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/prospect1/goldgip.html), but "concentrated by geologic processes" into lodes and placers. In one hypothesis the USGS calls widely accepted, many lodes, especially in volcanic and sedimentary rocks, form when water, mostly rain, seeps down fractures, is heated by magma, dissolves metals from rock and drops them as veins in cooler rock nearer the surface. The agency lists two other ideas: fluids given off by cooling magma, and water driven out of rocks buried and heated in mountain belts.

Gold-bearing fluids are [dilute](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/gold-nugget-formation-from-earthquake-induced-piezoelectricity-in/), **under 1 milligram of gold per kilogram**, which makes big nuggets hard to explain. A 2024 [Monash University study](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/gold-nugget-formation-from-earthquake-induced-piezoelectricity-in/) led by Christopher Voisey looked to quartz, "the only abundant piezoelectric mineral on Earth". Earthquakes stress a vein's quartz thousands of times. In lab experiments and models, that stress made enough voltage to plate dissolved gold out of solution, and existing grains became the focus of growth because gold conducts.

Placers are the work of erosion. Gold is "extremely resistant to weathering", says the [USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/prospect1/goldgip.html), and once freed it travels downstream as dust, flakes, grains and nuggets. Impure gold is 16 to 18 times as dense as water, against about 2.5 for waste rock, the [USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/prospect1/goldgip.html) notes, so it works down through stirred gravel in floods and often settles on or near bedrock; concentrations of gold in gravel are called **pay streaks**.

## Four stages, four levels of certainty

| Stage | Where | Evidence | How sure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Atoms made | Neutron-star collisions, perhaps rare supernovae and magnetar flares | The 2017 merger's light | Mergers seen; tally unsolved |
| First gold sinks | The core, more than 99% of it | Wood 2006; Goettingen 2025 | Established; a leak reported in 2025 |
| Mined gold lands | The mantle, by meteorite | Greenland tungsten | Isotope-backed hypothesis |
| Gold gathered | Quartz veins, river gravels | USGS; Voisey 2024 | Veins accepted; quartz voltage new |

Astronomers have caught two neutron stars colliding and glowing with fresh heavy elements, and they still cannot account for all the gold in the galaxy. The metal in a riverbed nugget was made by events they have not finished counting. Strictly FYI.
