William Bradford, later Plymouth Colony's [second governor](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/thanksgiving-national-day-of-mourning/the-first-thanksgiving/) and its historian, walked into a deer snare on Cape Cod in November 1620. He was one of a [party of 16 armed men](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/the-pilgrims-landing-in-america/) exploring on foot, and after a very rainy night it had lost its way in the woods.

They came on a young tree "bowed down over a bow, and some acorns strewed underneath," and Stephen Hopkins said it was meant to catch deer. Bradford, at the rear, went round to look, and "it gave a sudden jerk up, and he was immediately caught by the leg," according to [Mourt's Relation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/66359/pg66359-images.html), the colonists' journal printed in 1622. The colonists admired the trap's makers: it was "a very pretty device, made with a rope of their own making."

On the morning the snare caught him, the Mayflower had been at anchor inside the tip of Cape Cod since 11 November, and the ship would not reach Plymouth for another month.

## When did the Mayflower land?

The Mayflower first landed at Cape Cod: it anchored off what is now Provincetown on **11 November 1620**, and a party went ashore that day, according to [Pilgrim Hall Museum](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/the-pilgrims-landing-in-america/) in Plymouth. Plymouth came a month later, when the exploring party "went ashore at Plymouth on December 11, 1620 (Old Style)," and the ship followed shortly after.

Those are the Old Style (Julian) dates the passengers wrote. On today's calendar they are **21 November** and **21 December**, and the Plymouth landing sometimes appears as ["December 11/21."](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/old-new-style-date/)

| Event | Date as written (Old Style) | Today's calendar (New Style) |
|---|---|---|
| Sails from Plymouth, England | 6 September 1620 | 16 September |
| Cape Cod sighted | 9 November | 19 November |
| Anchors at Cape Cod; Compact signed | 11 November | 21 November |
| Boat party lands at Plymouth | 11 December | 21 December |
| Mayflower anchors at Plymouth | 16 December | 26 December |

## They were bound for the Hudson River and turned back at the shoals

The passengers sighted Cape Cod on [9 November](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/the-pilgrims-landing-in-america/) and anchored two days later. Plymouth had been their third start for America: they first sailed from Southampton "about the fifth of August" with a second ship, the Speedwell, which leaked so badly that both turned back, putting in at Dartmouth and then at Plymouth, and the Mayflower finally sailed alone on 6 September, [Pilgrim Hall's history of the ship](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/the-mayflower/) records. It calls what followed "The 66-day voyage."

Bradford's history, [*Of Plymouth Plantation*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/24950/24950-h/24950-h.htm), says they first turned south "to finde some place aboute Hudsons river for their habitation," ran into "deangerous shoulds and roring breakers," and turned back.

Before going ashore, the men signed the agreement now called the [Mayflower Compact](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/the-mayflower-compact/), which [Bradford's copy](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/24950/24950-h/24950-h.htm) dates "at Cap-Codd" on 11 November. Some passengers had been saying that once ashore "they would use their owne libertie," Bradford explains, because the colony's patent was "for Virginia, and not for New-england."

The same day, [Mourt's Relation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/66359/pg66359-images.html) records, "fifteen or sixteen men, well armed" went ashore, partly to fetch wood, "for we had none left." The ship could get no closer than three quarters of a mile, so people going ashore were "forced to wade a bow shot or two," which "caused many to get colds and coughs, for it was nigh times freezing cold weather."

## Plymouth was found by a small boat a month later

The colonists' shallop, a boat they had carried over in pieces, set out on **6 December** with ten colonists who had volunteered, Bradford among them, and some of the crew, according to the party list in [Mourt's Relation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/66359/pg66359-images.html). In a storm of snow and rain the rudder broke and the mast split "in three pieces," and the party spent the night on what is now [Clark's Island](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/the-pilgrims-landing-in-america/), in Plymouth Harbor.

They rested there through the Sabbath. "On Munday" they sounded the harbor, [Bradford wrote](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/24950/24950-h/24950-h.htm), "and founde it fitt for shipping," then marched inland "& found diverse cornfeilds, & litle runing brooks." [James Thacher's 1832 history of Plymouth](https://archive.org/details/historyoftownofp02thac) dates that Monday to **11 December**, on "the united testimony" of the early accounts.

On 16 December, [Bradford continues](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/24950/24950-h/24950-h.htm), the wind came fair "and they arrived safe in this harbor." The ship had carried [102 passengers](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/the-mayflower/). Within two or three months, Bradford wrote, "halfe of their company dyed," especially in January and February.

## The written dates run ten days behind ours

England kept the old Julian calendar, built on [Julius Caesar's leap-year rule](/blog/why-does-february-have-28-days/), until 1752, when it switched to the Gregorian calendar used today. By 1582, when Pope Gregory XIII issued the new calendar, the old one had drifted **10 days** out, [Pilgrim Hall](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/old-new-style-date/) explains. The dates in the Pilgrims' own accounts are Old Style, so adding ten gives the New Style date.

Plymouth's own anniversary picked up an extra day. The Old Colony Club, formed in 1769, held its first celebration of the landing on **22 December** that year, [Thacher records](https://archive.org/details/historyoftownofp02thac), adding the 11-day gap of its own century: 1700 had been a leap year under the old calendar but not under the new one, so the difference grew from ten days to eleven. For 1620, Thacher pointed out, "only ten days, instead of eleven, should be added." That gives 21 December, the date the [Pilgrim Society now keeps](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/old-new-style-date/) for Forefathers' Day.

## Plymouth Rock was pointed out more than 120 years later

Neither Bradford's history nor Mourt's Relation mentions a rock in its account of the landing. "Both simply say that the Pilgrims landed," [Pilgrim Hall's history of the rock](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/history-of-plymouth-rock/) observes. The editor of the [1963 edition of Mourt's Relation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/66359/pg66359-images.html), Dwight B. Heath, was blunter in a footnote:

> "Clearly there is nothing here that can be construed as referring to 'Plymouth Rock.'"

The tradition goes back to Thomas Faunce, a Plymouth church elder. About **1741**, [Thacher writes](https://archive.org/details/historyoftownofp02thac), Faunce heard that a wharf was to be built over the rock. He was in his nineties and lived three miles away, so "a chair was procured" and he was "conveyed to the shore," where he pointed out the rock "which his father had assured him was that which had received the footsteps of our fathers." Then he "bedewed it with his tears and bid to it an everlasting adieu."

Thomas had been born about 1647. His father, John Faunce, had arrived in 1623 on the ship Anne, "just three years after the Mayflower," and died in 1653, when Thomas was about six, [Pilgrim Hall notes](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/history-of-plymouth-rock/), though Thomas had four older siblings "who may have also been inheritors of the story." [Thacher said](https://archive.org/details/historyoftownofp02thac) the fact had come down "by unquestionable tradition."

The rock first reached print in **1775**, a year after local patriots split it in two while hauling it to Town Square. The *Pennsylvania Journal* reported that Captain William Coit, back with two captured British ships, "made the prisoners land upon the same rock our ancestors first trod," according to the [museum's account](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/history-of-plymouth-rock/). In 1880 the Pilgrim Society reunited the top of the rock with its base, and the date "1620" was cut into the face.

That leaves the rock an unverified tradition: nothing written at the time confirms it, and nothing found since rules it out. James Baker's *Plymouth Rock's Own Story*, a 2020 history published by the Pilgrim Society, says the claims about it "can never be definitively addressed," as quoted by [Pilgrim Hall](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/history-of-plymouth-rock/).

## The Thanksgiving harvest feast came the next autumn

The harvest gathering remembered as the first Thanksgiving took place in the autumn of 1621. Edward Winslow described it in a [letter dated 11 December 1621](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/thanksgiving-national-day-of-mourning/the-first-thanksgiving/), a year to the day after the boat party walked into Plymouth. "Our harvest being gotten in, our governor sent four men on fowling," he wrote, and Massasoit came "with some ninety men, whom for three days we entertained and feasted." He gave no date for the feast itself.

Bradford's history adds that by that autumn there was "great store of wild Turkies, of which they tooke many," as [Pilgrim Hall's transcription](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/thanksgiving-national-day-of-mourning/the-first-thanksgiving/) shows.

The rock carries only a year. Bradford, who was in the boat that December, gave the day they first walked into Plymouth a single word in his history: "Munday." Strictly FYI.
