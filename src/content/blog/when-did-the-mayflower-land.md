William Bradford, later Plymouth Colony's second governor and its historian, walked into a deer snare on Cape Cod in November 1620. His exploring party of 16 armed men had lost its way in the woods after a night of heavy rain when it came on a young tree "bowed down over a bow, and some acorns strewed underneath." Stephen Hopkins said it was meant to catch deer.

Bradford, at the rear of the file, went round to look, and "it gave a sudden jerk up, and he was immediately caught by the leg," according to [Mourt's Relation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/66359/pg66359-images.html), the colonists' journal printed in 1622. The colonists admired the trap's makers: it was "a very pretty device, made with a rope of their own making."

On the morning the snare caught him, the Mayflower had been at anchor inside the tip of Cape Cod since 11 November, and the ship would not reach Plymouth for another month.

## When did the Mayflower land?

The Mayflower first landed at Cape Cod: it anchored in what is now Provincetown Harbor on **11 November 1620**, and men went ashore that day. Plymouth came a month later, when a boat party landed on **11 December**. Those are the Old Style (Julian) dates the passengers wrote; on today's calendar they are **21 November** and **21 December**.

[Pilgrim Hall Museum](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/the-pilgrims-landing-in-america/), in Plymouth, gives the same two arrivals: the Mayflower came "to anchor in Cape Cod Bay on November 11, 1620," and the exploring party "went ashore at Plymouth on December 11, 1620 (Old Style)." The ship itself reached Plymouth on **16 December**. Neither of the passengers' own accounts of the landings mentions Plymouth Rock, which was first pointed out about 1741.

## They were bound for the Hudson River and turned back at the shoals

The passengers sighted Cape Cod at daybreak on 9 November, **64 days** after leaving Plymouth, England, and anchored two days later. Plymouth had been their third departure from an English port: a leaky second ship, the Speedwell, had sent both vessels back to port twice since they first sailed from Southampton about 5 August.

Bradford's history, [*Of Plymouth Plantation*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/24950/24950-h/24950-h.htm), says they first turned south "to finde some place aboute Hudsons river for their habitation," ran into "deangerous shoulds and roring breakers," and turned back.

Before going ashore, the men signed the agreement now called the [Mayflower Compact](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/the-mayflower-compact/), which Bradford's copy dates "at Cap-Codd ye 11. of November." Some passengers had been saying that once ashore "they would use their owne libertie," Bradford explains, because the colony's patent was "for Virginia, and not for New-england."

The same day, Mourt's Relation records, "fifteen or sixteen men, well armed" went ashore, partly to fetch wood, "for we had none left." The ship could get no closer than three quarters of a mile, so people going ashore were "forced to wade a bow shot or two," which "caused many to get colds and coughs, for it was nigh times freezing cold weather."

## Plymouth was found by a small boat a month later

The colonists' shallop, a large boat they had carried over in the ship, set out on 6 December with 10 of their leading men, Bradford among them, and some of the crew. In a storm of snow and rain the rudder broke and the mast split "in three pieces," and the party spent the night on what is now Clark's Island, in Plymouth Harbor.

They rested there through the Sabbath. "On Munday they sounded ye harbor, and founde it fitt for shipping," Bradford wrote, and "marched into ye land, & found diverse cornfeilds, & litle runing brooks." James Thacher's 1832 [history of Plymouth](https://archive.org/details/historyoftownofp02thac) dates that Monday to **11 December**, on "the united testimony" of the early accounts.

On 16 December, Bradford continues, "ye winde came faire, and they arrived safe in this harbor." Of the **102** passengers, Pilgrim Hall notes, only half were alive by spring.

## The written dates run ten days behind ours

England kept the old Julian calendar, built on [Julius Caesar's leap-year rule](/blog/why-does-february-have-28-days/), until 1752, when it switched to the Gregorian calendar used today. By 1582, when Pope Gregory XIII issued the new calendar, the old one had drifted **10 days** out, [Pilgrim Hall](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/old-new-style-date/) explains, and Thacher notes the gap between the two stayed at ten until 1700. The dates in the Pilgrims' own accounts are Old Style, so adding ten gives the New Style date, and the Plymouth landing sometimes appears as "December 11/21." The gap has since grown to 13 days, and Orthodox churches still [calculate Easter on the Julian calendar](/blog/why-does-easter-change-every-year/).

The 11 November at the foot of the Compact is therefore not the 11 November of [Veterans Day](/blog/why-do-we-celebrate-veterans-day/); on today's calendar the signing fell on 21 November.

Plymouth's own anniversary picked up an extra day. The Old Colony Club, formed in 1769, held its first celebration of the landing on **22 December** that year, adding the 11-day gap of its own century. Thacher pointed out that "only ten days, instead of eleven, should be added," which gives 21 December, the date the Pilgrim Society now keeps for Forefathers' Day.

## Plymouth Rock was pointed out more than 120 years later

Bradford's history and Mourt's Relation never mention a rock. "Both simply say that the Pilgrims landed," [Pilgrim Hall's history of the rock](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/history-of-plymouth-rock/) observes. The editor of the 1963 edition of Mourt's Relation, Dwight B. Heath, was blunter in a footnote:

> "Clearly there is nothing here that can be construed as referring to 'Plymouth Rock.'"

The tradition of the rock goes back to Thomas Faunce, a church elder born in Plymouth about 1647. About **1741**, Thacher writes, Faunce heard that a wharf was to be built over the rock. He was in his nineties and lived three miles away, so "a chair was procured" and he was "conveyed to the shore," where he pointed out the rock "which his father had assured him was that which had received the footsteps of our fathers." Then he "bedewed it with his tears and bid to it an everlasting adieu."

His father, John Faunce, had arrived in 1623 on the ship Anne, "just three years after the Mayflower," and died in 1653, when Thomas was about six. Pilgrim Hall adds that Thomas had four older siblings "who may have also been inheritors of the story." Thacher said the fact had come down "by unquestionable tradition."

The rock first reached print in **1775**, a year after local patriots split it in two while hauling it to Town Square. The *Pennsylvania Journal* reported that Captain William Coit, back with two captured British ships, "made the prisoners land upon the same rock our ancestors first trod."

That leaves the rock an unverified tradition: nothing written at the time confirms it, and nothing found since rules it out. James Baker's *Plymouth Rock's Own Story*, a 2020 history published by the Pilgrim Society, says the claims about it "can never be definitively addressed."

## The Thanksgiving harvest feast came the next autumn

The harvest gathering remembered as the first Thanksgiving took place in the autumn of 1621. Edward Winslow described it in a [letter dated 11 December 1621](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/thanksgiving-national-day-of-mourning/the-first-thanksgiving/), a year to the day after the boat party walked into Plymouth. "Our harvest being gotten in, our governor sent four men on fowling," he wrote, and Massasoit came "with some ninety men, whom for three days we entertained and feasted." He gave no date for the feast itself.

Bradford's history adds that by that autumn there was "great store of wild Turkies, of which they tooke many," the bird now blamed for [the after-dinner slump](/blog/why-does-turkey-make-you-sleepy/).

The rock now carries the year 1620, cut into its face in 1880, when its two halves were reunited. Bradford, who was in the boat that December, gave the day they first walked into Plymouth only one word in his history: "Munday." Strictly FYI.
