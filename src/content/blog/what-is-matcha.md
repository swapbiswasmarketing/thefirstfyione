The shogun had a hangover. An entry in the Azuma kagami, the chronicle of Japan's Kamakura shogunate, dated the fourth day of the second month of 1214, records that the Zen monk Eisai came to pray over Minamoto no Sanetomo and brought, as [Takuya Hino's 2020 study of the text](https://rcwbc.ryukoku.ac.jp/cms/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Journal-of-World-Buddhist-Cultures-vol.3-2020.3.pdf) summarizes it, "a cup of tea delivered from the main temple and a single volume of writings describing the values of tea."

That volume is generally identified as the Kissa Yojoki, A Record of Drinking Tea for Good Health. Its first version opens, [in Hino's translation](https://rcwbc.ryukoku.ac.jp/cms/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Journal-of-World-Buddhist-Cultures-vol.3-2020.3.pdf), by calling tea "an elixir [made of powdered tea leaf] for everlasting good health," the words in brackets being Hino's gloss. The tea in that bowl was very likely powdered, the ancestor of matcha, and the method in Eisai's book is, by his own account, the way tea was made in Song China.

## What is matcha?

Matcha is a Japanese green tea made from leaves [shaded for **two to three weeks**](https://www.caa.go.jp/policies/policy/food_labeling/food_labeling_act/assets/food_labeling_cms101_210317_12.pdf) before picking, then steamed and dried flat without rolling into a leaf called tencha. The tencha is milled to a fine powder in a stone mill or similar. Unlike steeped teas, whose leaves are removed before drinking, the powder is [whisked into hot water](https://theconversation.com/green-gruel-pea-soup-what-westerners-thought-of-matcha-when-they-tried-it-for-the-first-time-263014) and drunk whole.

The shading, steaming, unrolled drying and milling are the definition in [the Japan Tea Central Association's green tea labeling standard](https://www.caa.go.jp/policies/policy/food_labeling/food_labeling_act/assets/food_labeling_cms101_210317_12.pdf), which Japan's Consumer Affairs Agency reprints for reference. It names the covers too: reed screens, woven mats or cheesecloth.

Tea simply crushed in a grinder is a different product under the same standard, called funmatsucha, or powdered tea. The historian Rebecca Corbett's definition draws the same line: matcha is ["a form of green tea in which young tea leaves have been ground to a powder using a stone mill."](https://theconversation.com/green-gruel-pea-soup-what-westerners-thought-of-matcha-when-they-tried-it-for-the-first-time-263014)

The same standard places matcha among its neighbors, and [its definitions](https://www.caa.go.jp/policies/policy/food_labeling/food_labeling_act/assets/food_labeling_cms101_210317_12.pdf) show that shade alone does not make matcha:

| Tea | Shaded before picking | Leaf rolled? | Final form |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sencha | Not part of the definition | Yes | Loose leaf |
| Kabusecha | About 7 days | Yes | Loose leaf |
| Gyokuro | About 20 days, nearly all sunlight blocked | Yes | Loose leaf |
| Tencha, sold as matcha | 2 to 3 weeks | No | Milled to powder |

## The whisked powder began in Song China

The most prized Chinese tea under the Song dynasty came pressed into cakes, the finest of them stamped with dragons and phoenixes. Emperor Huizong's Treatise on Tea, written [**in 1107**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Treatise_on_Tea), says those cakes "have been made exclusively for the imperial family," in the [English translation published by Global Tea Hut magazine](http://archive.globalteahut.org/docs/pdf_articles/2016-04/2016-04-a036.pdf). Drinkers ground the tea to powder, beat it with a bamboo whisk in a bowl, and judged the foam.

The color they wanted was not green. [**"The best tea is pure white in color,"**](http://archive.globalteahut.org/docs/pdf_articles/2016-04/2016-04-a036.pdf) Huizong wrote, and the tea was at its peak when the surface looked "like a crisp frost or freshly fallen snow." He recommended dark bowls, bluish-black, because they ["heighten the whiteness of the tea froth."](http://archive.globalteahut.org/docs/pdf_articles/2016-04/2016-04-a036.pdf)

> Whisk badly, Huizong warned, and "the result is a bowl of disappointingly dull green water without any white foam to speak of." - [Treatise on Tea, in Global Tea Hut's translation](http://archive.globalteahut.org/docs/pdf_articles/2016-04/2016-04-a036.pdf)

## Eisai carried the habit to Japan

Tea itself was not new to Japan. A court chronicle records the monk Eichu serving it to Emperor Saga [in 815](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matcha), but interest faded. Eisai studied Zen in China and [returned in **1191**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eisai), and the Kissa Yojoki includes his account of how the Song made tea.

He wrote the book twice. The first version carries a preface dated the first day of the first month of **1211**, and a second version, copied in the first month of **1214**, is [believed to be the one offered to Sanetomo](https://rcwbc.ryukoku.ac.jp/cms/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Journal-of-World-Buddhist-Cultures-vol.3-2020.3.pdf).

Its method is plainly Chinese. In the Song, Eisai explains, ["one harvests tea in the morning and steams and roasts it while it is very fresh,"](https://rcwbc.ryukoku.ac.jp/cms/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Journal-of-World-Buddhist-Cultures-vol.3-2020.3.pdf) finishing the roasting overnight. To drink it, he says, put two or three spoonfuls of the powder into "extremely hot water."

Modern tencha keeps the steaming. Under [the same labeling standard](https://www.caa.go.jp/policies/policy/food_labeling/food_labeling_act/assets/food_labeling_cms101_210317_12.pdf), the steamed leaf is then dried flat, without rolling, in a tencha furnace, a dryer that carries the leaves spread out on a conveyor.

## China let it go after a Ming decree

Two centuries after Eisai came home, China began to give the drink up. In **1391** the founding Ming emperor prohibited the manufacture of tea cakes, a Hong Kong government notice for the Flagstaff House Museum of Tea Ware explains, ["bringing about the adoption of loose-leaf tea."](https://www.info.gov.hk/gia/general/200108/08/0808194.htm) The same notice says the ewers and tea bowls of the old method went out of fashion, and teapots for brewing whole leaves took their place.

The late-Ming writer Shen Defu [recorded that the emperor stopped the dragon cakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matcha) because of the heavy burden their manufacture placed on the people, and ordered only tea buds sent to court. The whisked powder did not vanish at once: the [Cha Pu, a tea manual written in **1440**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cha_Pu) by Zhu Quan, the founding emperor's 17th son, still has chapters on the tea mill, on whisking tea and on the tea whisk. In China the practice faded over the following centuries of the Ming, while Japan kept grinding and whisking.

## Shade and the name came later, in Japan

The word came later than the drink. A Japanese connoisseur's manual of 1476 already illustrates "matcha jars," and the characters for matcha appear in a dictionary of 1548. Stone mills made for tea, which give a finer powder than the older grinders, [came into use in Japan in the 14th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matcha).

Covering the bushes is Japanese as well, and probably older than the documents suggested. Historians had dated it to the second half of the 16th century, until a 2019 soil and radiocarbon study of the oldest existing tea plantation in Uji estimated that shading there began [**in the first half of the 15th century**](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/dojo/90/6/90_900602/_article/-char/en), "approximately 150 years" earlier.

So the popular line that matcha is an ancient Japanese invention is half right. The powder and the whisk came from Song China, and so did steaming the fresh leaf, as Eisai's account of the Song shows. Covering the bushes before picking was added in Japan over the next few centuries, along with the name.

## Westerners first compared it to pea soup

When Western travelers met matcha in Meiji Japan, it did not at first suit their palates, [Corbett writes](https://theconversation.com/green-gruel-pea-soup-what-westerners-thought-of-matcha-when-they-tried-it-for-the-first-time-263014). The American writer Eliza Scidmore described the thick tea served at one ceremony, twelve spoonfuls of powder to the bowl, as ["a bowl of green gruel more bitter than quinine"](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/72989/pg72989-images.html) in Jinrikisha Days in Japan, first published in 1891.

A Canadian visitor, Katharine Schuyler Baxter, called it ["thick like pea-soup, fragrant, and not very palatable"](https://theconversation.com/green-gruel-pea-soup-what-westerners-thought-of-matcha-when-they-tried-it-for-the-first-time-263014) in 1895, and Corbett found pea soup the most common comparison of the era.

Huizong, the Song emperor whose Treatise on Tea described the whisked powdered tea behind matcha, wanted its froth as white as snow. The version of his drink that outlived the Song is the green one. Strictly FYI.
