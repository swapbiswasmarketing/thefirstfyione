If tryptophan were the reason turkey makes people sleepy, chicken would do the job just as well. In the US Department of Agriculture's food composition tables, roasted turkey breast carries **0.287 or 0.364 grams** of the amino acid per 100 grams, depending on the sample set, and roasted chicken breast **0.362 grams**. Hard parmesan beats both birds at **0.482 grams**.

## Why does turkey make you sleepy?

A meal, the mid-afternoon body-clock dip and alcohol can all make you sleepy, and turkey is not special. In a 1998 study, people were measurably sleepier **1.5 hours after eating** than before, whether the meal was rich in fat or carbohydrate. Turkey has about as much tryptophan as chicken breast, and in rats, protein diets that raised blood tryptophan left brain tryptophan unchanged.

## The tryptophan myth breaks at the blood-brain barrier

The myth rests on real chemistry, since the brain builds the neurotransmitter serotonin from tryptophan; its weak link is getting more tryptophan into the brain. In 1972, John Fernstrom and Richard Wurtman reported in [Science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5077329/) that when rats were given injections of tryptophan or insulin, or fed carbohydrate, tryptophan rose in their blood and in their brains. Diets containing protein pushed blood tryptophan up even further, and **brain tryptophan did not change**.

What seemed to decide the brain's share, they wrote, was not blood tryptophan alone but its ratio to five other amino acids (tyrosine, phenylalanine, leucine, isoleucine and valine) "that compete with it for uptake into the brain." Turkey delivers all six together. In both of USDA's roasted turkey breast records, the five competitors outweigh the tryptophan **roughly 18 to 1**.

> In Fernstrom and Wurtman's rats, protein diets put more tryptophan into the blood than carbohydrate did, yet the carbohydrate raised brain tryptophan and the protein did not. Roasted turkey breast carries about 18 grams of competing amino acids for every gram of tryptophan.

In people, a 2003 MIT study in the [American Journal of Clinical Nutrition](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12499331/) found that the blood tryptophan ratio differed by a median **54 percent** between a carbohydrate-rich and a protein-rich breakfast, and noted that high-protein diets were already known to depress it.

## In rats, a little protein cancels the carbohydrate effect

A common correction moves the blame one dish along. [Wikipedia's tryptophan article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tryptophan) says the drowsiness "may be caused by other foods eaten with the turkey, particularly carbohydrates": insulin from the carbohydrate pushes competing amino acids into muscle, and tryptophan gets a clearer run at the brain.

In a 1986 study in [Metabolism](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3747840/), Yokogoshi and Wurtman gave fasted rats meals of 70 to 75 percent carbohydrate, which raised the blood tryptophan ratio. **Adding as little as 5 percent casein fully blocked the rise**, probably, they wrote, because the protein added far more competing amino acids than tryptophan to the blood. At 10 percent protein, casein, peanut meal and gelatin fully blocked it, egg white only partly, and lactalbumin not at all.

The carbohydrate-rich breakfast in the 2003 study held **5.2 grams of protein**; 100 grams of roasted turkey breast holds about 30 grams. "The carbs did it" was shown on meals that were mostly carbohydrate, and as an account of a turkey dinner it is overstated.

## A meal can make people measurably sleepier

In a 1998 University of Sheffield study in the [Journal of Applied Physiology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9475860/), 16 men and women ate a high-fat and a high-carbohydrate meal of equal energy, four hours apart. On the Multiple Sleep Latency Test, which times how quickly a person falls asleep, sleepiness was **significantly greater 1.5 hours after the meals than before**, with "no differences in sleepiness according to the composition of the meal." The authors, who staggered meal times by two hours between groups, suggested the rise was "not solely due to circadian rhythms."

Size can add to it. At Loughborough University, 12 young men limited to five hours' sleep the night before ate a 305-calorie lunch and, on another occasion, a 922-calorie one, each followed by two hours in a car simulator ([Physiology and Behavior, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22155490/)). After the heavy lunch they drifted out of lane more often and their EEGs showed more sleepiness, though only after the first 30 minutes.

Neither study measured blood flow, so they offer no support to the popular explanation that a heavy meal brings on drowsiness by shifting blood to the digestive system.

## The afternoon dip can arrive without lunch

In a 2005 review in [Clinics in Sports Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15892914/), Timothy Monk of the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center describes a mid-afternoon dip in performance, seen on some tasks and in some people, that comes with a rise in sleepiness. He calls it "a real phenomenon that can occur even when the individual has had no lunch and is unaware of the time of day." He adds that a high-carbohydrate lunch makes it worse, a point the Sheffield comparison did not bear out.

The Loughborough paper calls the dip "largely unrelated to lunch." It comes from the body's internal clock, the same kind of timer that [sets a rooster crowing before dawn](/blog/why-do-roosters-crow/). A feast served in mid-afternoon lands on top of it.

Alcohol hits harder at a sleepy hour. Reviewing the research in 2001 for the [National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism's journal](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6707127/), Timothy Roehrs and Thomas Roth of Henry Ford Hospital report that alcohol made people fall asleep faster on the Multiple Sleep Latency Test, more so at higher doses. They add that "increased sleepiness compounded alcohol's effects," even in the same person tested at different times of day.

## The myth was a sitcom plot by 1997

On **6 November 1997**, the Seinfeld episode "The Merv Griffin Show" sent Jerry and George after a girlfriend's vintage toys, which she would not let Jerry touch. In [Wikipedia's episode summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Merv_Griffin_Show_%28Seinfeld%29), the plan for putting her to sleep is a dinner of turkey, which "contains tryptophan," and red wine, followed by a four-hour home movie. Even the sitcom did not trust the bird to work alone.

Put a roast chicken on the Thanksgiving table instead, serve it at the same hour, and nothing in these studies predicts livelier guests. The turkey has been taking the blame for the meal and the hour. Strictly FYI.
