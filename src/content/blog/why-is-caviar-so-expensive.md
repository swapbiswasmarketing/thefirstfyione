On 21 April 2004, the US Fish and Wildlife Service published a rule in the Federal Register about a fish that has never swum in American waters. It runs for pages in the flat register of federal rulemaking, and buried in the biology section are two numbers that explain the price of caviar better than any menu.

The fish is the beluga sturgeon, *Huso huso*, whose range is now limited to the Caspian and Black Sea basins. The numbers describe how long a female waits before she can breed, and how long she waits before doing it again.

## Why is caviar so expensive?

Because the supply is throttled at the source. A female beluga sturgeon reaches reproductive capacity at **14 to 20 years of age** and thereafter **reproduces once every 4 to 8 years**, according to the Fish and Wildlife Service. Wild stocks collapsed a century ago, and international trade has been licensed since 1998.

## Maturity is only half the clock

Slow maturity is the fact every caviar seller quotes, and it is correct. The [2004 listing rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2004-04-21/html/04-8934.htm) puts male beluga at **10 to 16 years** and females at **14 to 20**. On its own that would make caviar a late crop, and a late crop still arrives every year.

The sentence that does the damage comes next. Females reproduce **once every 4 to 8 years**, so a fish fed and guarded for two decades then yields intermittently rather than annually. A grower waits once for the animal to grow up, and keeps waiting between spawnings, on a fish that can live past 50.

| Fish | Age at first spawning | Gap between spawnings |
|---|---|---|
| Beluga sturgeon, female | 14 to 20 years | 4 to 8 years |
| Beluga sturgeon, male | 10 to 16 years | 4 to 7 years |
| Atlantic sturgeon, female | close to 20 years | 3 to 5 years |
| Atlantic sturgeon, Saint Lawrence stock | 22 to 34 years | not stated |

Figures from the 2004 Fish and Wildlife Service rule, the [Chesapeake Bay Program](https://www.chesapeakebay.net/discover/field-guide/entry/atlantic-sturgeon) and [NOAA Fisheries](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/atlantic-sturgeon).

The rule states the transaction without decoration: "the female beluga sturgeon is harvested to produce beluga caviar". Each tin costs a breeding female, taken from a population where breeding females are the scarce part.

One number runs in the fish's favour. An adult female beluga can produce **up to 12 percent of her body weight in roe**, which is why a single large fish is worth chasing at all. Animal biology hands out lopsided figures like that constantly, and [the reason bird droppings come out white](/blog/why-is-bird-poop-white/) is another of them.

> Most food animals are priced by the season. A beluga is priced by the generation: 14 to 20 years before a female carries roe at all, then a gap of 4 to 8 years before she carries it again.

## America ate its own sturgeon in a single generation

The popular story says caviar has always been rare and always been food for kings, so the price reflects a permanent fact of nature. The American record contradicts that.

NOAA Fisheries describes what happened on the east coast: "During the late 1800s, people flocked to the eastern United States in search of caviar riches from the sturgeon fishery, known as the 'Black Gold Rush.'" The [Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission](https://asmfc.org/species/atlantic-sturgeon/) supplies the scale. In the late 1800s Atlantic sturgeon "were second only to lobster among important fisheries, with landings estimated at seven million pounds per year just prior to the turn of the century."

Seven million pounds a year, from a fish it is now illegal to catch, keep or take eggs from. The 1800s ran on animals in ways that are easy to forget now, from the sturgeon crowding the rivers to [the working horses that needed iron nailed to their feet](/blog/why-do-horses-need-shoes/).

By the beginning of the 1900s, NOAA says, populations "had declined drastically and continued to decline until the coastwide moratorium", which the Commission and the federal government imposed in late 1997 and early 1998. In 2012 Atlantic sturgeon were listed under the Endangered Species Act as five distinct population segments, four of them endangered and the Gulf of Maine population threatened.

Europe lost its stock a second way. Beluga harvest on the Danube averaged **23 metric tons a year** in the mid-1970s, and the 2004 rule records that after the Djerdap I and II dams went up the harvest fell to **12.7 tons** within a decade. A sturgeon blocked from its spawning river does not go and spawn somewhere else instead.

The scarcity has dates and causes on both sides of the Atlantic, and people supplied both. Other seafood reputations are younger still: before the Second World War a lot of bluefin tuna sold so cheaply that some went for cat food, and it caught on as [a sushi topping](/blog/where-did-sushi-originate/) only after fast freezing developed.

## Then the paperwork arrived

The collapse rewrote the law, and the law rewrote the price.

In 1998, all previously unlisted sturgeons and paddlefishes were added to Appendix II of CITES, the treaty that controls international trade in threatened species. The April 2004 rule then added the beluga to the US threatened list, effective that October. Every legal tin since carries permits, inspection and paperwork before anyone tastes anything.

Illegal supply fills the gap and warps everything around it. The 2004 rule cites a 1996 estimate that illegal harvest of Caspian Sea sturgeons ran 6 to 10 times higher than legal harvest, and notes later assessments suggesting the illegal trade "may be some 11 times greater than the legal market". Rules written around a valuable commodity leave permanent marks on it, much as [the milled edge of a coin records an old fraud](/blog/why-coins-have-ridges/).

For an official price anchor, the same rule states: "In 1995, the retail price for one pound of beluga caviar in the United States was US$1,000."

## The scarcity has a date on it

That rule also records what the fish used to be. During the early 20th century, 100-year-old beluga sturgeon "were commonly taken". The oldest fish harvested now run 50 to 55 years, with an average age under 35. The same document puts individual fish at up to 6 metres and more than a ton in weight.

The tin on the counter holds roe from a younger, smaller animal than the ones that built caviar's reputation, and it costs what it costs because there are fewer of them, and there are fewer of them because three or four generations of buyers moved faster than the fish could breed. The price is a receipt. Strictly FYI.
