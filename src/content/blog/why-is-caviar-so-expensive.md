On 21 April 2004, the US Fish and Wildlife Service [published a rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2004-04-21/html/04-8934.htm) in the Federal Register listing as threatened, under the Endangered Species Act, a fish that is not native to the United States. It runs for pages in the flat register of federal rulemaking, and buried in its background section are two numbers that explain the price of caviar better than any menu.

The fish is the beluga sturgeon, *Huso huso*, whose range is now limited to the Caspian and Black Sea basins. The numbers describe how long a female waits before she can breed, and how long she waits before doing it again.

## Why is caviar so expensive?

Because the supply is throttled at the source. A female beluga sturgeon matures at **14 to 20 years of age** and then reproduces only **once every 4 to 8 years**, according to the [Fish and Wildlife Service](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2004-04-21/html/04-8934.htm). America's own caviar fish, the Atlantic sturgeon, was fished down more than a century ago, and since 1998 international trade in sturgeon caviar has needed CITES permits.

## Maturity is only half the clock

Slow maturity is the usual explanation, and it is correct as far as it goes. A late crop, once it starts, still arrives every year. A female beluga does not: in the wild she breeds only every few years, so a stock stripped of its breeding females refills slowly.

| Fish | Age at maturity | Gap between spawnings |
|---|---|---|
| Beluga sturgeon, female | 14 to 20 years | 4 to 8 years |
| Beluga sturgeon, male | 10 to 16 years | 4 to 7 years |
| Atlantic sturgeon, female | nearly 20 years (Chesapeake Bay Program) | 2 to 5 years (NOAA Fisheries) |
| Atlantic sturgeon, Saint Lawrence River | 22 to 34 years | not given for this population |
| Siberian sturgeon, female, farmed | about 7 years | not every year, among broodstock |

Beluga figures from the Fish and Wildlife Service rule; Atlantic figures from the [Chesapeake Bay Program](https://www.chesapeakebay.net/discover/field-guide/entry/atlantic-sturgeon) and [NOAA Fisheries](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/atlantic-sturgeon); the farmed row from an [FAO species fact sheet](https://www.fao.org/fishery/docs/CDrom/aquaculture/I1129m/file/en/en_acipenser.htm).

The [rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2004-04-21/html/04-8934.htm) states the transaction without decoration: "the female beluga sturgeon is harvested to produce beluga caviar." It calls beluga sturgeon "highly vulnerable to depletion, due to their unique life-history characteristics, and because the fishery for them targets the reproductive segment of the population." The prize is large: an adult female can produce **up to 12 percent of her body weight in roe**, and the beluga is considered "the most economically valuable fish in the world".

Farming shortens the clock without stopping it. The [FAO fact sheet](https://www.fao.org/fishery/docs/CDrom/aquaculture/I1129m/file/en/en_acipenser.htm) on the Siberian sturgeon says that on farms puberty comes "considerably earlier", at about **7 years** for females, and that in many western countries caviar has become the main purpose of rearing the fish. The females "are kept and grown on for several years until they are mature enough to produce caviar. They are then harvested for meat."

Even the females a farm keeps back for breeding "do not ovulate every year", the [fact sheet](https://www.fao.org/fishery/docs/CDrom/aquaculture/I1129m/file/en/en_acipenser.htm) adds, and are not all in step, so the mature females obtained from one cohort in a year vary between 35 and 63 percent of the stock. The processing steps it lists for caviar include stunning, gutting and removal of the ovaries. A farmed tin, like a wild one, usually costs the whole fish.

> A female beluga needs 14 to 20 years to mature and then breeds only once every 4 to 8 years, and every tin of wild beluga caviar comes from one of those females.

## America ate its own sturgeon within a few decades

The popular story says caviar has always been rare and always been food for kings, so the price reflects a permanent fact of nature. The American record contradicts that.

In 1883 *Scientific American* reported that caviar was "not yet in as general use in this country as in Russia", and that "The value of the best hard caviare in the South, early in the spring, is said to be from 15 to 20 cents a pound", in an article the US Fish Commission [reprinted in its 1884 Bulletin](https://archive.org/details/bulletinofunited041884unit/page/346/). The same article describes sturgeon entering the Delaware River in such numbers that one fisherman had seen his net sink under their weight as soon as he threw it out.

[NOAA Fisheries](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/atlantic-sturgeon) describes the boom on the east coast: "During the late 1800s, people flocked to the eastern United States in search of caviar riches from the sturgeon fishery, known as the 'Black Gold Rush.'" It cautions that historical landings records "generally failed to differentiate between Atlantic sturgeon and the smaller shortnose sturgeon".

The [Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission](https://asmfc.org/species/atlantic-sturgeon/) supplies the scale: in the late 1800s Atlantic sturgeon "were second only to lobster among important fisheries, with landings estimated at seven million pounds per year just prior to the turn of the century." In US waters it is now [illegal to fish for, catch or harvest](https://www.chesapeakebay.net/discover/field-guide/entry/atlantic-sturgeon) the fish, eggs included.

By the beginning of the 1900s, [NOAA says](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/atlantic-sturgeon), populations "had declined drastically and continued to decline until the coastwide moratorium". The Commission and the federal government imposed that moratorium in late 1997 and early 1998, and the [Commission's plan](https://asmfc.org/species/atlantic-sturgeon/) keeps it in place "through at least 2038, in order to build up 20 year classes", a closure of at least 40 years. In 2012 Atlantic sturgeon were [listed under the Endangered Species Act](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/atlantic-sturgeon) as five distinct population segments, four of them endangered and the Gulf of Maine population threatened.

The Danube shows a second way to lose a stock. In the 19th century, the [2004 rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2004-04-21/html/04-8934.htm) notes, Black Sea beluga spawned as far upstream as Austria and Bavaria, and its verdict is that "Loss of spawning habitat has had the greatest impact on the survival of beluga sturgeon populations." It also cites a 2002 study which found that much of the catch "was under-reported by local fishers" and that a larger population may survive in the Danube and the Black Sea Basin than was believed. The recorded Danube beluga harvest averaged **23 tons** a year in the mid-1970s and fell to **12.7 tons** in 1994.

That peak itself had a dam behind it. Djerdap I, the Iron Gate I dam, was built in 1972 and Djerdap II in 1984, and a [2016 review in Danube News](https://www.danube-iad.eu/docs/DN_articles/2016-Major_obstacles_Danube_sturgeon_spawning_migration-DanubeNews-Bloesch.pdf) says the 1970s catches came from "great peaks of arriving sturgeons, stopped by the first dam and harvested by fishermen".

## Then the paperwork arrived

In 1998, all previously unlisted sturgeons and paddlefishes were added to [Appendix II of CITES](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2004-04-21/html/04-8934.htm), the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species, and the listing required all exports and re-exports of those species in international trade to carry a CITES export permit or re-export certificate. It was aimed at an illegal trade that was already enormous: the 2004 rule cites a 1996 estimate that illegal harvest of Caspian Sea sturgeons ran 6 to 10 times higher than the legal harvest, and a later assessment that the illegal trade "may be some 11 times greater than the legal market".

Illegal harvest, the [rule concludes](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2004-04-21/html/04-8934.htm), "persists and remains a serious threat to all sturgeon species." For a price anchor, it records that "In 1995, the retail price for one pound of beluga caviar in the United States was US$1,000", and that by the time of the rule "beluga caviar sells for around US$1,500 per pound on the U.S. retail market".

## Hundred-year-old beluga were once common

That rule also records what the fish used to be. During the early 20th century, the [rule says](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2004-04-21/html/04-8934.htm), 100-year-old beluga sturgeon "were commonly taken" in the northern Caspian Sea. The oldest fish harvested by the early 2000s ran 50 to 55 years, with an average age under 35, and historic reports put individual fish at up to 6 meters and more than a ton.

The tin on the counter holds roe from a younger animal than the ones that built caviar's reputation. It costs what it costs because sturgeon take years to mature on a farm and as long as two decades or more in the wild, and people took the wild fish and their spawning rivers faster than the fish could replace themselves, in America, on the Danube and in the Caspian. The price is a receipt. Strictly FYI.
