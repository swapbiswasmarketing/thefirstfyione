After colon surgery in Vilnius, patients given decaffeinated coffee had a first bowel movement sooner than those given caffeinated coffee or water. Between January 2013 and December 2014, surgeons at Lithuania's National Cancer Institute randomly assigned cancer patients having part of the colon removed by keyhole surgery to drink [coffee with caffeine, coffee without caffeine, or water](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26445179/), 100 mL three times a day after the operation.

The decaf drinkers needed an average of **3.00 days** to their first bowel movement, against [3.75 days for caffeinated coffee and 4.14 days for water](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26445179/). The trial was measuring how long the bowel stays stalled after an operation, a condition called postoperative ileus, and the authors drew a conclusion that cuts against the usual answer to why coffee sends people to the toilet: ["Caffeine is not a main ingredient affecting the length of postoperative ileus."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26445179/)

## Coffee works the colon, and caffeine is only part of the push

Coffee makes some people need the toilet because it makes the colon contract: in people who feel it, the lower colon grows more active **[within four minutes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2338272/)** of a cup. That seemed too fast for the coffee to have reached the colon, so the Sheffield team that measured it suspected a ["gastrocolonic response"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1378422/), set off by receptors in the stomach or small bowel and carried by nerves or gut hormones. In a 1998 study at the University of Iowa, caffeinated coffee stirred the colon about as much as a **1,000-calorie meal** and [23% more than decaffeinated coffee](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9581985/), though the paper did not find the two coffees significantly different. So caffeine may add to the effect, but decaf can set it off too, which points to something else in the drink.

## Most of the Sheffield volunteers reported no urge

In a paper published in 1990, three researchers at the Royal Hallamshire Hospital in Sheffield reported [handing out questionnaires](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1378422/) on bowel habits and the drinks that affected them to 120 students aged 17 to 27, and 99 came back. Seven were [excluded from the count](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1378422/), for reasons such as a past peptic ulcer, not drinking coffee or an incomplete form, and of the 58 men and 34 women left, only 29% said coffee brought on a desire to defecate within 20 minutes: 53% of the women and 19% of the men.

The team then placed pressure sensors in the rectosigmoid, where the colon meets the rectum, of 14 people, 8 of whom said coffee affected them, and gave each black, unsweetened coffee. In those 8, [colon activity rose within four minutes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2338272/) of both regular and decaffeinated coffee, and the rise after coffee lasted at least 30 minutes, while the other six showed no rise. Hot water, served at the same 45 degrees C, did not raise the group's average activity, though the paper's [own chart of each responder](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1378422/) shows it climbing in two of the eight.

Warmth may still play a part. In a 2005 study at Erasmus Medical Centre in Rotterdam, warm water on its own raised rectal tone by [30%, against 45% for strong coffee](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16109656/), and the two effects were not significantly different.

## An Iowa probe put coffee beside a 1,000-calorie meal

Researchers at the University of Iowa threaded a six-sensor probe as far as the transverse colon in 12 healthy subjects. Over 10 hours the next day, each received [240 ml of three drinks at 45 degrees C](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9581985/), black Colombian coffee with 150 mg of caffeine, decaf and water, in random order, plus a 1,000-calorie meal.

| Stimulus | Colonic activity in the Iowa study |
|---|---|
| Caffeinated coffee | Similar to the meal, 60% stronger than water, 23% stronger than decaf |
| Decaffeinated coffee | Not significantly different from water or caffeinated coffee; lower than the meal |
| 1,000-calorie meal | Stronger than water and than decaf |
| Water | The baseline the others were measured against |

The Iowa report is not consistent about decaf. It lists decaffeinated coffee alongside caffeinated coffee and the meal as drinks and food that [induced more activity in the colon](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9581985/) than water, and then reports that its effects were not significantly different from water's.

## Decaf also triggers gut hormones

Coffee does other things to the gut that survive decaffeination, at least in part. In a 1986 study of 10 healthy volunteers, Acquaviva and colleagues found that 100 ml of regular or decaffeinated coffee raised the hormone gastrin, with mean outputs [2.3 and 1.7 times](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3745848/) those after the control drink of sugared water. They put the effect down to ["some other unidentified ingredient."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3745848/)

In a Leiden study published in 1990, six healthy regular coffee drinkers were given 165 mL of regular coffee, decaf or a salt solution. The gallbladder contracted by an average of [33% after regular coffee and 29% after decaffeinated](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2393014/), against 10% after the salt solution, and both coffees raised the hormone cholecystokinin. Neither study measured the colon.

A 1999 review from University Hospital Utrecht, by Boekema and three colleagues, argued that because coffee contains no calories, and its effects on the gut cannot be put down to volume, acidity or osmolality (how concentrated the drink is), ["it must have pharmacological effects."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10499460/)

> "Caffeine cannot solely account for these gastrointestinal effects." - [Boekema, Samsom, van Berge Henegouwen and Smout, 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10499460/)

## Caffeine on its own: two clean trials, two answers

Surgeons in St Gallen, Switzerland, set out to show that caffeine was the active part. After a 2012 trial led by Muller found that patients given coffee after colon surgery had their first bowel movement after a mean of [about 60 hours against 74 for water](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22987303/), a St Gallen team including Muller wrote in a 2016 trial protocol that ["caffeine seems to be the most likely stimulating agent."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27044596/) The protocol planned for [180 patients, with an interim analysis after 60](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27044596/).

The trial, called CaCo, reported on sixty patients given capsules of 100 mg or 200 mg of caffeine, or a corn-starch placebo, three times a day after keyhole colon surgery. The mean times to first bowel movement came out at [68.2 hours, 67.9 hours and 67.3 hours](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35909263/) respectively, and the authors concluded that ["Caffeine was not associated with reduced time to first bowel movement."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35909263/)

A double-blinded trial in Jerusalem, published in 2021, found the opposite: patients given 100 mg of caffeine three times a day after keyhole colon surgery passed their first stool [18 hours sooner](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33900493/) than those given a placebo.

Trials of coffee itself after surgery disagree too. Unlike the Vilnius trial, a three-armed trial in Los Angeles, published in 2023, found that coffee, [caffeinated or decaffeinated, "does not expedite the return of bowel function"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37470901/) after minimally invasive operations. A gut restarting after surgery is also a different test from a morning cup.

So the popular answer, that coffee is a laxative because of its caffeine, holds only in part. Caffeinated coffee came out ahead [in the Iowa measurements](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9581985/), though not by a margin the paper counted as significant, and the Sheffield lab subjects who said coffee gave them the urge responded to decaf too. None of these papers pins down what else in the cup does the rest of the work. Strictly FYI.
