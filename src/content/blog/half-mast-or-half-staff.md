The rule everyone repeats is that half-mast belongs to ships and half-staff belongs to land. Then the British government lowers a flag over a stone building with no mast anywhere near it, and its own [designated days guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/designated-days-for-union-flag-flying) logs the change as half-masting.

That guidance never once says half-staff, and the American law governing the practice never says half-mast. Neither word is a mistake. Both were English before there was a United States, and each side of the Atlantic wrote one of them into its rules.

## Why is it called half mast instead of half staff?

Both half-mast and half-staff are correct. Half-staff is the American legal term: [4 U.S.C. 7](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/4/7), the Flag Code section titled Position and manner of display, uses it throughout, never says half-mast, and carries in its notes a 1954 proclamation that puts flags at half-staff on "all buildings, grounds, and naval vessels of the Federal Government". American law, in other words, uses half-staff on ships too. Half-mast is the British form, and the one the UK government's own guidance uses.

[Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/half-mast) dates the phrase half-mast to the 1620s, within two decades of the lowering described below. Half-staff is not an American coinage either. In his diary entry for 30 January 1675-6 (January 1676 in modern reckoning), the naval chaplain [Henry Teonge](https://archive.org/details/diaryofhenryteon00teon) recorded that his ship marked "the day of our King's marterdome" with "our jacks and flaggs only halfe staff high". The king was Charles I, and the Admiralty Librarian William Gordon Perrin notes in [British Flags](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/46370) that after the Restoration the navy observed the anniversary of his execution in a similar manner.

Which of the two words sounds like the sloppy modern version depends entirely on where you grew up.

## Nobody wrote down why it started

The custom is roughly four centuries old and enters the record already in progress. In July 1612 an English expedition was working the west coast of Greenland when its commander, **James Hall**, was speared. The [Dictionary of Canadian Biography](https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/hall_james_1E.html) dates the attack to **22 July** in Rammel's, or Amerdlok, Fjord, says he died the next day, and describes the voyage as mineral prospecting and trade.

When the Heart's Ease rejoined her consort the Patience, the quartermaster [John Gatonbe](https://archive.org/details/voyagesofwilliam00markrich) wrote that she came in with "her flag hanging down" and her ancient, or ensign, hanging over the poop, "which was a sign of death." Perrin, in [British Flags](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/46370), called it "The earliest instance in which I have met it" and added that the ship lowered flag and ensign again on entering the Thames two months later, "in token and sign of the death of Mr Hall".

One contributor to the volunteer flag archive [Flags of the World](https://www.crwflags.com/fotw/flags/xf-half.html), David Prothero, offers the least romantic reason the custom began at sea: in the Middle Ages, he suggested in 1998, tall poles with a suitable hoisting mechanism were probably found only on ships.

## The invisible flag of death is a modern explanation

Many pages on the subject supply the same cause. The flag is lowered, they say, to leave room for an invisible flag of death to fly above it.

A quieter variant says the gap marks the passing of the authority the flag represents, and that one has a named author. Perrin offered it in [British Flags](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/46370) only as a guess: "We may conjecture that the original signification of the lowered flag was the passing away of the authority which that flag connoted."

Prothero, in the same post on [Flags of the World](https://www.crwflags.com/fotw/flags/xf-half.html), called both stories **modern attempts to explain a practice which has much older origins**. Neither Gatonbe's journal nor Perrin's history mentions an invisible flag.

So the gesture is older than every surviving explanation of it, and a tidy origin story attached itself afterwards. A naval explanation is duller and at least names a mechanism. [Flags of the World](https://www.crwflags.com/fotw/flags/xf-half.html) quotes Leland P. Lovette's 1934 US Naval Institute book Naval Customs, which calls half-masting **a survival of the days when a slovenly appearance** characterized mourning, alongside slack rigging and yards left askew.

## The flag goes all the way to the top first

[4 U.S.C. 7](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/4/7) sets out the choreography, and its half-staff rules begin by sending the flag the wrong way. The flag, when flown at half-staff, **should be first hoisted to the peak for an instant and then lowered to the half-staff position**. The same subsection then says it **should be again raised to the peak before it is lowered for the day**.

> A flag at half-staff is not supposed to be run straight up to the middle of the pole. The code sends it to the very top first, for an instant, then down - and back to the top once more before it is taken in for the night.

Both trips to the top happen on days when the flag otherwise flies below the peak. The statute sets out the sequence and gives no reason for it.

British practice runs the same way. When Prince Philip died in April 2021, the [Flag Institute](https://www.flaginstitute.org/wp/flags-news/half-mast-for-hrh-the-duke-of-edinburgh/) told flag-flyers to "first raise it to the top of the flagpole, hold it there for a second, then lower it to the correct position", and gave no reason either.

## Half-mast is not always halfway

American law reads the word literally. [4 U.S.C. 7](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/4/7) defines half-staff as **the position of the flag when it is one-half the distance between the top and bottom of the staff**. [Canada's federal guidance](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/culture/canadian-identity-society/anthems-symbols/national-flag/halfmasting/etiquette-times-mourning.html) is just as literal: **the centre of the flag should be exactly halfway down the flag pole**.

The dictionary is looser. [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/half-mast) defines half-mast as **a point some distance but not necessarily halfway down below the top of a mast or staff**. British practice stops higher: the same Flag Institute notice puts British half-mast at **two-thirds of the way up the flagpole**, with at least the height of the flag left between the top of the flag and the top of the pole.

New Zealand's flag booklet, quoted on [Flags of the World](https://www.crwflags.com/fotw/flags/xf-half.html), sets the same minimum: the flag **should always be more than its own depth from the top of the flagpole**, low enough to avoid the appearance of a flag which has accidentally fallen away from the top.

## The clock is written into the statute

How long a flag stays down is fixed by office in the same section, not left to judgement on the day.

| Death of | Flag at half-staff for |
|---|---|
| President or former President | 30 days |
| Vice President, Chief Justice, retired Chief Justice, Speaker of the House | 10 days |
| Associate Justice, Secretary of an executive or military department, former Vice President, Governor of a State, territory, or possession | Until interment |
| Member of Congress | Day of death and the day after |

Memorial Day is the clause that catches people out. Under [4 U.S.C. 7](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/4/7) the flag should be displayed at half-staff **until noon only**, then raised to the top of the staff for the rest of the day, so a flag left down all afternoon is out of step with the code.

[Another statute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/36/129) designates December 7 as [National Pearl Harbor Remembrance Day](/blog/why-did-japan-attack-pearl-harbor/) and asks the president to issue a proclamation each year calling for flags at half-staff, "in honor of the individuals who died as a result of their service at Pearl Harbor." [Veterans Day](/blog/why-do-we-celebrate-veterans-day/), which the [Department of Veterans Affairs](https://department.va.gov/veterans-day/facts-and-information/) says is largely intended to thank living veterans, is a display day in [4 U.S.C. 6](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/4/6) with no half-staff rule at all.

Both words are right, then, and the better question is the one sitting underneath the one people ask. Four centuries of lowered flags, a federal statute specifying the exact order of hoists, and no surviving record of who first did it or why a lowered flag came to mean death. The gesture is understood everywhere and explained by no one who was there. Strictly FYI.
