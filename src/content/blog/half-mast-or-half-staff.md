The rule everyone repeats is that half-mast belongs to ships and half-staff belongs to land. Then the British government lowers a flag over a stone building with no mast anywhere near it, and its own [designated days guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/designated-days-for-union-flag-flying) logs the change as half-masting.

That guidance never once says half-staff, and the American law governing the practice never says half-mast. Neither word is a mistake. One idea crossed an ocean and came back with two names.

## Why is it called half mast instead of half staff?

Both half-mast and half-staff are correct. Half-staff is American English, and it is the only form appearing in [4 U.S.C. 7](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/4/7), the US Flag Code section on display. Half-mast is the British form, and the only one the UK government's own guidance uses. No statute assigns one word to sea and the other to land. Family trees have the reverse problem: one dictionary gives [second cousin](/blog/what-is-a-second-cousin/) two senses that name two different relatives, and genealogy accepts only the first.

[Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/half-mast) dates the phrase half-mast to the 1620s, within a decade of the earliest lowering anyone has found. Which of the two sounds like the sloppy modern version depends entirely on where you grew up, the same trick that fools people about [writing Christmas with an X](/blog/why-do-people-say-xmas/).

## Nobody wrote down why it started

The custom is roughly four centuries old and enters the record already in progress. The volunteer flag archive [Flags of the World](https://www.crwflags.com/fotw/flags/xf-half.html) heads its earliest citation **First(?) recorded instance, July 1612**, and the question mark is theirs.

That July an English expedition was working the west coast of Greenland when its commander, **James Hall**, was speared and killed. The [Dictionary of Canadian Biography](https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/hall_james_1E.html) dates the attack to **22 July** in Rammel's, or Amerdlok, Fjord, and describes the voyage as mineral prospecting and trade rather than the North-West Passage search that Flags of the World attaches to it. When the Heart's Ease rejoined her consort the Patience, she came in with her flag hanging down and her ensign hanging over the poop, which the account calls a sign of death.

Flags of the World also offers the least romantic reason the custom began at sea: tall poles with a suitable hoisting mechanism were probably found only on ships. There was nowhere on land to half-mast anything.

## The invisible flag of death is a modern explanation

Almost every page on the subject supplies the same cause. The flag is lowered, they say, to leave room for an invisible flag of death to fly above it. A quieter variant says the gap marks the passing of the authority the flag represents.

Flags of the World, which collects flag documentation rather than flag folklore, says both stories **are modern attempts to explain a practice which has much older origins**. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-mast) carries the invisible-flag line too, attributed to "some sources" rather than to any document.

So the gesture is older than every surviving explanation of it, and a tidy origin story attached itself afterwards. The same thing happened to [the thing people say after a sneeze](/blog/why-we-say-bless-you/), whose plague story nobody can source either. The oldest suggestion on offer here is duller and at least has a mechanism attached. Flags of the World quotes the 1934 US Naval Institute handbook Naval Customs, which calls half-masting **a survival of the days when a slovenly appearance** characterized mourning, alongside slack rigging and yards left askew.

## The flag goes all the way to the top first

[4 U.S.C. 7](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/4/7) sets out the choreography in detail, and the first step surprises almost everyone. The flag, when flown at half-staff, **should be first hoisted to the peak for an instant and then lowered to the half-staff position**. The same subsection then says it **should be again raised to the peak before it is lowered for the day**.

> A flag at half-staff is never run straight up to the middle of the pole. The code sends it to the very top first, for an instant, then down - and back to the top once more before it is taken in for the night.

Both movements happen on days when the flag will spend every visible minute below the peak. The statute sets out the sequence and gives no reason for it.

## Half-mast is rarely halfway

American law reads the word literally. Half-staff is defined as **the position of the flag when it is one-half the distance between the top and bottom of the staff**. Almost nowhere else means it that way.

[Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-mast) records the common recommendation that a flag at half-mast be lowered **only as much as the hoist, or width, of the flag**. Flags of the World states the same rule from the other end: the flag **should always be more than its own depth from the top of the flagpole**, low enough that it does not read as one that has slipped its halyard.

## The clock is written into the statute

How long a flag stays down is fixed by office in the same section, not left to judgement on the day.

| Death of | Flag at half-staff for |
|---|---|
| President or former President | 30 days |
| Vice President, Chief Justice, retired Chief Justice, Speaker of the House | 10 days |
| Associate Justice, Cabinet secretary, former Vice President, state governor | Until interment |
| Member of Congress | Day of death and the day after |

Memorial Day is the clause that catches people out. The flag should be displayed at half-staff **until noon only**, then raised to the top of the staff for the rest of the day, so a flag left down all afternoon is out of step with the code.

Rules this specific usually leave a paper trail, even when the trail ends somewhere flatter than the legend. That gap between the story and the record turns up in subjects with nothing else in common, including [what actually puts caviar out of most people's reach](/blog/why-is-caviar-so-expensive/).

Both words are right, then, and the better question is the one sitting underneath the one people ask. Four centuries of lowered flags, a federal statute specifying the exact order of hoists, and no surviving record of who first did it or what they meant by it. The gesture is understood everywhere and explained nowhere. Strictly FYI.
