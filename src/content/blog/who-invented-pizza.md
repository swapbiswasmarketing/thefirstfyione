Pizzas were among the dues on a watermill by the Garigliano river, under a lease that Bernard, bishop-elect of Gaeta and son of a duke named Marinus, granted to a married couple, Merco and Fasana, in May 997. The lease, printed from the cathedral archive in the [Codex diplomaticus Cajetanus](https://archive.org/details/00073451bsb/page/n198/mode/1up), charged **one hundred modia of grain** a year for the mill, more grain for the estate beside it and a third of the wine from its vineyards. On top of that, every Christmas, the couple owed the bishop "duodecim pizze et una spatula de porco; et unum lumbulum," in the words of [the printed lease](https://archive.org/details/00073451bsb/page/n200/mode/1up).

In a study due to be presented in February 2015, the food historian Giuseppe Nocca offered the lease as the word's first written appearance and rendered those Christmas dues as "dodici pizze, una spalla di maiale e un rognone," twelve pizzas, a pork shoulder and a kidney, [la Repubblica reported](https://web.archive.org/web/20211116203026/https://napoli.repubblica.it/cronaca/2015/02/09/news/sorpresa_la_parola_pizza_nata_a_gaeta-106914635/). At Easter came "duodecim pizze et unum parium de pulli," twelve more pizzas and a pair of chickens, in [the same lease](https://archive.org/details/00073451bsb/page/n200/mode/1up). La Repubblica's headline had the word born at Gaeta, but [the report](https://web.archive.org/web/20211116203026/https://napoli.repubblica.it/cronaca/2015/02/09/news/sorpresa_la_parola_pizza_nata_a_gaeta-106914635/) calls the lease only "prima evidenza scritta," first written evidence, not proof.

The lease says nothing about what Merco and Fasana baked. Whatever it was, it carried no tomato: the plant was domesticated in Mesoamerica, and the Italian botanist Mattioli was describing its fruits in Europe [in 1544](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9162183/).

## Who invented pizza?

Nobody invented pizza. The word's first known written appearance is [a Latin lease from Gaeta in 997](https://web.archive.org/web/20211116203026/https://napoli.repubblica.it/cronaca/2015/02/09/news/sorpresa_la_parola_pizza_nata_a_gaeta-106914635/), and the modern dish took shape in the streets and shops of Naples, which already had [54 pizzerias in 1807](https://lavocedinewyork.com/food/cucina-italiana/2020/04/23/effetto-cornavirus-a-napoli-e-il-divieto-di-pizza-storia-medica-di-un-cibo-sicuro). Raffaele Esposito, the man often credited, owes that credit to [a popular legend](https://scottspizzatours.com/blog/today-is-the-125th-anniversary-of-the-pizza) about a pizza for Queen Margherita in **1889**.

The paper trail runs like this:

| 1807 | [Records in the State Archive of Naples](https://lavocedinewyork.com/food/cucina-italiana/2020/04/23/effetto-cornavirus-a-napoli-e-il-divieto-di-pizza-storia-medica-di-un-cibo-sicuro) | 54 pizzerias, with owners and addresses |

| 1889 | [The letter at Pizzeria Brandi](https://scottspizzatours.com/blog/today-is-the-125th-anniversary-of-the-pizza) | Three kinds of pizza, toppings unnamed |

## The flatbread is ancient, the tomato is not

Baked dough with something on it is far older than the word. Greeks baked plakous, and the comic poet Antiphanes made a riddle of it, preserved by Athenaeus: drops from bleating goats and "streams proceeding from the yellow bee," set on a broad base given by "the chaste virgin born of holy Ceres," until the other speaker begs him to just say "a cheesecake," in [Yonge's Victorian translation](https://archive.org/details/deipnosophistsor02athe). The word pizza itself has no settled origin; Italy's Treccani dictionary notes that it was already in medieval Latin and suggests Old High German bizzo or pizzo, "boccone, pezzo di pane, focaccia," [a mouthful, a piece of bread, a flatbread](https://www.treccani.it/vocabolario/pizza/). In 2023, archaeologists at Pompeii uncovered a fresco of a flatbread, painted next to a wine goblet, that Italy's culture ministry said ["may be a distant ancestor of the modern dish"](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-66031341), though it lacks the classic ingredients to count as a pizza.

What made the Naples dish new was a fruit from the Americas, and when tomato first went onto the dough is disputed. Carol Helstosky's *Pizza: A Global History* notes that the Associazione Verace Pizza Napoletana dates the pizza marinara to 1734, while "others argue that the tomato was not commonly used on pizza until the early nineteenth century," [she writes](https://archive.org/details/pizzaglobalhisto0000hels). In the paper trail above, it first shows up among the toppings Dumas listed, with no name attached. When UNESCO honored pizza in 2017, it inscribed a skill, [the art of the Neapolitan pizzaiuolo](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/art-of-neapolitan-pizzaiuolo-00722), or pizza maker, as intangible cultural heritage.

## Naples ate pizza in the street long before anyone took credit

Documents in the State Archive of Naples list **54 pizzerias** in the city in 1807, "con nome, cognome e indirizzo," with each owner's name and address, [writes Donatella Mattozzi](https://lavocedinewyork.com/food/cucina-italiana/2020/04/23/effetto-cornavirus-a-napoli-e-il-divieto-di-pizza-storia-medica-di-un-cibo-sicuro), whose article draws on Antonio Mattozzi's history *Inventing the Pizzeria*. Some had rooms to sit in. Francesco De Sanctis, born in 1817, remembered that at sixteen he and his friends would go in the evening "a mangiare la pizza in certe stanze al largo della Carita," to eat pizza in certain rooms on the Largo della Carita, in [his memoir of his youth](https://archive.org/details/lagiovinezzadifr00desauoft).

Alexandre Dumas, writing up his travels in Naples in [*Le Corricolo*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/9262), said the Naples lazzarone, the city's street poor, "ne mange en general que deux choses: la pizza et le cocomero," eats in general only two things, pizza and watermelon. The pizza came in kinds, and [his list](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/9262) ran "la pizza est a l'huile, la pizza est au lard, la pizza est au saindoux, la pizza est au fromage, la pizza est aux tomates, la pizza est aux petits poissons," with oil, bacon, lard, cheese, tomatoes or small fish. Its price rose and fell with those ingredients, so [he called it](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/9262) "le thermometre gastronomique du marche," the market's food thermometer.

## A Naples writer defined pizza as whatever you put on it

Emmanuele Rocco's sketch of the pizza maker, "Il pizzajuolo," ran in the second volume of Francesco De Bourcard's illustrated survey of Naples customs. That volume has [1858 on its title page](https://archive.org/details/rcin.org.pl.WA248_143354_F-24166-2_neapol2_o_116709/page/n8/mode/1up), but a Polish Academy of Sciences library that holds the scanned copy records [1866 on its cover](https://rcin.org.pl/dlibra/doccontent?id=116709), and an essay printed before Rocco's already refers to [Vesuvius in 1859](https://archive.org/details/rcin.org.pl.WA248_143354_F-24166-2_neapol2_o_116709/page/n136/mode/1up), so his pages went to press between 1859 and 1866. Pizza was missing from the Crusca dictionary, [Rocco noted](https://archive.org/details/rcin.org.pl.WA248_143354_F-24166-2_neapol2_o_116709/page/n171/mode/1up), partly because it was "una specialita dei napoletani, anzi della citta di Napoli," a specialty of the Neapolitans, or rather of the city of Naples.

His recipe is a single sentence. [Stretch a piece of dough](https://archive.org/details/rcin.org.pl.WA248_143354_F-24166-2_neapol2_o_116709/page/n171/mode/1up) with a rolling pin or the palms of your hands, "metteteci sopra quel che vi viene in testa," put on it whatever comes into your head, dress it with oil or lard, bake it, eat it, "e saprete che cosa e una pizza," and you will know what a pizza is.

Then he listed the usual toppings. The plainest pizza had oil, salt, oregano and garlic; others had grated cheese, lard and a few basil leaves, and on those often went "delle sottili fette di muzzarella," thin slices of mozzarella, [Rocco wrote](https://archive.org/details/rcin.org.pl.WA248_143354_F-24166-2_neapol2_o_116709/page/n171/mode/1up), while ham, tomato and clams appeared now and then. Every ingredient of a Margherita was on Naples pizzas by the time his sketch went to press, though his list never puts the trio on a single pizza.

## The queen's pizza rests on one doubted letter

The popular story, as the historian Zachary Nowak retold it for BBC Food before taking it apart, has the queen visiting Naples in 1889, tired of French food, and summoning Raffaele Esposito, the city's most famous pizza maker. Of his three pizzas, "She did not like the one with garlic (pizza marinara) or anchovies (pizza Napoli), but she loved the pizza with tomato sauce, mozzarella and a sprig of basil," [Nowak wrote](https://web.archive.org/web/20121231225517/http://www.bbc.co.uk/food/0/20515123), the colors of the Italian flag. Esposito named it for her, and her chamberlain's thank-you note still hangs in his old shop, now Pizzeria Brandi.

Nowak went looking in the Naples archives. The royal palace's papers have "no record of a letter from Galli, or to Esposito, that day," [he reported](https://web.archive.org/web/20121231225517/http://www.bbc.co.uk/food/0/20515123); the Raffaele Esposito who won permission to use the royal seal got it in 1871, for a shop selling wine and spirits; and an 1891 letter by the real Camillo Galli is in a different hand. No newspaper covered a royal pizza night at the time, notes the New York pizza-tour guide [Scott Wiener](https://www.scottspizzatours.com/blog/the-real-story-of-pizza-margherita/). Nowak set out the full case in a 2014 paper, ["Folklore, Fakelore, History"](https://doi.org/10.2752/175174414X13828682779249), in the journal *Food, Culture & Society*.

The letter, dated [11 June 1889](https://scottspizzatours.com/blog/today-is-the-125th-anniversary-of-the-pizza), is short, and Wiener published a translation of it:

> "Most esteemed Mr Raffaele Esposito Brandi. I confirm to you that the three kinds of pizza you prepared for Her Majesty the queen were found to be delicious." - Galli Camillo, Head of Table Services to the Royal Household, 1889, as translated by [Scott's Pizza Tours](https://www.scottspizzatours.com/blog/the-real-story-of-pizza-margherita/)

It names no topping and no flag. Its seal is rubber-stamped at the bottom, where the palace's real letters of the day had seals printed at the top left, and it calls the pizza maker Raffaele Esposito Brandi, adding his wife's surname, which Italian men did not take, [Nowak found](https://web.archive.org/web/20121231225517/http://www.bbc.co.uk/food/0/20515123). The Brandi brothers, nephews of Esposito's wife, had bought the pizzeria in 1932, and Nowak reads the letter as their marketing: its opening line is his "proof that the letter was composed in the 1930s and not the 1880s," [he wrote](https://web.archive.org/web/20121231225517/http://www.bbc.co.uk/food/0/20515123).

A queen with a taste for pizza was already news nine years earlier, before Esposito had even [purchased his pizzeria in 1883](https://www.scottspizzatours.com/blog/the-real-story-of-pizza-margherita/). A Geneva Gazette story, reprinted by the Washington Post on July 25, 1880, had Queen Margherita winning popularity in Naples by eating pizza, "a sort of cake beaten flat in a round form, and seasoned with various condiments," and had a famous pizza maker bring her "a menu of 35 different choices" at Capodimonte, [Wiener reports](https://www.scottspizzatours.com/blog/the-real-story-of-pizza-margherita/).

Wiener allows that the royal pizza night [may have happened](https://scottspizzatours.com/blog/today-is-the-125th-anniversary-of-the-pizza), but this letter cannot prove it.

The oldest pizzas on record were a bishop's dues: twelve of them, owed every Christmas under a lease made at Gaeta in 997, with a pork shoulder on the side. Nobody signed for inventing pizza, and the royal letter behind the Margherita never says what went on one. Strictly FYI.
