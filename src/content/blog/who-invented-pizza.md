Pizzas were among the dues on a watermill by the Garigliano river, under a lease that Bernard, bishop-elect of Gaeta and son of a duke named Marinus, granted to a married couple, Merco and Fasana, in May 997. The lease, printed from the cathedral archive in the [Codex diplomaticus Cajetanus](https://archive.org/details/00073451bsb/page/n198/mode/1up), charged **one hundred modia of grain** a year for the mill, more grain for the estate beside it and a third of the wine from its vineyards. On top of that, every Christmas, the couple owed the bishop "duodecim pizze et una spatula de porco; et unum lumbulum," in the words of [the printed lease](https://archive.org/details/00073451bsb/page/n200/mode/1up).

That is "twelve pizzas, a pork shoulder, and a pork kidney on Christmas Day," in [Wikipedia's translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pizza), with twelve more pizzas and a couple of chickens at Easter. The lease is thought to be [the oldest recorded usage of the word pizza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pizza), and it says nothing about what Merco and Fasana baked. Whatever it was, it carried no tomato, which [could not have been added before the 16th century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pizza) and the Columbian Exchange.

## Who invented pizza?

Nobody invented pizza. The word was first recorded in [a Latin manuscript from Gaeta in 997](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pizza), and modern pizza [evolved from similar flatbread dishes in Naples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pizza) in the 18th or early 19th century. Raffaele Esposito, the man often credited, owes that credit to [a popular legend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pizza_Margherita) about a pizza for Queen Margherita in **1889**.

The paper trail runs like this:

| Year | Record | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| 997 | [A lease in Gaeta](https://archive.org/details/00073451bsb/page/n200/mode/1up) | The word pizza, as dues owed to a bishop |
| 1807 | [A list in the State Archive of Naples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_pizza) | 54 pizzerias, with owners and addresses |
| 1830 | [Antica Pizzeria Port'Alba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_pizza) | Tables for eating pizza on the spot |
| 1843 | [Alexandre Dumas on Naples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_pizza) | Many toppings, tomatoes among them |
| 1859-66 | [Emmanuele Rocco, in De Bourcard's survey of Naples](https://archive.org/details/rcin.org.pl.WA248_143354_F-24166-2_neapol2_o_116709/page/n171/mode/1up) | Basil, mozzarella and tomato as toppings |
| 1889 | [The letter at Pizzeria Brandi](https://www.scottspizzatours.com/blog/the-real-story-of-pizza-margherita/) | Three pizzas, toppings unnamed |

## The flatbread is ancient, the tomato is not

Flat baked dough is far older than the word. Greeks baked plakous, [a flat cake that ancient writers describe as a dessert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Placenta_cake) of flour, goat's cheese and honey, and one suggested source of the word pizza is [Byzantine Greek and Late Latin pitta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pizza). In 2023, archaeologists at Pompeii uncovered a fresco of a flatbread, painted next to a wine goblet, that Italy's culture ministry said ["may be a distant ancestor of the modern dish"](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-europe-66031341), though it lacks the classic ingredients to count as a pizza.

What made the Naples dish new was a fruit from the Americas, and when tomato first went onto the dough is uncertain, with "many conflicting claims," [Wikipedia's pizza article says](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pizza). In the paper trail above it first shows up in the toppings Dumas listed, with no name attached to the step. When UNESCO honored pizza in 2017, it listed a skill, [the art of making Neapolitan pizza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pizza), as intangible cultural heritage.

## Naples ate pizza in the street long before anyone took credit

According to documents that historian Antonio Mattozzi found in the State Archive of Naples, the city had [**54 pizzerias** in 1807](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_pizza). Pizza was [sold from open-air stands and out of pizza bakeries until about 1830](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pizza), when what is usually counted as the first pizzeria with tables to sit at, [Antica Pizzeria Port'Alba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_pizza), opened. Nigiri sushi started out the same way, as [street food in Edo](/blog/where-did-sushi-originate/), in the same decades.

Alexandre Dumas, writing up his travels in Naples in [*Le Corricolo*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/9262), said the Naples lazzarone, the city's street poor, "ne mange en general que deux choses: la pizza et le cocomero," eats in general only two things, pizza and watermelon. The pizza came in kinds, and his list ran "la pizza est a l'huile, la pizza est au lard, la pizza est au saindoux, la pizza est au fromage, la pizza est aux tomates, la pizza est aux petits poissons," with oil, bacon, lard, cheese, tomatoes or small fish, [in the same chapter](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/9262). Its price rose and fell with those ingredients, so [he called it](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/9262) "le thermometre gastronomique du marche," the market's food thermometer, in a description that Wikipedia's history of pizza [dates to 1843](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_pizza).

## A Naples writer defined pizza as whatever you put on it

Emmanuele Rocco's sketch of the pizza maker, "Il pizzajuolo," ran in the second volume of Francesco De Bourcard's illustrated survey of Naples customs. That volume has [1858 on its title page](https://archive.org/details/rcin.org.pl.WA248_143354_F-24166-2_neapol2_o_116709/page/n8/mode/1up), but [Italian Wikipedia dates it to 1866](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Usi_e_costumi_di_Napoli_e_contorni_descritti_e_dipinti), and the essay printed straight after Rocco's opens on [the eve of 15 August 1859](https://archive.org/details/rcin.org.pl.WA248_143354_F-24166-2_neapol2_o_116709/page/n178/mode/1up), so his pages went to press between 1859 and 1866. Pizza was missing from the Crusca dictionary, [Rocco noted](https://archive.org/details/rcin.org.pl.WA248_143354_F-24166-2_neapol2_o_116709/page/n171/mode/1up), partly because it was "una specialita dei napoletani, anzi della citta di Napoli," a specialty of the Neapolitans, or rather of the city of Naples.

His recipe [is a single sentence](https://archive.org/details/rcin.org.pl.WA248_143354_F-24166-2_neapol2_o_116709/page/n171/mode/1up). Stretch a piece of dough with a rolling pin or the palms of your hands, "metteteci sopra quel che vi viene in testa," put on it whatever comes into your head, dress it with oil or lard, bake it, eat it, "e saprete che cosa e una pizza," and you will know what a pizza is.

Then he listed the usual toppings. The plainest pizza had oil, salt, oregano and garlic; others had grated cheese, lard and a few basil leaves, and on those often went "delle sottili fette di muzzarella," thin slices of mozzarella, [Rocco wrote](https://archive.org/details/rcin.org.pl.WA248_143354_F-24166-2_neapol2_o_116709/page/n171/mode/1up), while ham, tomato and clams appeared now and then. Every ingredient of a Margherita, including [mozzarella, a fresh cheese eaten young](/blog/how-is-cheese-made/), was on Naples pizzas by the time his sketch went to press, though his list never puts the trio on a single pizza.

## The queen's pizza rests on one doubted letter

The popular story says that in June 1889 the Royal Palace of Capodimonte commissioned Raffaele Esposito to make pizza for the visiting Queen Margherita. Of his three pizzas she preferred one in the colors of the Italian flag, red tomato, white mozzarella and green basil, and it was named for her, with a letter of thanks from the queen's "head of service" still displayed in his old shop, now Pizzeria Brandi, [as Wikipedia tells the legend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pizza_Margherita).

Later research undermined the letter, [the same article notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pizza_Margherita), pointing out that "no media of the period reported the supposed visit" and that the story and the name Margherita were first promoted in the 1930s and 1940s. The historian who made that case is Zachary Nowak, in a paper, ["Folklore, Fakelore, History"](https://doi.org/10.2752/175174414X13828682779249), in the journal *Food, Culture & Society*.

The letter itself is short. The New York pizza-tour guide Scott Wiener [published a translation of it](https://www.scottspizzatours.com/blog/the-real-story-of-pizza-margherita/):

> "Most esteemed Mr Raffaele Esposito Brandi. I confirm to you that the three kinds of pizza you prepared for Her Majesty the queen were found to be delicious." - Galli Camillo, Head of Table Services to the Royal Household, 1889, as translated by [Scott's Pizza Tours](https://www.scottspizzatours.com/blog/the-real-story-of-pizza-margherita/)

It names no topping and no flag. Summarizing Nowak, Wiener lists [the problems](https://www.scottspizzatours.com/blog/the-real-story-of-pizza-margherita/): the royal seal is in the wrong place, the signature does not match others on file in Italian archives, and the letter calls the pizza maker Raffaele Esposito Brandi, adding his wife's surname. Nowak suggests the letter may have been made by the Brandi brothers, nephews of Esposito's wife, who [purchased the pizzeria in the 1930s](https://scottspizzatours.com/blog/today-is-the-125th-anniversary-of-the-pizza), as marketing for their business.

A queen with a taste for pizza was already news nine years earlier, before Esposito had even [purchased his pizzeria in 1883](https://www.scottspizzatours.com/blog/the-real-story-of-pizza-margherita/). A Geneva Gazette story, reprinted by the Washington Post on July 25, 1880, had Queen Margherita winning popularity in Naples by eating pizza, "a sort of cake beaten flat in a round form, and seasoned with various condiments," and had a famous pizza maker bring her "a menu of 35 different choices" at Capodimonte, [Wiener reports](https://www.scottspizzatours.com/blog/the-real-story-of-pizza-margherita/).

A food line can attach to a queen without evidence, as [the cake line](/blog/what-does-let-them-eat-cake-mean/) did to Marie Antoinette. Wiener allows that the royal pizza night may have happened, but this letter cannot prove it.

The oldest pizzas on record were a bishop's dues: twelve of them, owed every Christmas under a lease made at Gaeta in 997, with a pork shoulder on the side. Nobody signed for inventing pizza, and the royal letter behind the Margherita never says what went on one. Strictly FYI.
