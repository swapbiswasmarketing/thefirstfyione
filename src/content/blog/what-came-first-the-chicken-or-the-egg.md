**At least 125 million years** separate the first amniotes in the fossil record from the oldest known eggs or eggshells laid on land. That gap is stated outright by Koen Stein and colleagues in [Scientific Reports](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6418122/), and it changes what the riddle is asking.

The answer itself is settled and dull. What has moved is the evidence underneath it. The famous study said to prove the chicken came first never addressed the question, and both ends of the timeline have shifted in the last few years: the first amniotes turn out older, and the first chickens younger, than the figures most people carry into this argument.

## What came first, the chicken or the egg?

The egg came first, by hundreds of millions of years. Amniotes, the group that today includes reptiles, birds and mammals, were leaving footprints [probably more than 354 million years ago](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12119326/), while the earliest confirmed bones of a domestic chicken date to roughly [1650 to 1250 BCE in Thailand](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9214543/). Eggs existed long before any chicken did.

## The 2010 headline was a chemistry paper

In July 2010 the story went round that British scientists had settled it in the chicken's favour. [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna38238685) ran it with a line from Colin Freeman of Sheffield University, quoted from the Daily Mail: "It had long been suspected that the egg came first but now we have the scientific proof that shows that in fact the chicken came first."

The paper under that sentence was Freeman, Harding, Quigley and Rodger, "Structural Control of Crystal Nuclei by an Eggshell Protein," in [Angewandte Chemie International Edition](https://doi.org/10.1002/anie.201000679). Its published abstract opens "Growing a good egg" and reports metadynamics simulations in which the eggshell protein **ovocleidin-17** induces calcite crystals to form out of amorphous calcium carbonate nanoparticles, then proposes a catalytic cycle for the first stages of shell formation.

The study that supposedly settled the riddle was a computer simulation of calcium carbonate crystallising, and its published abstract is about a catalytic cycle inside a hen rather than about which animal arrived first. No fossil is examined. No date is proposed for anything.

## The amniote date moved in 2025

In 2025, John Long and colleagues published "Earliest amniote tracks recalibrate the timeline of tetrapod evolution" in [Nature](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12119326/). A single track-bearing slab from the Snowy Plains Formation, found on the bank of the Broken River at Barjarg, on Taungurung Country in Victoria, Australia, carries footprints with **sharp claw impressions**. Claws, the paper notes, are "a derived character of crown amniotes" and absent in known stem amniotes, so the trackmaker was a true amniote, [most probably a primitive sauropsid](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12119326/).

The rock sits in the early Tournaisian, and the paper puts it [probably 358.9 to 354 million years old](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12119326/). That pushes the likely origin of crown-group amniotes back by at least 35 to 40 million years. Molecular divergence dates from 30 recent studies had clustered around a median of 319 million years, so any page still dating the origin of amniotes to around 319 million years is working from the number this slab displaced.

## The oldest eggshell turns up at least 125 million years late

An amniote is an animal whose embryo develops inside its own set of membranes, and that membrane system is the invention the answer's "egg" refers to. Eggs as such are older again: fish eggs, laid in water, have a fossil record going [at least as far back as the Devonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_egg_fossil). The hard chalky shell the riddle pictures is a separate feature, and in the fossil record a much later one.

Stein's 2019 study puts the oldest known amniote eggs in the Early Jurassic, in the Sinemurian, [195 to 192 million years ago](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6418122/): eggs of three sauropodomorph dinosaurs, Massospondylus, Lufengosaurus and Mussaurus, whose calcareous layer is **100 micrometres or less** over a thick shell membrane. Stein's team read them as thin but rigid.

A 2020 paper in [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-2412-8) led by Mark Norell read the Mussaurus eggs the other way, as originally soft-shelled, and its ancestral-state reconstruction concluded that the first dinosaur egg was soft-shelled, with the calcified, hard-shelled dinosaur egg evolving independently at least three times throughout the Mesozoic. The argument is not closed: in 2022 Seung Choi and colleagues replied in [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-05151-9) that the sauropodomorph eggshell might not be soft after all.

> The egg of the answer and the egg of the riddle are not the same object. Amniotes had been walking the earth for well over a hundred million years by the time of the oldest known egg laid on land.

Stein's team measured that blank stretch at [125 million years](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6418122/) against the amniote dates available in 2019. The Australian trackway stretches it to roughly 160.

## The chicken is about 3,500 years old

In 2022 a team led by Joris Peters and Greger Larson assessed more than 600 archaeological sites across 89 countries for [PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9214543/) and placed the first unambiguous domestic chicken bones at the Neolithic moated site of **Ban Non Wat** in Thailand, dated to about 1650 to 1250 BCE, where more than 95% of the bird remains have been ascribed to *Gallus*. The team ties the timing to farming: the production and storage of rice and millet, they suggest, "may have acted as a magnet" for red junglefowl, with fields and the fallow ground beside them making new habitat.

Peters and Larson's team dismisses the older claims in [PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9214543/): "Previous claims for the presence of chickens in the Yellow River basin 11,000 to 8,000 y ago" are "not supported by either the climatic, palynological, or archaeofaunal records," and a reanalysis of those bones found the remains did not belong to chickens.

A companion paper in [Antiquity](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0003598X21000909/type/journal_article) radiocarbon-dated 23 chicken bones from presumed early contexts. Only five matched their reported phasing, the other 18 came back more recent, and two bones from Galabovo in Bulgaria turned out to be over 3500 years younger than claimed. The team found no evidence for chickens in Europe before the first millennium BC, and concluded the first arrivals were probably kept as exotica and only centuries later treated as food.

## There is no first chicken

The riddle's second move is the definitional dodge, the one that asks whether you meant a chicken's egg specifically. That version assumes a first chicken existed, one bird standing on the far side of a line its parents were on the near side of. No such bird can be pointed to. Domestication is a process, and the boundary between red junglefowl and chicken is a label applied afterwards to a continuum. Even the archaeologists hunting the earliest chickens say [ongoing hybridization between wild red junglefowl](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9214543/) and the birds living in human settlements complicates the task of identifying early poultry husbandry.

The genetics push harder still. Yellow skin in domestic chickens does not trace to the red junglefowl at all. Jonas Eriksson, Greger Larson and colleagues traced the allele to another junglefowl species, most likely the grey junglefowl, *Gallus sonneratii*, and called their result "the first conclusive evidence for a hybrid origin of the domestic chicken" in [PLoS Genetics](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2265484/). The chicken is not the tidy descendant of one wild species, which makes a single founding bird harder to imagine than ever.

So the egg wins, by a margin large enough to stop meaning much. What survives is the shape of the question, which asks which end of a loop came first while handing you a species boundary biology never drew and an eggshell that turns up in the rock some 160 million years after the first amniotes. Both of those are assumptions the riddle carries in, supplied by people who needed it to have two ends. Strictly FYI.
