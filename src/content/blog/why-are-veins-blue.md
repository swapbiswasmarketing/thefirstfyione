The version most of us learn is tidy. Arteries carry bright red blood out to the body, veins carry blue blood back, and the proof is right there on the inside of the wrist, where the vessels under the skin read blue. Anatomy posters print them that way. Textbook diagrams do the same.

Blood is never blue, at any point in that loop. The correction that usually follows, though, does not survive measurement either, and a paper published on 29 January 2026 has the numbers.

## Why are veins blue?

Veins look blue because of contrast, not because anything inside them is blue. Human blood is red at every oxygen level. Red light penetrates deep enough to reach a vein and be absorbed there, so the skin over a vein returns less red than the skin beside it does. Human colour vision reads that local shortfall of red as blue.

## The blood inside a vein is dark red

Human blood is red because red blood cells "get their color from hemoglobin", in the words of the [American Society of Hematology](https://www.hematology.org/resources/patients/blood-basics). Oxygen shifts the shade without leaving the family. Christopher Baird, [associate professor of physics at West Texas A&M University](https://www.wtamu.edu/~cbaird/), sets out the range on [Science Questions with Surprising Answers](https://www.wtamu.edu/~cbaird/sq/2012/12/18/why-are-veins-blue/): blood carrying oxygen is "bright red" and blood that has lost its oxygen is "dark red".

[The 2026 optics paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12904536) opens on the same fact, that "human blood is always red" whatever its oxygenation state. Blood drawn from a vein is dark red, as [Baird notes](https://www.wtamu.edu/~cbaird/sq/2012/12/18/why-are-veins-blue/), and no part of the circuit ever takes the colour the diagram gives it.

## The usual correction is wrong too

Once the blood is ruled out, the blue has to come from the light, and the standard explainer takes its wrong turn right here. [Baird's explainer](https://www.wtamu.edu/~cbaird/sq/2012/12/18/why-are-veins-blue/) states the familiar version: "Skin scatters a lot of the red portion of white light before it can reflect off the blood, leaving the blue light to reflect off the blood and back to our eyes."

Measurement points the other way. Markus Wagner and five co-authors, among them Alwin Kienle, writing in [Biomedical Optics Express](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12904536) in January 2026, photographed artificial vessels buried in synthetic skin and reported that above the vein, "in absolute terms, more red light is reflected than blue light".

> More red light than blue comes back from above the phantom vein. The blue never arrives at your eye as blue. It is manufactured from the difference between that light and the light coming off the skin beside it.

## Depth decides whether you see it; oxygen only shifts the hue

[The team](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12904536) cast silicone phantoms matched to the optical properties of human skin, with cylindrical channels of 2 mm and 4 mm diameter set at 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0 mm below the surface. Those channels were filled with a silicone blood substitute at 70 percent oxygen saturation for veins and 100 percent for arteries, then evaluated with "calibrated photography and hyperspectral imaging".

| Depth below surface | Venous fill, 70% oxygen | Arterial fill, 100% oxygen |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 mm | Darker, more bluish | A violet hue |
| 1.0 mm | The blue of a real vein | Clearly visible; colour not described |
| 1.5 mm | Blue nearly indistinguishable | Blue nearly indistinguishable |
| 2.0 mm | Blue nearly indistinguishable | Blue nearly indistinguishable |

At 1.0 mm the venous phantom produced a blue [the authors](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12904536) call "comparable to observations in real human tissue", and the wider 4 mm channels stayed "somewhat more discernible" at that depth. At 1.5 and 2.0 mm the colour drained out of the image "irrespective of the oxygenation state".

The arterial channels settle the argument. Silicone mixed to mimic fully oxygenated blood, set half a millimetre under the surface, did not photograph red: in [the 2026 paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12904536) the arterial inclusions "exhibited a more violet hue". Oxygenation nudges the hue; depth decides whether there is anything to see.

## Your visual system does the last step

Turning a shortage of red into a blue vein takes an observer. [The 2026 paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12904536) leans on Retinex theory, the account of colour vision in which the brain judges reflectance against its surroundings instead of in absolute terms. "This relative reduction in red reflectance above the vein, compared to its surroundings, creates the spectral contrast that leads to the bluish perception," the authors write.

[The paper's abstract](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12904536) divides the labour without hedging, concluding that "vein blueness does not arise solely from blood's intrinsic absorption but from the complex interplay of tissue optics and visual perception".

Kienle had taken the question into a laboratory once before, in [Applied Optics in 1996](https://opg.optica.org/ao/abstract.cfm?uri=ao-35-7-1151), with a CCD camera and a set of Monte Carlo simulations. He and six co-authors named four things that set a vessel's colour: how skin scatters and absorbs at different wavelengths, the oxygenation state of blood, the diameter and depth of the vessel, and "the visual perception process".

Two honest limits sit in [the paper's own discussion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12904536). The phantoms "do not replicate the layered structure of skin", and the authors warn that "homogeneous phantoms may underestimate the depth and visibility of blood vessels compared to real, layered skin", so the depths in the table are not limits for a real arm. The phantoms also replicated the reflectance of one pale skin type, Fitzpatrick type I, and the authors ask that future work "explore the role of melanin, given its significant effect on the absorption and scattering of visible light".

## Two kinds of blue blood that are real

Blue blood exists, just not in people. Horseshoe crabs run on hemocyanin, which the [Smithsonian's National Zoo](https://nationalzoo.si.edu/animals/horseshoe-crab) says "makes their blood blue", and the [US Fish and Wildlife Service](https://www.fws.gov/species/atlantic-horseshoe-crab-limulus-polyphemus) describes the result exactly: "The straw-colored, copper-based blood turns blue when exposed to high concentrations of oxygen." That blood is straw-coloured until it meets high concentrations of oxygen, which is the reverse of the order the human story assumes.

The other blue blood is a phrase. English took up "blue blood" in 1809, translating the Spanish sangre azul, which the [Online Etymology Dictionary](https://www.etymonline.com/word/blue-blood) records as a claim staked by "certain families of Castile that held themselves uncontaminated by Moorish or Jewish admixture". The dictionary's note on the image behind it lands the joke: "the term probably is from the notion of the visible veins of people of fair complexion". If the dictionary is right, a phrase for aristocratic descent rests on veins showing through pale skin, a contrast effect mistaken for a pedigree.

Roll up a sleeve and the blue is still there, and it will stay there, because nothing about the vein has to change for the effect to work. If your arm behaves like the 2026 phantom, two things are true of that patch at once: more red light leaves it than blue light does, and less red leaves it than leaves the skin on either side. The second fact is the one your eye reports. The blood was dark red the whole time. Strictly FYI.
