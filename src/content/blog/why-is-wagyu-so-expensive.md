Of the 280,162 Wagyu steers graded in Japan in 2025, **71.7 percent** came out A5, the top mark for both meat yield and meat quality, by the yearly counts of the [Japan Meat Grading Association](https://www.jmga.or.jp/rating/). In 1995 the share was **16.9 percent**.

The top grade has become the ordinary outcome for a Wagyu steer in Japan, so the grade's rarity cannot explain the price. The cost of making the beef can.

## Why is wagyu so expensive?

Wagyu is expensive because it is costly to produce. A Japanese fattening farm pays a high price for a calf bred to marble, [about half](https://www.e-stat.go.jp/stat-search/files?page=1&layout=datalist&lid=000001479907) what the finished steer will sell for, then feeds it mostly imported grain for about a year and a half while fat builds inside the muscle.

## A5 is two grades written side by side

The letter grades yield: A goes to carcasses whose estimated yield of usable cuts scores 72 or more on the grading association's formula. The number grades quality on four items, marbling, meat color, firmness and texture, and fat, and the carcass takes its lowest score, under the association's [beef standard](https://www.jmga.or.jp/standard/beef/).

A 5 needs marbling of at least **No. 8** on the 12-step Beef Marbling Standard, and the [grading standard](https://www.jmga.or.jp/standard/beef/) applies to every breed.

Kobe beef does not even need A5. Its [rules](https://www.kobe-niku.jp/en/contents/about/definition.html) ask for purebred Tajima cattle bred, fattened and slaughtered in Hyogo Prefecture, a yield grade of A or B, a quality grade of 4 or better, marbling of No. 6 or higher and a carcass of no more than 499.9 kilograms.

The weight cap suits the Tajima strain, whose carcasses run about 50 kilograms lighter than the national average; farmers believe a bigger animal would lose the breed's fine texture, the [Kobe association](https://www.kobe-niku.jp/en/contents/faq/index.html) says, and only about **5,500** cattle a year are certified as Kobe, under 0.2 percent of the beef Japan eats, a scarcity it counts as part of the price.

## The calf costs half a finished steer before fattening starts

In 2024 the average Wagyu steer sold by a Japanese fattening farm fetched **1,289,233 yen**, according to the agriculture ministry's [production cost survey](https://www.e-stat.go.jp/stat-search/files?page=1&layout=datalist&lid=000001479907). The calf it started as, about nine months old when fattening began, was [valued](https://www.e-stat.go.jp/stat-search/files?page=1&layout=datalist&lid=000001479907) at **690,106 yen**, more than half that.

Add feed, labor and the rest, and the full cost of raising each steer came to **1,375,264 yen** in the [2024 survey](https://www.e-stat.go.jp/stat-search/files?page=1&layout=datalist&lid=000001479907), more than it sold for. In fiscal 2016 [the same survey](https://www.e-stat.go.jp/stat-search/files?page=1&layout=datalist&lid=000001202877) put the full cost at about 87 percent of the sale price.

## A year and a half of mostly imported grain

Japanese Black cattle eat a high-energy diet two or three times a day from 11 months of age until slaughter at 28 to 30 months, with concentrate rising from 36.8 to 86.4 percent of the ration by 18 months, according to a [2018 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6039323/) of the industry led by Takafumi Gotoh of Kagoshima University. Each animal normally goes through **4,000 to 5,000 kilograms** of feed while fattening, and **more than 90 percent of the concentrate is imported**. Holstein cattle, the dairy breed, are normally fattened only until 20 to 22 months, the review adds.

The months matter because fat keeps building. In purebred Japanese Black cattle, fat inside the muscle rises in a straight line with slaughter age from 16.7 months to more than 33.3 months, a [second 2018 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6039335/) notes.

## Japan has bred for more fat since beef imports were liberalized in 1991

Japan liberalized beef imports in 1991, a [2026 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13024740/) in *Foods* records, and breeding has stressed marbling ever since. Farmers and the government set out to produce "increasingly marbled beef" in response to "the serious threat posed by cheaper, imported, leaner beef," the [Gotoh review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6039323/) says; fat inside the loin of Japanese Black cattle has averaged more than 30 percent in recent years, reaching up to 60 percent in sirloin, and rose so fast that the grading association "was forced to further modify the marbling standard" in 2014.

In 2025 the average Japanese Black steer scored **9.2** on the marbling scale, against 2.1 for dairy-breed steers and 4.3 for crossbreds, the [grading association's survey](https://www.jmga.or.jp/rating/) shows.

## The Japanese Black breed dates from 1944

Wagyu means Japanese cattle, [the Kobe association explains](https://www.kobe-niku.jp/en/contents/faq/index.html), and the name covers four breeds: Japanese Black, Brown, Shorthorn and Polled. Ninety percent or more are Japanese Black, according to [JETRO](https://www.jetro.go.jp/en/trends/foods/ingredients/wagyu.html), Japan's trade agency, which calls Wagyu "a breed created by repeated crossing of Japanese native cattle with imported breeds."

In the early 1900s Japan crossed its native cattle with European breeds, among them Braunvieh, Simmental, Ayrshire, Devon, Shorthorn and Holstein, to make bigger draft and beef animals. Meat quality later slipped and the cattle grew too large, so the crossing stopped and the herd was bred within itself until the Japanese Black breed was established in **1944**, the [Gotoh review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6039323/) says.

## Outside Japan, Wagyu grew from a few hundred cattle

The first Wagyu to reach the United States were four bulls, two black and two red, imported by Morris Whitney in 1975, by the account on the [American Wagyu Association](https://wagyu.org/company/awhf/awhf-historic-museum)'s history page, whose photo captions date the bulls to 1976. More shipments followed in the 1990s, and then, the AWA says, the Japanese government banned further exports of Wagyu semen, embryos and live cattle.

The legal picture is murkier. A foreign law specialist at the [Law Library of Congress](https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2019/01/holy-cow-making-sense-of-japanese-wagyu-cow-export-rules/) wrote in 2019 that she "could not find a provision that directly prohibits the export of wagyu sperm," though such exports must clear quarantine, and that the 1990s shipments went ahead against the breed registry's request.

[ALIC](https://alic.go.jp/joho-c/joho05_003476.html), a Japanese government agriculture agency, cites a ministry count of 247 live cattle sent to the United States by 1998, while noting there is no official record, and says none have gone since, after Japan's cattle industry held back and the United States halted imports over a foot-and-mouth outbreak in 2000. In 2020 Japan passed [Act No. 22 of 2020](https://leap.unep.org/en/countries/jp/national-legislation/act-no-22-2020-prevention-unfair-competition-regarding-livestock) on unfair competition over livestock genetic resources. It took effect that October, along with an amendment to the Livestock Improvement Promotion Act, and the agriculture ministry calls the pair the [two wagyu-related laws](https://www.maff.go.jp/chushi/seisan/chikusan/katiku_iden.html); since then its regional offices have been inspecting every artificial insemination station in turn, checking each one's record books and stored semen.

The [AWA's history page](https://wagyu.org/company/awhf/awhf-historic-museum), quoting Wagyu International, counts **221** Wagyu cattle as the genetic base of all Wagyu in the United States, Canada and Australia. For breed, the AWA's USDA-verified [Authentic Wagyu program](https://s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/assets.wagyu.org/Manual-RQMT-001-002.pdf) requires only that a calf have one registered Wagyu parent, sire or dam. Wagyu raised abroad cannot be shipped to Japan and sold there as Wagyu, [JETRO says](https://www.jetro.go.jp/en/trends/foods/ingredients/wagyu.html).

## The massages and the beer are not standard practice

The popular story credits pampering: cattle massaged, fed beer and played music. The [Kobe association](https://www.kobe-niku.jp/en/contents/faq/index.html) allows that some farms rearing low numbers of cattle may be "doing things bordering on this," but says those methods are not the standard for prime Kobe beef. Music at feeding time can become a cue that "it is said, increases their appetite," though its effect on meat quality "has not yet been proved," and "there have been almost no cases of cows being raised on beer."

The association credits massage with lowering stress and so helping the meat indirectly, then adds:

> "Massage itself neither softens meat nor increases the amount of marbling," the [Kobe association](https://www.kobe-niku.jp/en/contents/faq/index.html) says.

The Gotoh review describes a standard system that mentions no massage or music: Japanese Black cattle are "permanently housed in small pens and fed enormous quantities of concentrate," [it says](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6039323/). Its feeding schedule does list beer bran, as roughage alongside hay and rice straw.

In 2007 the [BBC reported](http://news.bbc.co.uk/2/hi/uk_news/england/cornwall/6345289.stm) on a farm in Cornwall, England, whose five Limousin cattle got through up to 40 pints of local beer a day, with regular massages, in a bid for Kobe-style beef.

The A5 share is still climbing: from January to June 2026, **more than 75 percent** of the Wagyu steers graded in Japan made it, by the [grading association's monthly counts](https://www.jmga.or.jp/rating/). The price pays for the calf and the year and a half of grain it takes to get them there. Strictly FYI.
