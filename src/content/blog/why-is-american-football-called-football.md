Under the rules Walter Camp [printed in 1891](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/39743/pg39743-images.html) in his book *American Football*, a touchdown came with an obligation: "A side which has made a touch-down in their opponents' goal *must* try at goal." The kick decided what the touchdown was worth, **6 points** if it cleared the cross-bar and **4** if it missed. A goal kicked from the field, with no touchdown at all, counted **5**.

## Why is American football called football?

American football is called football because it grew out of the football games American colleges were already playing, and its rules descend from **rugby football**, one of several games that shared the name. Rugby, in which carrying the ball was legal, reached the United States through Harvard's [1874 games against McGill](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/football). The early game favoured the kick: in 1891, [under the rules in Camp's book](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/39743/pg39743-images.html), a field goal outscored a touchdown, **5 points to 4**.

As for why football is called football at all, the word is older than every code that uses it now, from association and rugby to Gaelic and Australian rules. [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/football) records it in the mid-1300s as the name of the inflated ball, and by about 1400 as "any open-air game involving kicking a ball".

## Rugby was one school's football

Rugby School was one of many English public schools with its own version of football, the [World Rugby Museum](https://worldrugbymuseum.com/from-the-vaults/uncategorized/william-webb-ellis-what-we-know) notes, and the rules differed from school to school. Rugby union and rugby league are named for Rugby School because both evolved from its version of the game. The kicking version was codified as association football in **1863**.

Camp drew the same line [in 1891](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/39743/pg39743-images.html), in a first chapter he called "English and American Rugby". The Association game's "essential characteristic is, that it is played with the feet, in distinction from the Rugby, in which the ball may be carried in the hands." The chapter's opening sentence says where the American game came from: "it is from the Rugby Union Rules that our American Intercollegiate game was derived". Carrying the ball made a game rugby, and rugby was still football.

## Harvard learned the rugby game from McGill

American college football began as a kicking game. On 6 November 1869, the [Pro Football Hall of Fame's chronology](https://www.profootballhof.com/football-history/chronology-of-professional-football) records, Rutgers and Princeton played "a college soccer football game, the first ever", using "modified London Football Association rules". [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/football) adds that Princeton described both that game and the rematch on its own ground, on 13 November, as foot-ball.

Rugby came south from Montreal. [The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/football) says McGill University was invited to Harvard in 1874 and found on arrival that Harvard played a kicking game closer to soccer. Harvard won 3-0 under its own rules on 14 May, and the next day the sides tied 0-0 under Canadian rules. Then "Harvard took immediately to the new game and sent to England for the current rugby rules."

The Hall of Fame dates the first written rules for American football to the [Massasoit convention of 1876](https://www.profootballhof.com/football-history/chronology-of-professional-football), the year Walter Camp, "who would become known as the father of American football", first became involved with the game.

## A touchdown once scored nothing

The Rugby Football Union's [first laws](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Laws_of_the_Rugby_Football_Union_%281871%29), from 1871, say "A match shall be decided by a majority of goals only", and so does the [edition adopted on 31 March 1874](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Laws_of_the_Rugby_Football_Union_%281874%29), about six weeks before Harvard played McGill. Touching the ball down behind the opponents' goal line earned a kick: the side that touched down "shall try at goal either by a place-kick or a punt out."

The rule Camp printed twenty years later is the same law, reworded. The [World Rugby Museum's](https://worldrugbymuseum.com/from-the-vaults/evolution-of-rugby/points-scoring-through-the-ages) most notable example of the flawed goals-only system comes from 1872: Royal High School Former Pupils scored **eight tries to Edinburgh University's two**, each side converted one, and the published result was a draw.

The word touchdown came with the rules. [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/touchdown) dates it to 1864, "originally in rugby". The [NFL rulebook](https://operations.nfl.com/rules-officiating/2026-nfl-rulebook) defines a Try as the attempt, after a touchdown, to add one point by a field goal or two points by a touchdown, so the extra-point kick is still a field goal in the rulebook's own terms.

> Under the Rugby Football Union's first laws, a touchdown scored nothing. It earned a try at kicking the goal, and the NFL rulebook still calls the play that follows a touchdown a Try.

## Four rule changes turned the scoring around

The Hall of Fame chronology dates each change that reversed Camp's values.

| Season | Touchdown | Field goal |
|---|---|---|
| 1891, in Camp's book | 4 | 5 |
| 1898 | 5 | 5 |
| 1904 | 5 | 4 |
| 1909 | 5 | 3 |
| 1912 | 6 | 3 |

Touchdown values leave out the kick at goal that followed. The touchdown drew level in **1898**, passed the field goal in **1904** and was worth exactly twice as much by 1912, [the chronology shows](https://www.profootballhof.com/football-history/chronology-of-professional-football).

Throwing the ball forward was illegal for most of that stretch. The rules printed in [Camp's book](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/39743/pg39743-images.html) allowed a pass "in any direction except towards opponents' goal", and in 1891 Camp called "skilled kicking" the only way to beat an aggressive defence. The [Hall of Fame records](https://www.profootballhof.com/football-history/chronology-of-professional-football) that "the forward pass was legalized" in **1906**.

## The horseback story is a theory, the Webb Ellis story a legend

Two origin stories travel with this question, and neither is established fact.

One says football was named for games played on foot, as opposed to on horseback. [Der Spiegel](https://www.spiegel.de/international/naming-the-beautiful-game-it-s-called-soccer-a-420024.html) has reported it as the view of the Australian historian Bill Murray: the name "originally referred to any ball game played on foot rather than on horseback". The dictionary record starts with the object. The earliest sense [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/football) records, from the mid-1300s, is the inflated ball, a word built from foot plus ball, and the game follows by about 1400.

The other says William Webb Ellis invented rugby in 1823 by picking up the ball and running with it. The [World Rugby Museum](https://worldrugbymuseum.com/from-the-vaults/uncategorized/william-webb-ellis-what-we-know) answers "Did he invent rugby?" with "No", and says the story was "first circulated by Matthew Bloxam in 1876", more than half a century later. Phil McGowan, the museum's curator, writing in 2017, [points out](https://worldrugbymuseum.com/from-the-vaults/club-rugby/william-webb-ellis-and-the-origins-of-rugby-a-new-perspective) that Bloxam had it from "an unnamed third party".

The museum trims the legend itself, too. In [its reading](https://worldrugbymuseum.com/from-the-vaults/uncategorized/william-webb-ellis-what-we-know) of Bloxam's two accounts, Webb Ellis never picks the ball up: he "catches the ball and rushes forwards with it", which was against the rules in his day and "did not become legal until 1841." The museum still calls him "the first person for whom an account exists of a player catching and running with the ball."

Even the commemorative stone at Rugby School calls the game football: it honours Webb Ellis's ["fine disregard for the rules of football"](https://worldrugbymuseum.com/from-the-vaults/club-rugby/william-webb-ellis-and-the-origins-of-rugby-a-new-perspective).

## Soccer is British slang

The word Americans use for the other football is British. [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/soccer) traces it to "Assoc.", short for association, and [Andy Mitchell of Scottish Sport History](https://www.scottishsporthistory.com/sports-history-news-and-blog/the-mysterious-origin-of-soccer-what-happened-in-1885) has it in print by **1885**, in three English school magazines, spelled "Soccer" or "Socker". Stefan Szymanski of the University of Michigan found that it was "only later adopted by Americans to distinguish it from gridiron", and that British use peaked between 1960 and 1980, [according to the university's 2014 release](https://news.umich.edu/despite-its-british-origins-americans-get-a-bad-rap-for-using-the-word-soccer/).

Between 1898 and 1912 the points moved from the kick to the touchdown, and the name never followed them. American football still carries the name of the kicking game it started as. Strictly FYI.
