Read "A Visit from St. Nicholas" aloud this December and listen for Santa's age. The poem calls him old twice, as ["a little old driver, so lively and quick"](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Troy_Sentinel/1823/12/23/Account_of_a_Visit_from_St._Nicholas) and as "a right jolly old elf," and gives him a beard "as white as the snow." It never puts a number on him.

The figure depends on which layer of the legend is being dated. Each layer has a year attached, though the bishop's rests on church tradition rather than a birth record.

## How old is Santa?

Santa is **about 1,750 years old** if he is St. Nicholas, the bishop of Myra whose birth the [St. Nicholas Center's timeline](https://www.stnicholascenter.org/who-is-st-nicholas/timeline) places around AD 270. Counted from a New York newspaper notice of [St. A Claus in **December 1773**](https://boston1775.blogspot.com/2016/12/st-claus-was-celebrated-at-protestant.html), he is more than 250. Counted from the [1823 poem](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Troy_Sentinel/1823/12/23/Account_of_a_Visit_from_St._Nicholas) with the eight named reindeer, he is past 200.

Each layer in date order, with its year from the [St. Nicholas Center's timeline](https://www.stnicholascenter.org/who-is-st-nicholas/timeline) and the sources below:

| Where the count starts | Year | Santa's age by that count |
|---|---|---|
| Nicholas is born in Patara, Lycia | Around 270 | About 1,750 |
| St. A Claus in a New York newspaper | 1773 | More than 250 |
| Irving's comic history of New York | 1809 | More than 215 |
| Santeclaus comes on Christmas Eve | 1821 | More than 200 |
| "A Visit from St. Nicholas" | 1823 | More than 200 |

## Nicholas of Myra was born around AD 270, give or take

Under every later layer sits a fourth-century bishop. Nicholas [was born during the third century in the village of Patara in Asia Minor](https://www.stnicholascenter.org/who-is-st-nicholas), on what is now the southern coast of Turkey, the St. Nicholas Center says, and he died on **December 6, AD 343**, in Myra.

Those dates are firmer in the retelling than in the record. The 1911 Catholic Encyclopedia gives his death as ["6 December, 345 or 352"](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/11063b.htm) and says there is ["scarcely anything historically certain about him except that he was Bishop of Myra in the fourth century."](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/11063b.htm) Any answer precise to the year rests on a birth date that the sources themselves hedge.

His one agreed day marks his death. December 6, St. Nicholas Day, is [the day St. Nicholas died](https://www.stnicholascenter.org/who-is-st-nicholas/faqs), because, the center explains, saints' days "commemorate the day of death, or entrance into life eternal."

## His bones went to Bari in 1087, and they belong to a man over 70

In the spring of 1087, sailors from Bari, a seaport on the southeast coast of Italy, [succeeded in spiriting away the bones](https://www.stnicholascenter.org/who-is-st-nicholas) from Myra, and a church was built over his crypt. His relics, the Catholic Encyclopedia says, [are still preserved in the church of San Nicola in Bari](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/11063b.htm).

Data on those bones were gathered in the 1950s. In 2004 the facial anthropologist Caroline Wilkinson used the earlier measurements to model the saint's head, and the result, [the St. Nicholas Center reports](https://www.stnicholascenter.org/who-is-st-nicholas/real-face), is the image of a Greek man "about 60-years old, 5-feet 6-inches tall, who had a heavy jaw and a broken nose."

The bones themselves look older than the face. The examinations of 1953 and 1957, the center says, showed them to be part of one skeleton of ["a man over seventy years of age."](https://www.stnicholascenter.org/who-is-st-nicholas/real-face/anatomical-examination)

## New York printed St. A Claus in 1773

Santa's American name turns up in a New York newspaper of December 1773. Hugh Gaine's New-York Gazette of **December 20**, as the Boston 1775 historian J.L. Bell transcribes it, reported that ["the Anniversary of St. Nicholas, otherwise called St. A Claus, was celebrated at Protestant-Hall"](https://boston1775.blogspot.com/2016/12/st-claus-was-celebrated-at-protestant.html) the previous Monday, which Bell dates to December 13, a week after the feast. Rivington's New-York Gazetteer ran the same item three days later.

That later printing is the one the scholar Charles Jones called ["The first surviving mention of [Nicholas] in America"](https://www.stnicholascenter.org/who-is-st-nicholas/origin-of-santa) in his book Saint Nicholas of Myra, Bari, and Manhattan. The revelers were not keeping an old Dutch custom, Jones argued in 1954: when New Yorkers resurrected St. Nicholas, ["they did so because he was anti-British, not because he was pro-Dutch"](https://www.stnicholascenter.org/who-is-st-nicholas/origin-of-santa/knickerbocker), a local answer to the English societies named for Saint George.

> In Saint Nicholas of Myra, Bari, and Manhattan, Charles Jones said those sons of the saint "seem not to have known their father's birthday," meaning his feast day.

## The Dutch-colony Santa is the part the record barely supports

The popular story says Dutch settlers brought Santa to New Amsterdam. Mary Booth's 1859 History of the City of New York told it with a St. Nicholas figurehead on the first emigrant ship and a "jolly, rosy-cheeked little old man" driving a reindeer sleigh over the roofs of New Amsterdam, [as Jones quoted her](https://www.stnicholascenter.org/who-is-st-nicholas/origin-of-santa/knickerbocker).

Jones searched the newspapers, diaries and broadsides and found nothing to support it. "There is no evidence that it existed in New Amsterdam or for a century after English occupation," [he wrote](https://www.stnicholascenter.org/who-is-st-nicholas/origin-of-santa/knickerbocker). The center's own history is a shade softer: scholars find ["scant evidence of such traditions in Dutch New Netherland."](https://www.stnicholascenter.org/who-is-st-nicholas/origin-of-santa) What it believes is the earliest reference to Sinterklaas in colonial America is a March 1675 Albany baker's receipt to Maria van Rensselaer for ["suntterclaesgoet" (Sinterklaas goods)](https://www.stnicholascenter.org/who-is-st-nicholas/origin-of-santa/flemish-influence), and the center calls March a curious date for them.

## New York gave St. Nicholas a new shape between 1809 and 1821

The St. Nicholas Center traces the Dutch story to Washington Irving. His A History of New York, a parody that became a publishing sensation in 1809, gained a new scene in its [1812 second edition](https://archive.org/details/ahistorynewyork31irvigoog): a Dutch settler dreams that "good St. Nicholas came riding over the tops of the trees, in that self-same wagon wherein he brings his yearly presents to children," in the wording [Humanities magazine quotes](https://www.neh.gov/humanities/2016/fall/feature/how-washington-irving-shaped-christmas-in-america). The center calls Irving's saint ["an elfin Dutch burgher with a clay pipe"](https://www.stnicholascenter.org/who-is-st-nicholas/origin-of-santa) and names his flights of imagination as the source of the New Amsterdam legends.

John Pintard, the antiquarian behind the New-York Historical Society, founded in 1804, promoted St. Nicholas as patron of the society and the city. For its first St. Nicholas dinner, on **December 6, 1810**, he commissioned the artist Alexander Anderson to make what the center calls [the first American image of Nicholas](https://www.stnicholascenter.org/who-is-st-nicholas/origin-of-santa), with children's treats in stockings at a fireplace. In 1821 the picture book The Children's Friend sent ["Old Santeclaus"](https://rpo.library.utoronto.ca/content/old-santeclaus) and his reindeer "o'er chimney-tops" on "Christmas eve," which the center counts as his first appearance on that night rather than on December 6.

## The 1823 poem called him old and stopped there

"A Visit from St. Nicholas" ran as "Account of a Visit from St. Nicholas" in the [Troy Sentinel](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Troy_Sentinel/1823/12/23/Account_of_a_Visit_from_St._Nicholas) on **December 23, 1823**, two years after The Children's Friend. Its sleigh, its pipe, its white beard and the [eight reindeer it calls by name](/blog/what-are-the-names-of-santas-reindeer/) formed what the center's timeline calls ["lasting images of an American St. Nicholas."](https://www.stnicholascenter.org/who-is-st-nicholas/timeline)

Its saint is small, though: an "elf" with ["a miniature sleigh, and eight tiny rein-deer."](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Troy_Sentinel/1823/12/23/Account_of_a_Visit_from_St._Nicholas) The full-size Santa came about a century later. Each year from 1931 to 1964 Haddon Sundblom drew a new one for Coca-Cola, and his ["life-size Santas in white fur-trimmed red suits are now the American Santa Claus,"](https://www.stnicholascenter.org/who-is-st-nicholas/timeline) the timeline says.

Not one of these layers gives Santa a birthday. The poem that made him famous settled for calling him old. Strictly FYI.
