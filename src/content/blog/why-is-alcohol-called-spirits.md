Chaucer's Canon's Yeoman has served an alchemist for seven years and is, by his own account, "never the neer," no closer to understanding the science. Blowing on the fire has changed the colour of his face, he tells the Host in [*The Canterbury Tales*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/22120/pg22120-images.html), which Chaucer wrote [**between 1387 and 1400**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Canterbury_Tales).

He does know the vocabulary. After a long jumble of jargon he admits he cannot put in order, [he recites what he was taught](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/22120/pg22120-images.html): "The foure spirites and the bodies sevene, By ordre, as ofte I herde my lord hem nevene," the four spirits and seven bodies, in order, as he often heard his master name them. He then names the spirits in turn: quicksilver, orpiment, sal ammoniac and brimstone.

In modern terms those are mercury, [a yellow arsenic mineral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orpiment), [ammonium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salammoniac) and sulfur. **None of them is a drink**, yet the same word now covers whisky, gin and vodka, and it got there through alchemy.

## Why is alcohol called spirits?

Because to medieval alchemists a spirit was a ["volatile substance; distillate,"](https://www.etymonline.com/word/spirit) something that rose as vapour when heated, from Latin *spiritus*, "breath," and distilled alcohol is collected from exactly that kind of vapour. The alchemical sense is on record from the late 14th century, and [by the **1670s**](https://www.etymonline.com/word/spirit) the plural *spirits* alone meant strong alcoholic liquor.

## In the alchemist's furnace, spirits were the things that rose

The yeoman draws the distinction himself, complaining that neither "our spirites ascencioun" nor ["our materes that lyen al fixe adoun"](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/22120/pg22120-images.html) can help them.

The Chaucer editor Walter Skeat glossed the pair in his [notes to the poem](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/43016/pg43016-images.html). The ascension of spirits, he wrote, is "the rising of gases or vapours from certain substances," and the fixed matters are the solids left at the bottom. He listed the four substances that "were technically termed 'spirits'" as sulfur, sal ammoniac, quicksilver and arsenic, "or (as some said) orpiment."

## A 15th-century treatise gave the essence of wine three names

Wine had been distilled for centuries by the yeoman's day: it is described in [Arabic works attributed to al-Kindi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_(chemistry)), who died in 873, and al-Farabi, who died about 950. The historian of Islamic science [Ahmad Y. al-Hassan](http://www.history-science-technology.com/notes/notes7.html) quotes the perfume book credited to al-Kindi, in which "wine is distilled in wetness and it comes out like rosewater in colour," and notes that a text credited to al-Farabi mentions adding sulphur when distilling wine.

[Latin recipes for *aqua ardens*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_(chemistry)), "burning water," appear from the twelfth century. Alcohol boils at about [**78 C**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethanol), well below water's 100 C, so the vapour off heated wine is richer in alcohol than the wine itself.

In English, [*The Book of Quinte Essence*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/17179/pg17179-images.html), a treatise copied in about **1460-70**, has the reader distil wine seven times for "good brennynge watir," which the printed edition sums up in a side-note: "Distil 7 times to get Burning Water." The quintessence drawn from that burning water "hath .iij. names by the philosophoris," and the side-note lists them: "1. Burning Water; 2. the Soul in the spirit of Wine; 3. Water of Life." Frederick Furnivall, editing it in 1866, wrote that the tract "appears to be a great fuss about Alcohol or Spirits of Wine."

In that second name the soul sits inside the spirit of wine. The third, *watir of lijf*, is the Latin *aqua vitae*, which, according to Barnhart as cited in the [Online Etymology Dictionary](https://www.etymonline.com/word/whiskey), had been applied to intoxicating drinks since the early 14th century. Gaelic *uisge beatha*, "water of life," the source of the word whisky, is probably a loan-translation of it.

When *The Balance and Columbian Repository*, a newspaper in Hudson, New York, printed the [earliest known definition](https://wordhistories.net/2019/09/19/cocktail-earliest-definition/) of the [cocktail](/blog/why-are-cocktails-called-cocktails/) in **1806**, it called the drink "a stimulating liquor, composed of spirits of any kind, sugar, water, and bitters." US federal law still lists ["spirits of wine"](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/27/211) in its definition of distilled spirits, right after "hydrated oxide of ethyl."

## The word also named a lamp fuel and a builder's level

Other distillates carried the name too. Augustus Webb of New York coined the name camphene in his [**1838** patent](https://aoghs.org/products/camphene-to-kerosene-lamps/) for a lamp burning distilled spirits of turpentine, according to the American Oil and Gas Historical Society. The spirit level, a name [on record from **1768**](https://www.etymonline.com/word/spirit), is "so called for the liquid in the clear tube," and that liquid is [commonly alcohol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirit_level). The instrument is older than its English name: the French scientist Melchisedech Thevenot [invented it some time before February 2, 1661](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirit_level).

## Alcohol began as a black powder for the eyes

In **1615** the English traveller George Sandys, describing Turkish women, wrote that they put ["a certaine blacke powder"](https://archive.org/details/b30330671/page/n82/mode/1up) between the eyelids and the eye with a fine long pencil, made of a mineral brought from Fez "and called Alcohole." That powder is **kohl**.

The [Online Etymology Dictionary](https://www.etymonline.com/word/alcohol) traces *alcohol* to Arabic *al-kuhul*, kohl, "the fine metallic powder used to darken the eyelids," by way of Medieval Latin *alcohol*, "powdered ore of antimony." Even ancient Egyptian kohl was not one recipe: when chemists analysed 11 ancient Egyptian kohl containers from the Petrie Museum, they found [inorganic ingredients "not only lead-based but also manganese- and silicon-based"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8994005/), along with plant and animal materials.

In English the word meant powder first. A glossary of "straunge wordes" in the [**1543** English translation](https://archive.org/details/b30338955/page/n564/mode/1up) of the Italian surgeon Giovanni da Vigo explains that "barbarous" authors use "alchohol" or "alcofoll" for "moost fyne poudre." [Paracelsus (1493-1541)](https://www.etymonline.com/word/alcohol) used it for a fine powder "but also a volatile liquid," and by the 1670s English applied it to "any sublimated substance, the pure spirit of anything."

> By **1706** a dictionary could state the link outright: the sixth edition of [Edward Phillips's dictionary](https://archive.org/details/b30452600/page/n26/mode/1up), revised by John Kersey, says "the highest rectify'd Spirit of Wine is call'd Alcohol Vini."

The sense ["intoxicating ingredient in strong liquor"](https://www.etymonline.com/word/alcohol) is attested by **1753**, short for *alcohol of wine*, and the formerly preferred terms were "rectified spirits or brandy."

## The al-ghawl story does not match the verse it cites

A [2016 Scientific American blog post](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/food-matters/a-spirited-debate-how-did-some-alcohols-come-to-be-known-as-spirits/) offered as one theory that *alcohol* comes from the Arabic *al-ghawl*, calling this "the most straightforward way to link alcohol and spirits, as the word means spirit," and said that Quran 37:47 uses the word "to refer to a demon or spirit that produces intoxication," though the same post said kohl "may be the more credible theory." [Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/food/alcohol/why-liquor-called-spirits) repeated the claim in 2021, citing Scientific American. It is a folk etymology: the verse does not support it, and the dictionaries trace *alcohol* to kohl.

The [Quranic Arabic Corpus](https://corpus.quran.com/translation.jsp?chapter=37&verse=47) sets seven English translations of the verse side by side, and none makes *ghawl* a spirit or a demon: they give it as a "bad effect," a "headache," "headiness," "trouble," "sickness," harm or hurt. Mohsin Khan's version keeps the Arabic as "Ghoul" but [glosses it](https://corpus.quran.com/translation.jsp?chapter=37&verse=47) as "any kind of hurt, abdominal pain, headache, a sin, etc." Five of the seven name intoxication as a separate thing, as [Sahih International does](https://corpus.quran.com/translation.jsp?chapter=37&verse=47): "No bad effect is there in it, nor from it will they be intoxicated." Even if *al-ghawl* had given English *alcohol*, it could not account for *spirits*, a word from Latin that was alchemy's term in English by Chaucer's day.

A second story, which the blog [Gentlemen Ranters](https://www.gentlemenranters.com/why-is-alcohol-called-spirits/) found in a bartending handbook called BarSmarts Advanced, credits Aristotle with the name, on the idea that the drink left its drinker with an invigorated spirit. Aristotle was among the ancient writers who knew that [the exhalations of wine are flammable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_(chemistry)), but no text of his is cited for the name, and he wrote in Greek, centuries before medieval alchemy gave the Latin-derived *spirit* its distilling sense.

Spirit and alcohol started out in different languages for different things: a Latin word for breath that alchemists gave to the substances rising from their fires, and an Arabic word for the powder that darkened eyelids. Distillation joined them, and by the 18th century the purest spirit of wine was called alcohol. Strictly FYI.
