Chaucer's Canon's Yeoman has served an alchemist for seven years and is, by his own account, "never the neer," no closer to understanding the science. Blowing on the fire has changed the colour of his face, he tells the Host in [*The Canterbury Tales*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/22120/pg22120-images.html), which Chaucer wrote [**between 1387 and 1400**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Canterbury_Tales).

He does know the vocabulary. After a long jumble of jargon he admits he cannot put in order, he recites what he was taught: "The foure spirites and the bodies sevene, By ordre, as ofte I herde my lord hem nevene," the four spirits and seven bodies, in order, as he often heard his master name them.

The four spirits are quicksilver, orpiment, sal ammoniac and brimstone, or in modern terms mercury, a yellow arsenic mineral, ammonium chloride and sulfur. **None of them is a drink**, yet the same word now covers whisky, gin and vodka, and it got there through alchemy.

## Why is alcohol called spirits?

Because to medieval alchemists a spirit was a ["volatile substance; distillate,"](https://www.etymonline.com/word/spirit) something that rose as vapour when heated, from Latin *spiritus*, "breath," and distilled alcohol is collected from exactly that kind of vapour. The alchemical sense is on record from the late 14th century, and by the **1670s** the plural *spirits* alone meant strong alcoholic liquor.

## In the alchemist's furnace, spirits were the things that rose

The yeoman draws the distinction himself, complaining that neither "our spirites ascencioun" nor "our materes that lyen al fixe adoun" can help them.

The Chaucer editor Walter Skeat glossed the pair in his [notes to the poem](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/43016/pg43016-images.html). The ascension of spirits, he wrote, is "the rising of gases or vapours from certain substances," and the fixed matters are the solids left at the bottom. He listed the four substances that "were technically termed 'spirits'" as sulfur, sal ammoniac, quicksilver and arsenic, "or (as some said) orpiment."

## A 15th-century treatise gave the essence of wine three names

Wine had been distilled for centuries by the yeoman's day: Arabic works attributed to al-Kindi and al-Farabi describe it in the ninth and tenth centuries, and [Latin recipes for *aqua ardens*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_(chemistry)), "burning water," appear from the twelfth. Alcohol boils at about [**78 C**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethanol), well below water's 100 C, so the vapour off heated wine is richer in alcohol than the wine itself.

In English, [*The Book of Quinte Essence*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/17179/pg17179-images.html), a treatise copied in about **1460-70**, tells the reader to "distille this wiyn .7. tymes" for "good brennynge watir." It says the quintessence drawn from wine "hath .iij. names by the philosophoris": "brennynge watir," "the soule in the spirit of wyn" and "watir of lijf." Frederick Furnivall, editing it in 1866, wrote that the tract "appears to be a great fuss about Alcohol or Spirits of Wine."

That second name is the nearest the tract comes to the popular claim that spirits are the soul of a drink, and it puts the soul inside the spirit. *Watir of lijf* is the Latin *aqua vitae*, which the [Online Etymology Dictionary](https://www.etymonline.com/word/whiskey) says was applied to intoxicating drinks from the early 14th century. Gaelic *uisge beatha*, "water of life," the source of the word whisky, is probably a loan-translation of it.

When Harry Croswell's newspaper printed the [first definition of the cocktail](/blog/why-are-cocktails-called-cocktails/) in **1806**, the recipe began with "spirits of any kind." US federal law still lists ["spirits of wine"](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/27/211) in its definition of distilled spirits.

## The word also named a lamp fuel and a builder's level

Other distillates carried the name too. Augustus Webb of New York coined the name camphene in an [**1838** patent](https://aoghs.org/products/camphene-to-kerosene-lamps/) for a lamp burning what the American Oil and Gas Historical Society calls distilled spirits of turpentine, a fuel that also figures in [the early history of dry cleaning](/blog/how-does-dry-cleaning-work/). The [spirit level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirit_level), a name on record from **1768**, is ["so called for the liquid in the clear tube,"](https://www.etymonline.com/word/spirit) and that liquid is commonly alcohol.

## Alcohol began as a black powder for the eyes

In **1615** the English traveller George Sandys, describing Turkish women, wrote that they put between the eyelids and the eye ["a certaine blacke powder with a fine long pensil, made of a minerall brought from the kingdome of Fez, and called Alcohole"](https://archive.org/details/b30330671/page/n82/mode/1up). That powder is **kohl**.

The [Online Etymology Dictionary](https://www.etymonline.com/word/alcohol) traces *alcohol* to Arabic *al-kuhul*, kohl, "the fine metallic powder used to darken the eyelids," by way of Medieval Latin *alcohol*, "powdered ore of antimony." Kohl was traditionally made by [crushing stibnite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kohl_(cosmetics)), an antimony sulfide mineral.

In English the word meant powder first. A glossary of "straunge wordes" in the [**1543** English translation](https://archive.org/details/b30338955/page/n564/mode/1up) of the Italian surgeon Giovanni da Vigo explains that "barbarous" authors use "alchohol" or "alcofoll" "for moost fyne poudre." Paracelsus (1493-1541) used it for a fine powder "but also a volatile liquid," and by the 1670s English applied it to "any sublimated substance, the pure spirit of anything."

> By **1706** a dictionary could state the link outright: the sixth edition of [Edward Phillips's dictionary](https://archive.org/details/b30452600/page/n26/mode/1up), revised by John Kersey, says "the highest rectify'd Spirit of Wine is call'd Alcohol Vini."

The sense "intoxicating ingredient in strong liquor" is attested by **1753**, short for *alcohol of wine*. Before that, the preferred terms were ["rectified spirits or brandy."](https://www.etymonline.com/word/alcohol)

| About 1460-70 | spirit of wine | In "the soule in the spirit of wyn" |

## The al-ghawl story does not match the verse it cites

A [2016 Scientific American blog post](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/food-matters/a-spirited-debate-how-did-some-alcohols-come-to-be-known-as-spirits/) said that deriving *alcohol* from the Arabic *al-ghawl* is "the most straightforward way to link alcohol and spirits, as the word means spirit," and that Quran 37:47 uses it "to refer to a demon or spirit that produces intoxication." [Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/food/alcohol/why-liquor-called-spirits) repeated the claim in 2021, citing Scientific American. It is a folk etymology: the verse does not support it, and the dictionaries trace *alcohol* to kohl.

In that verse, all eight English translations that [quran.com](https://quran.com/37/47) serves give *ghawl* as harm, a "bad effect," a "headache" or "headiness," and none makes it a spirit or a demon. [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/alcohol) calls *al-ghawl* "rather poetical" and adds that "the relation to stibium is well documented." Even if *al-ghawl* had given English *alcohol*, it could not account for *spirits*, a word from Latin that was alchemy's term in English by Chaucer's day.

A second story, which the whisky blog [Gentlemen Ranters](https://www.gentlemenranters.com/why-is-alcohol-called-spirits/) found in a bartending handbook called BarSmarts Advanced, credits Aristotle with the name, on the idea that the drink invigorates the spirit. No text of Aristotle is cited for it, and he wrote in Greek, centuries before medieval alchemy gave the Latin-derived *spirit* its distilling sense.

Spirit and alcohol started out in different languages for different things: a Latin word for breath that alchemists gave to the substances rising from their fires, and an Arabic word for the powder that darkened eyelids. Distillation joined them, and by the 18th century the purest spirit of wine was called alcohol. Strictly FYI.
