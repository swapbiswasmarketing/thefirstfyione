Cotton Candy grapes taste so much like the fairground sweet that plenty of people assume a lab put the flavor there. In a [trade-magazine column](https://cdn.perishablepundit.com/2022/09/Cotton-Candy-Story-Fruit-Grower-News-Oct-2015.pdf), John Clark recalled a waitress in Aptos, California, who had eaten the grape that day and "suspected it was some sort of GMO, but she liked it anyway."

Grapery, the California grower that began selling the grapes in limited quantities in [2011](https://www.npr.org/sections/thesalt/2013/08/05/209222126/the-cotton-candy-grape-a-sweet-spin-on-designer-fruit), has heard stranger theories. "We get people asking if we inject cotton candy flavor into every berry, or if we spread cotton candy over the soil and the roots pick it up," Jim Beagle, Grapery's chief executive, told [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/california-farmers-grow-grapes-with-surprising-candy-like-flavors/) in 2016.

Clark had reason to know better. One of the grape's parents came out of the University of Arkansas fruit breeding program, where he worked from [July 1980 until January 2023](https://aaes.uada.edu/news/john-clark-career-retrospective/).

## How are cotton candy grapes made?

Cotton Candy grapes are made by cross-breeding, not genetic engineering. The variety's plant patent, [**US PP23,399**](https://patents.google.com/patent/USPP23399P2/en), records that a University of Arkansas grape hybrid was hand-pollinated with a grape called Princess in **May 2003**, that the embryos were cultured because the seeds would have aborted, and that one seedling was picked in **July 2005**, with breeder David Cain named as inventor. [No flavor is added](https://www.grapery.biz/index.php/faq).

The application was filed on August 24, 2011, and the patent was [granted](https://patents.google.com/patent/USPP23399P2/en) on **February 19, 2013**, to International Fruit Genetics, naming the variety **IFG Seven**, the cultivar the [Australian Table Grape Association](https://australiangrapes.com.au/varieties/cotton-candy/) lists under the trade name Cotton Candy.

## One parent came from Arkansas, the other from the USDA

James N. Moore [started the Arkansas program in 1964](https://aaes.uada.edu/fruit-breeding/). He had left a USDA small-fruit breeding job in Beltsville, Maryland, and his goal, [Clark wrote](https://cdn.perishablepundit.com/2022/09/Cotton-Candy-Story-Fruit-Grower-News-Oct-2015.pdf), was seedless grapes "that would provide the foundation for an Eastern U.S. table grape industry."

In 2001, David Cain, who had begun a new table grape breeding company in Bakersfield, California, called International Fruit Genetics, came to see Clark, and after many months of talks an agreement let IFG use Arkansas selections in crosses "with pure V. vinifera parents," [Clark recalled](https://cdn.perishablepundit.com/2022/09/Cotton-Candy-Story-Fruit-Grower-News-Oct-2015.pdf).

> "Cain and I were very impressed with this selection soon after it was found, but wondered if it had too much flavor compared to the neutral-flavored grapes most Americans were accustomed to," Clark wrote of Cotton Candy.

The patent names the parents. One is **A2674**, "an unnamed interspecific Vitis selection received under contract from the University of Arkansas," [the patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/USPP23399P2/en) says, meaning a numbered seedling bred from more than one grape species. The [Los Angeles Times](https://www.latimes.com/business/la-xpm-2013-jul-31-la-fi-cotton-candy-grape-20130801-story.html) called it a Concord, and [NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/thesalt/2013/08/05/209222126/the-cotton-candy-grape-a-sweet-spin-on-designer-fruit) reported that Cain could not say which "Concord-like grape" he used "because it's a trade secret."

The other is **Princess**, a greenish-white seedless table grape bred by David Ramming and Ron Tarailo of the USDA's research service in Fresno, released in **1999** as Melissa and renamed over a trademark conflict, according to a [University of California guide](https://class.ucanr.edu/sites/default/files/2011-05/98667.pdf). According to [the patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/USPP23399P2/en), the new vine most closely resembles Princess, which it calls its pollen parent (a later line gives that role to A2674), but has "a very strong aromatic flavor that the Princess variety lacks."

## Seedless grapes abort their seeds, so the embryos were rescued

The cross produced what [the patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/USPP23399P2/en) calls "abortive seed traces," which were embryo cultured before the plant went into the field in **April 2004**.

In seedless grapes of the Sultanina type (Sultanina is also called [Thompson Seedless](https://ojs.openagrar.de/index.php/VITIS/article/view/6068/5747)), "fertilization occurs, and embryos may develop, but their development is often stopped, and normal seeds cannot form," a [2023 paper in *Plants*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10575281/) explains. Grapery's [FAQ](https://www.grapery.biz/index.php/faq) puts it more plainly: "all grapes have seeds," and in seedless ones the seeds "naturally stop growing at a very early stage."

The workaround, embryo rescue, grows extracted ovules or immature embryos in artificial media, letting the plantlet develop "without the abortion caused by a natural process," in [the paper's words](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10575281/).

In [1983](https://ojs.openagrar.de/index.php/VITIS/article/view/6068), three researchers reported in the journal *Vitis* that they had raised "normal embryos and seedling plants" from the "normally abortive ovules of seedless grapes." The [paper's introduction](https://ojs.openagrar.de/index.php/VITIS/article/view/6068/5747) named the problem it set out to solve: "Seedless cultivars can be used only as male parents." Its authors, at a USDA fruit research unit in Fresno, California, judged it "reasonable to assume that embryos of seedless grapes are genetically sound," and suggested the abortion "may result from a physiological imbalance in the parental tissues," [the introduction continues](https://ojs.openagrar.de/index.php/VITIS/article/view/6068/5747).

The 2023 paper dates "the first reported vines" raised by ovule culture from a seedless grape to that work, [citing it](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10575281/). Its first author was [D.W. Cain](https://ojs.openagrar.de/index.php/VITIS/article/view/6068), and the [Los Angeles Times](https://www.latimes.com/business/la-xpm-2013-jul-31-la-fi-cotton-candy-grape-20130801-story.html) reported that David Cain started out in the 1970s as a USDA grape researcher in Fresno. For breeding, the USDA's research service credits Princess's breeder: a [2006 release](https://www.ars.usda.gov/is/pr/2006/061130.htm) calls Ramming "the first to successfully apply, and refine, the technique specifically for breeding seedless grapes."

Cain's team grew such embryos "in individual test tubes in the lab," [NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/thesalt/2013/08/05/209222126/the-cotton-candy-grape-a-sweet-spin-on-designer-fruit) reported in 2013, and Cain made around **100,000** of these test-tube plants before he found the cotton candy flavor.

## The GMO and injection rumors, checked against the record

Grapery's [FAQ page](https://www.grapery.biz/index.php/faq) answers the GMO question with "No." It also says "We didn't add any cotton candy flavor" and "there are no additives or infusions in any of our grapes," the grower's answer to the injected berries and the sugared soil.

The [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/agricultural-biotechnology/science-and-history-gmos-and-other-food-modification-processes) calls GMO the common term for foods created through genetic engineering, which copies a gene from one organism and inserts it into the DNA of another. Grape crossing moves pollen instead: pollen from male flowers is "carefully brushed onto the female clusters of the target plant," the [Los Angeles Times](https://www.latimes.com/business/la-xpm-2013-jul-31-la-fi-cotton-candy-grape-20130801-story.html) explained.

Embryo culture copies and inserts no genes either. The [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/agricultural-biotechnology/science-and-history-gmos-and-other-food-modification-processes) counts cross-breeding among the "traditional modification methods" people have used "for thousands of years."

## High sugar, little tartness and candy-scented compounds

Most commercial grapes are vinifera, with a neutral flavor of sugar and acid and "no strong aromatic component," [the patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/USPP23399P2/en) explains, so breeders have used native American species to "incorporate aromatic flavors into the vinifera species."

On IFG Seven vines grown near Delano, California, in 2010, [the patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/USPP23399P2/en) records a flavor "Similar to Toffee or Cotton Candy having a hint of Vitis riparia flavor," about **19.2 Brix**, a measure of sugar, and a sugar-to-acid ratio of about **68.6**. *Vitis riparia* is the river grape, a wild vine of riverbanks whose fruit tends to be "sour to human tastes even when ripe," according to the Native Plant Trust's [Go Botany](https://gobotany.nativeplanttrust.org/species/vitis/riparia/).

Beagle told [CBS](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/california-farmers-grow-grapes-with-surprising-candy-like-flavors/) the grapes run about **20 percent** natural sugars, against around **17 percent** for most table grapes. [NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/thesalt/2013/08/05/209222126/the-cotton-candy-grape-a-sweet-spin-on-designer-fruit) had reported a smaller gap in 2013: about 18 grams of sugar per 100 grams of grapes, or about 12 percent more sugar than regular table grapes, and a chef told NPR the fruit has "very little tartness."

Federica Bonello and colleagues measured the aroma in a [2024 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244391/) of five table grape varieties supplied by an Italian breeders' network and harvested in 2022.

Cotton Candy held an estimated **461.4 micrograms of furaneol per kilogram**, more than four times the next-highest variety, at 106.1, in [the study's table of free aroma compounds](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244391/). Flavor references describe furaneol's smell as "Candy, sweet, candyfloss, caramellic," and [the authors wrote](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244391/) that the grape's candyfloss odors were confirmed "through the high level of compounds like furaneol."

Overall, though, the authors rated the grape "neutral in its aroma," with "a notably low content of monoterpenes," the compounds that mark Muscat-type grapes, [the study reports](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244391/). In this otherwise plain grape, the study credits the candyfloss notes to high amounts of three compounds, furaneol among them.

## Eight years from hand cross to first sales

The patent record corrects the [Los Angeles Times](https://www.latimes.com/business/la-xpm-2013-jul-31-la-fi-cotton-candy-grape-20130801-story.html), which wrote in 2013 that the grape "was patented in 2010," before the application had even been filed. From the hand cross in May 2003, the grape took eight years to reach its first buyers. IFG's own count, in a [2023 anniversary statement](https://www.freshplaza.com/north-america/article/9505567/breeder-celebrates-20-years-of-cotton-candy-grapes/), is "10 more years" before the grape began to appear in retail stores.

The waitress in Aptos guessed GMO, and the grape did pass through a lab, as an embryo in a test tube during its first year. The flavor took far longer. Clark puts the whole effort at about **50 years**, about 35 of them in Arkansas and about 15 in California, [by his own count](https://cdn.perishablepundit.com/2022/09/Cotton-Candy-Story-Fruit-Grower-News-Oct-2015.pdf).
