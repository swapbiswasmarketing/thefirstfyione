Two Scottish doctors usually share the credit, and childbirth usually gets the motive. Under the headline "When Chainsaws Helped Deliver Babies," History.com says that ["Aitken and Jeffray developed a flexible saw made of a chain link held between wooden handles at both ends"](https://www.history.com/articles/chainsaw-invention-origins-delivery-labor) so that a surgeon could separate the mother's pelvis and widen the birth canal. The Star's QuickCheck column in Malaysia took up the question of whether the chainsaw was ["invented as a medical instrument to assist childbirth"](https://www.thestar.com.my/news/nation/2026/04/03/quickcheck-were-chainsaws-originally-invented-to-aid-childbirth) and rated it TRUE.

The two names hold up. The joint project and the single motive do not. James Jeffray's own account, printed in Glasgow in [**1806**](https://archive.org/details/b33488319), says he thought of his saw after watching an attempt to cut a diseased piece out of a thigh bone, and that while he waited to get one made, "a sketch of such a saw was published by Dr John Aitken of Edinburgh."

## The two original chain saws were made to cut bone and spare what lay around it

The chainsaw began in the late 18th century as a hand-pulled surgical saw for cutting bone deep inside the body without slicing through the flesh around it. A 2004 history from the University of Glasgow's anatomy laboratory dates the chain hand saw to [around **1783-1785**](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15209147/) and credits John Aitken and James Jeffray with it "for symphysiotomy and excision of diseased bone respectively." One was aimed at blocked births, the other at diseased joints.

The problem Jeffray described was the straight saw. It cuts along a line, so reaching a bone buried in muscle meant opening a long wound. A chain could go round the back instead.

Jeffray's saw, an 1807 review explained, could be ["drawn behind a bone by a crooked needle, like a thread"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5711164/) and then "cut the bone from behind forward, without injuring the soft parts." The Glasgow history sums up his case: the chain saw ["would allow a smaller wound and protect the adjacent neurovascular bundle."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15209147/)

## Aitken's flexible saw was for births a Caesarean could not save

Aitken's version appears in the second edition of his Principles of Midwifery, published in Edinburgh in **1785**. The alternatives he weighed were grim. Of the Caesarean section, he wrote that [in Britain "it has never fully had the desired effect, all the mothers having died."](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_principles-of-midwifery-_aitken-john-md_1785)

His proposal was to cut the pelvis instead. Through four incisions, the bones at the front of the pelvis [are "divided by the flexible saw, without wounding the peritonaeum, bladder, or vagina,"](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_principles-of-midwifery-_aitken-john-md_1785) so that "the anterior segment of the pelvis becomes moveable, and yields to the pressure of the child."

The symphysiotomy of the French doctor Jean-Rene Sigault cut [through the joint at the front of the pelvis](https://www.thestar.com.my/news/nation/2026/04/03/quickcheck-were-chainsaws-originally-invented-to-aid-childbirth), an operation History.com says was ["performed with a small knife without anesthesia."](https://www.history.com/articles/chainsaw-invention-origins-delivery-labor) Aitken's saw went through bone instead, though the Glasgow history files it under symphysiotomy.

The saw itself is drawn in [a plate at the back of the 1785 book](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_principles-of-midwifery-_aitken-john-md_1785). The 1807 reviewer remembered seeing Aitken ["divide bones with, in his anatomical demonstrations"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5711164/) a saw "which he called his flexible saw."

## Jeffray's chain came from a thigh bone and the long wound a straight saw would need

Jeffray, professor of anatomy and surgery at Glasgow, took his starting point from H. Park of the Liverpool Hospital, whose case for cutting diseased joints out instead of amputating the limb was set out in a letter [dated **1782** and published in 1783](https://archive.org/details/b33488319). Soon after, Jeffray watched an attempt to remove a diseased piece near the middle of the thigh bone. With the common saw, he wrote, the wound ["must have been made to extend almost from the knee to the groin."](https://archive.org/details/b33488319)

A rasping saw was tried instead, and the time it took and the pain the patient seemed to suffer left Jeffray, he recalled, unable to stop thinking about an easier way to cut bones out. His answer, in his own words, was a saw ["with joints like the chain of a watch"](https://archive.org/details/b33488319), which a Mr Richards in London made for him some years later. It was used in Jeffray's anatomy class every year ["ever since the year 1790"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5711164/).

> "There is no purpose to which it is so well fitted, as that for which it was originally intended, viz. the Excision of Bones from Carious Joints." - James Jeffray, 1806

Carious joints are joints where the bone is decaying from disease. By Jeffray's account the saw was also lent to surgeons now and then, who used it in operations.

## The sources split on which surgical saw lasted longer

On which use survived, the sources split. History.com says that ["The Aitken's saw remained the tool of choice for childbirth emergencies until the end of the 19th century."](https://www.history.com/articles/chainsaw-invention-origins-delivery-labor) The Glasgow history reads the record the other way: [symphysiotomy "had too many complications for most obstetricians but Jeffray's ideas became accepted"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15209147/), especially once anaesthetics arrived.

The bone saw got more elaborate. The German orthopaedist Bernhard Heine developed his chain osteotome in **1830**, and an article from the National Museum of Health and Medicine says his design, ["similar to the modern chainsaw, cut through bone quickly"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11999-015-4658-2), sparing patients the hammer and chisel.

It was costly: the New York maker George Tiemann and Co. listed one at [**USD 300** in its 1872 catalog, against USD 5 for a standard capital saw](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11999-015-4658-2). In the later 19th century the chain saw was [superseded in surgery by the Gigli twisted wire saw](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15209147/), though the same history calls it "the prototype of the chain saw familiar today in the timber industry."

## The logging chainsaw grew from a different problem

Whatever the debt, the timber saw's paper trail starts elsewhere. Jim Wardrop, an assistant curator of history at the British Columbia Provincial Museum, traced it in 1976 to Harvey Brown of New York City, who in **1858** patented an ["endless section sawing mechanism"](https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/MCR/article/download/16942/23057) with moving saw bits on a guide bar. His history of the timber saw never mentions surgeons.

Frederick Magaw of Flatlands, New York, patented an [improvement in "Chain Sawing-Machines"](https://patents.google.com/patent/US279780A/en) in 1883. Samuel Bens of San Francisco, building on a chain saw patent granted to him on January 17, 1905, wrote in a patent granted in 1908 that long guide frames were liable to lack the stiffness needed for heavy work, ["as for instance with the huge redwood timber of the Pacific coast."](https://patents.google.com/patent/US893897A/en)

Another idea came from a fence. James Shand, a millwright and farmer in Dauphin, Manitoba, patented his "endless" chain saw in **1918** after he found that [barbed wire, drawn by horses, "had sawn through a seven-inch oak post"](https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/MCR/article/download/16942/23057), Wardrop wrote. He built working models with his son's bicycle chain, but the industry showed no interest, and he let his rights lapse in 1930.

None of the machines that followed Brown's in the 19th and early 20th centuries was easily portable, Wardrop wrote, because large gasoline or steam engines drove them. The Reed-Prentice Corporation of Worcester, Massachusetts, began developing a saw with its own engine in [**1929**](https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/MCR/article/download/16942/23057), but its models were far too heavy, and during the Depression the lead passed to the German industrialist Andreas Stihl, whose two-stroke motor was light and powerful.

In British Columbia the push came from the workforce as much as the trees. By 1936 logging companies were having increasing difficulty finding workers willing to use the traditional cross-cut saw, and at the Pacific Logging Congress that year a successful demonstration of a German Stihl saw prompted the assembly to predict that the ["day of the faller and the bucker"](https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/MCR/article/download/16942/23057) would be over except in small operations. The saws of the day were two-man machines weighing 120 pounds, yet by October 1940 Bloedel, Stewart and Welch could report that **80%** of the timber felled that year had been cut by a power saw.

| Year | Saw | What it was for or did |
|---|---|---|
| 1785 | Aitken's flexible saw, in print | Cutting the pelvis in blocked births |
| 1790 | Jeffray's chain saw, in use | Cutting diseased bone out of joints |
| 1830 | Heine's chain osteotome | Bone surgery without hammer and chisel |
| 1858 | Brown's endless section sawing mechanism | Moving saw bits on a guide bar |
| 1908 | Bens's improved endless chain saw | A stiffer guide frame for heavy redwood timber |
| 1918 | Shand's endless chain saw | A small gasoline motor; the idea came from barbed wire |
| 1929 | Reed-Prentice's power saw | A saw with its own engine, at first far too heavy |

So the childbirth story has the right century and half the reason: the original chain saws were made to cut bone without cutting the soft parts around it, in a pelvis that had to open for a baby or a joint decaying from disease. The loggers' saw had to wait for an engine light enough to carry, and for a shortage of men willing to pull the cross-cut saw. Strictly FYI.
