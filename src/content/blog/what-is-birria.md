With his Saturday special due and no goat on the truck, Gerardo Guerrero reached for the brisket. The owner of La Tunita 512, a food truck in a south Austin gas station parking lot, had forgotten to pick up the meat for his birria de chivo, or goat birria, in November 2019, so he [substituted the birria's goat shanks with a five-pound hunk of brisket](https://austin.eater.com/22243046/birria-tacos-quesabirria-food-trucks-austin) bought from a wholesale chain for the day's barbacoa.

The truck went on to become, Eater says, the unofficial face of Austin's beef birria scene, though Guerrero admits he was not the first in the city to sell it. The goat he forgot is the meat the dish was built on in Jalisco, and the word on his menu has at least three proposed origins.

## What is birria?

Birria is meat, traditionally **goat or lamb**, seasoned with a spicy chile adobo and slow-cooked until tender, then served with a consomme made from its own juices. It [originated in the state of Jalisco](https://www.foodnetwork.com/how-to/packages/food-network-essentials/what-is-birria), in western Mexico. In Tijuana and much of the United States it is now usually made with **beef**, and often served in tacos.

Accounts of the old **Jalisco** method differ. Eater Austin describes traditional Jaliscan birria as goat, or in some cases lamb, shanks [wrapped in maguey leaves and slow-cooked in an underground stone-lined oven](https://austin.eater.com/22243046/birria-tacos-quesabirria-food-trucks-austin), which can be eaten dry, with a side of consomme from the meat drippings. Food Network instead describes birria cooked in a pot with a sealed lid, traditionally set in the same kind of oven used for barbacoa. Guerrero compares the beef version to a crawfish boil: fatty beef simmered in a stockpot with adobo and water for roughly five hours.

The adobo is built on dried red chiles. Food Network says [ancho or guajillo peppers are a must in almost all birria recipes](https://www.foodnetwork.com/how-to/packages/food-network-essentials/what-is-birria); cumin is common, Mexican oregano appears in several recipes, and some add sweet spices like cloves and cinnamon. It describes goat, the traditional meat, as strong and gamey, similar to lamb but less intense.

Spanish dictionaries have tracked the dish for well over a century, and they do not quite agree on where it belongs.

| Year | Dictionary | What birria means there |
|---|---|---|
| 1898 | Felix Ramos i Duarte, [Diccionario de mejicanismos](https://archive.org/details/diccionariodemej00ramo) | Goat barbacoa, or roast goat; marked "D. F.", the Federal District |
| 1925 | Dario Rubio, [La anarquia del lenguaje en la America espanola](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a7/La_anarqu%C3%ADa_del_lenguaje_en_la_Am%C3%A9rica_espa%C3%B1ola_-_Dar%C3%ADo_Rubio_%281925%29_Tomo_1.pdf) | Another name the common people give to barbacoa |
| 1974 edition | Francisco J. Santamaria, [Diccionario de mejicanismos](https://archive.org/details/diccionariodemej00sant) | Lamb or goat prepared like barbacoa, typical mainly of Guadalajara, Jalisco; also barbacoa in general |
| Today | Real Academia Espanola, [Diccionario de la lengua espanola](https://dle.rae.es/birria) | In Mexico, barbacoa of lamb or goat |

[Ramos i Duarte's **1898** entry](https://archive.org/details/diccionariodemej00ramo) reads, in full, "Birria (D. F.), sf. Barbacoa de chivo, o chivo asado." His key expands D. F. as Distrito Federal, the Federal District, today Mexico City, so he records the word there rather than in Jalisco, which appears in the table only with Santamaria. Every one of the four definitions leans on [barbacoa](/blog/what-is-barbacoa/) to explain birria.

## The word may be Latin, Portuguese or Coca

The Real Academia Espanola files birria, the Mexican dish, under the same headword as an everyday Spanish put-down. Its first sense of birria is ["Persona o cosa de poco valor o importancia"](https://dle.rae.es/birria), a person or thing of little value or importance, and the Academy derives the word from a reconstructed Vulgar Latin form, verrea, meaning stubbornness or whim and "cosa despreciable", a contemptible thing, which in turn comes from the Latin verres, a boar.

Rubio recorded a different derivation in 1925: Cuervo, he wrote, held that the word was ["adulteracion de la portuguesa birra"](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a7/La_anarqu%C3%ADa_del_lenguaje_en_la_Am%C3%A9rica_espa%C3%B1ola_-_Dar%C3%ADo_Rubio_%281925%29_Tomo_1.pdf), a corruption of the Portuguese birra, and that it meant tirria, a grudge. That was Rufino Jose Cuervo, and the birria he explained was not the Mexican dish but a word of Bogota speech, which [his notes on that speech](https://archive.org/details/apuntacionescr00cueruoft) list among words taken from Portuguese, Galician and Asturian.

Santamaria's dictionary adds a third, Indigenous possibility. It reports that Jose Ignacio Davila Garibi, an "eminente aztequista", or eminent scholar of the Aztec language, thought the word [could have its origin in the Coca language](https://archive.org/details/diccionariodemej00sant), spoken in a region of Jalisco but "hoy casi olvidada", almost forgotten today. Santamaria gives it as Davila Garibi's opinion, and none of the three accounts is settled.

## The worthless-goats origin story is folklore

A story repeated widely online starts with the conquest. Food Network's version says the goats the Spanish brought [started reproducing at an alarmingly rapid rate](https://www.foodnetwork.com/how-to/packages/food-network-essentials/what-is-birria), and that the Spanish "were said to not be too fond of goat meat" because it was gamey and tough. In the version that, PBS's Beyond the Menu says, kept coming up over and over in its research, the Spanish gave the surplus goats to the Mesoamericans ["as they were considered birria, or worthless,"](https://www.pbs.org/video/your-birria-taco-is-ancient-historyheres-why-w5gd7t) and Indigenous cooks made them delicious with chilies.

The show's own guest doubted it. Daniela Gutierrez Flores, an assistant professor of Hispanic literature at UC Davis, said of birria meaning worthless:

> "This is a very modern meaning. It doesn't look like birria had that meaning that early on." - Daniela Gutierrez Flores, on PBS's [Beyond the Menu](https://www.pbs.org/video/your-birria-taco-is-ancient-historyheres-why-w5gd7t)

Neither telling names a document from the time of the conquest. The episode does grant that the goats were a real problem, and that the literature records them destroying local crops; what lacks support is the step from that surplus to a name meaning worthless. Treat the worthless-goats origin of the word as folklore.

## Tijuana's beef birria rests on one name and a legend

The Tijuana branch has a reputed founder. "Guadalupe Zarate is the godfather of birria in Tijuana," the food writer Bill Esparza says on [Beyond the Menu](https://www.pbs.org/video/your-birria-taco-is-ancient-historyheres-why-w5gd7t), and the city's birrieros, he adds, all agree that Zarate started the business. Look Zarate up online, the episode notes, and there is little more to find: "No pictures, no statue, no menus."

The legend, as [the same episode tells it](https://www.pbs.org/video/your-birria-taco-is-ancient-historyheres-why-w5gd7t), is that Zarate, from Puebla, moved to Tijuana in the **1950s** selling goat birria, and that the beef came later. The sources do not agree on why, or even on who Zarate was:

- **PBS:** the episode says Tijuana is a northern Mexican town, where beef was more popular.
- **Food Network:** [a woman named Guadalupe Zarate](https://www.foodnetwork.com/how-to/packages/food-network-essentials/what-is-birria) switched to beef because goat meat was more expensive in Tijuana, and added more liquid to the stew.
- **Eater Austin:** Don Guadalupe Zarate is [the man who legend says first conceived of the beefy stew](https://austin.eater.com/22243046/birria-tacos-quesabirria-food-trucks-austin) for sale in the city.

What Tijuana built is still a breakfast. Eater Austin reports that south of the border beef birria is served almost exclusively in the morning, and that asking for a cup of dipping broth in Tijuana will earn a confused stare.

## Los Angeles added the dunk and the cheese

The red taco that spread across the United States is a Los Angeles version. Eater Austin dates its arrival there to **2013** and credits [young Pueblan entrepreneurs like Omar Gonzalez and Teddy Vasquez](https://austin.eater.com/22243046/birria-tacos-quesabirria-food-trucks-austin), the founders of Birrieria Gonzalez and Teddy's Red Tacos, with popularizing it. Its vermillion color often comes from double-dipping the tortilla in consomme and bright chile oil before it goes on the griddle.

Food Network describes Los Angeles birria tacos dunked in the red-tinged consomme the meat is braised in, and quesabirrias, the same red tacos with melted cheese added. In Tijuana, the food historian Gustavo Arellano told Eater, the tortillas turn orange from the beef fat but never that red.

Guerrero told Eater Austin he still makes birria with goat on occasion, but only for celebrations with family and friends. The dish he sells became beef in Tijuana and red in Los Angeles, and for the word itself scholars have proposed a Latin root, a Portuguese one and a nearly forgotten language of Jalisco.
