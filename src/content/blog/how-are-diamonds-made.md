Coal is still the diamond's origin story in many science classrooms, the geologist Hobart King writes for Geology.com, and his own verdict on it runs to one line: ["Coal has rarely - if ever - played a role in the formation of diamonds."](https://geology.com/articles/diamonds-from-coal/)

In the classroom telling, buried coal is pressed until it turns to diamond. What sinks it is a pair of measurements, of how deep diamonds grow and how long ago they grew.

## Mined diamonds grow in the mantle and ride up in rare eruptions

Nearly all the diamonds ever mined crystallized from carbon deep in the Earth's mantle, mostly beneath the stable interiors of the continents, and reached the surface inside volcanic rock. King places the growth zones [about 150 kilometers or more below the surface](https://geology.com/articles/diamonds-from-coal/), at temperatures of at least 1050 degrees Celsius, and calls the deep-source eruptions that carry them up "extremely rare," a kind never observed by modern humans.

Those eruptions rip pieces of mantle loose and carry them up fast, leaving behind vertical pipes of kimberlite and lamproite, the rocks diamond prospectors look for. Most of the pipes hold little or no diamond, and mines go only into the few that hold enough. Some diamonds have weathered out of them and now lie in the gravels of streams and coastlines.

[Most natural diamonds have ages between 1 billion and 3.5 billion years](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond), and most formed between **150 and 250 kilometers** down. King adds that a few natural diamonds form in other ways: in subducting plates, at asteroid impact sites and in space.

## The diamonds are far older than the volcanoes that delivered them

Minerals trapped inside a diamond can be dated, and in 1984 a team of four researchers, Richardson, Gurney, Erlank and Harris, did it with garnets. Garnets sealed inside diamonds from 90-million-year-old southern African kimberlites gave [model ages of 3,200 to 3,300 million years](https://www.nature.com/articles/310198a0), the team reported in Nature, which puts more than **3 billion years** between the growth of those diamonds and the eruption that delivered them. For nearly all of that time the stones simply sat in the mantle.

## Coal came too late and stays too shallow

Coal is made from buried plant debris, and land plants are young by diamond standards: [most diamonds that have been dated are far older than the first land plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond). For most dated stones there were no land plants, and so no plant-made coal, when they formed.

Depth rules it out too. King writes that coal [is rarely buried to depths greater than two miles (3.2 kilometers)](https://geology.com/articles/diamonds-from-coal/), and he says the carbon of mantle diamonds is most likely "carbon trapped in Earth's interior at the time of the planet's formation or delivered to great depths by subduction." Even the rocks look different: coal usually lies in near-horizontal seams, while diamonds come out of vertical pipes of igneous rock.

| | Coal | Mantle diamonds |
|---|---|---|
| Depth | Rarely below about 3.2 km | About 150 km or more |
| Age | Younger than the first land plants | Mostly 1 to 3.5 billion years; 3,200 to 3,300 million years for the 1984 garnets |
| Shape in the rock | Usually near-horizontal seams | Vertical pipes of igneous rock |

## The deepest diamonds hold traces of ocean crust and molten metal

Subduction, where ocean plates sink into the mantle, is how carbon from the surface can reach diamond depths. In 2011 a team led by Michael Walter studied superdeep diamonds from the Juina-5 kimberlite in Brazil, whose inclusions matched what basalt, the rock of the ocean crust, crystallizes into under lower-mantle conditions. The diamonds also had [carbon isotope signatures "consistent with surface-derived carbon"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21921159/).

Blue diamonds point the same way. In 2018 Evan Smith and colleagues found inclusions in them from ocean slabs sunk as deep as the lower mantle, and proposed that some of the boron ["in seawater-serpentinized oceanic lithosphere is subducted into the deep mantle"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30068951/). They called the blue type IIb stones "among the deepest diamonds ever found."

Some of the greatest gems came from the deep mantle too. In 2016 the same group reported that large, exceptional gem diamonds ["like the Cullinan, Constellation, and Koh-i-Noor"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27980206/) carry evidence of crystallizing from a metallic liquid in the deep mantle, with trapped bits of iron-nickel-carbon-sulfur melt inside.

## A Swedish company made diamonds before GE and announced them later

The Swedish electrical firm ASEA succeeded with diamond synthesis on **15 February 1953**, after more than ten years of work, according to [a 1961 account in the Swiss chemistry journal Chimia](https://www.chimia.ch/chimia/article/download/1961_251/7321/25679), which notes that the date fell "genau zwei Jahre" - exactly two years - before the first announcement of General Electric's synthesis. The same article describes the GE process as having become known "kurz vorher", shortly before ASEA's own first reports, and says the Swedish diamonds did not match GE's in size or crystal quality.

## General Electric's first claimed diamond turned out to be natural

At General Electric, Tracy Hall was convinced of his own process and built a new pressure chamber for it. On **December 16, 1954**, by the account of the American Society of Mechanical Engineers, [Hall produced the first verifiable synthetic diamond using reproducible means](https://www.asme.org/topics-resources/content/asme-landmark-made-diamonds-using-heat-and-pressure). The chamber [reached 1,600 degrees Celsius and 100,000 atmospheres of pressure](https://www.asme.org/topics-resources/content/asme-landmark-made-diamonds-using-heat-and-pressure), figures the society takes from Lydia Pyne's book Genuine Fakes.

GE [announced the first synthetic diamonds in February 1955](https://www.asme.org/topics-resources/content/asme-landmark-made-diamonds-using-heat-and-pressure), and Hall, Francis Bundy, Herbert Strong and Robert Wentorf reported the synthesis in ["Man-Made Diamonds"](https://www.nature.com/articles/176051a0) in Nature that July. Fuller details followed in [a second Nature paper in 1959](https://www.nature.com/articles/1841094a0), "Preparation of Diamond".

Hall's was not the team's first claimed diamond. Eight days earlier Herbert Strong had begun Experiment 151, [using two small natural diamonds to seed diamond crystal growth](https://www.sciencefriday.com/articles/diamond-fake-excerpt/), and Pyne writes that the team [spent weeks trying to duplicate Strong's results and never could](https://www.sciencefriday.com/articles/diamond-fake-excerpt/).

In 1993 six researchers, Bundy, Strong and Wentorf among them, re-examined that run 151 diamond. Modern spectroscopy showed that it [is indeed "a small piece of a natural type Ia diamond"](https://www.nature.com/articles/365019a0), they wrote, which bore out the team's old suspicion that it was a fragment of a natural seed diamond.

> "From the outset there were doubts in our team as to whether the first diamond grown by our technique ... was truly synthetic at all." - [Bovenkerk, Bundy and colleagues, Nature, 1993](https://www.nature.com/articles/365019a0)

The technique itself was sound, and the same letter says the 1955 results [marked the beginning of the present synthetic-diamond industry](https://www.nature.com/articles/365019a0).

## Labs now grow diamonds two ways

Heat and pressure, the route of Hall's run, is one way labs grow diamonds. A second method, chemical vapor deposition, needs no great pressure, and the ASME account describes it as taking place [at low pressures, with a vapor of carbon-rich gas being deposited on a surface](https://www.asme.org/topics-resources/content/asme-landmark-made-diamonds-using-heat-and-pressure). The gas is [a carbon source, typically methane, and hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_diamond), energized by microwaves or other means until its carbon builds up as diamond on a prepared surface.

Lab-grown diamonds take an enormous amount of electricity, and King points out that some of it is likely to come from burning coal. His page leaves coal a small opening, as a possible carbon source at asteroid impact sites, but he saves his best example for the factory: ["These might be the best examples of diamonds being made using coal."](https://geology.com/articles/diamonds-from-coal/) Strictly FYI.
