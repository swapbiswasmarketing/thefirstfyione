The [marathon page](https://worldathletics.org/disciplines/road-running/marathon) on World Athletics' website says the event "is named after the legendary 26-mile run made by a Greek soldier called Philippides (also known as Pheidippides) from the scene of the battle of Marathon to Athens." For the distance it turns to the [1908 London Olympics](https://worldathletics.org/disciplines/road-running/marathon), where the distance was "extended to the imperial measurement of 26 miles" and "increased another 385 yards when the starting line was pulled back so it could be seen by the children in the Royal Nursery at Windsor and still finish in front of Queen Alexandra at the White City Stadium in west London."

That page belongs to the body that fixed the distance: World Athletics [began in 1912 as the IAAF](https://worldathletics.org/about-iaaf). The [Official Report](https://archive.org/details/the-fourth-olympiad-london-1908/page/68/mode/1up) of the 1908 Games tells it differently. Its chapter on the marathon opens with a note that the course ran from Windsor Castle to the Olympic Stadium, "in which 385 yards were run on the cinder track to the finish, below the Royal Box, the runner turning to his left as he entered the track on the side opposite the Royal Box."

## Why is a marathon 26.2 miles?

A marathon is 26.2 miles because the 1908 London Olympic course was that long: **26 miles** from Windsor Castle to the White City stadium, then **385 yards** of track to a finish below the Royal Box. The total, **26 miles 385 yards**, or **42.195 kilometres**, was made the international standard by the IAAF, now World Athletics, in [**1921**](https://worldathletics.org/disciplines/road-running/marathon).

The 0.2 is those 385 yards, a little over a fifth of a mile. The report's [distance table](https://archive.org/details/the-fourth-olympiad-london-1908/page/69/mode/1up) marks mile 26 at the "Entrance of Stadium"; everything after that was track. The same table converts the full distance to "42.263 kilometres," about 68 metres too many: 26 miles 385 yards works out to 42.195.

## The 385 yards are what was left of a lap

Writing in the [Journal of Olympic History](https://digital.la84.org/digital/collection/p17103coll10/id/3877) in 2008, Bob Wilcock rebuilt the course's history from contemporary documents and newspaper reports. The race agreed with the IOC in 1907 was **25 miles**, or 40 kilometres. The first route, published that November, came to about **25 1/2 miles**, measured from Queen Victoria's statue in the town of Windsor; the start itself was not yet settled.

Moving the start up to the East Terrace of Windsor Castle added **700 yards**. The organisers wanted the castle grounds "to ensure that degree of privacy which is almost essential for a race of this kind," The Sporting Life reported in November 1907, [Wilcock notes](https://digital.la84.org/digital/collection/p17103coll10/id/3877). Complaints about tram lines and cobbles over the last few miles brought a detour across Wormwood Scrubs, which added about **1,000 yards** more. That made **26 miles** to the stadium plus a full lap of the track, **586 2/3 yards**, to a finish in front of the Royal Box: about **26 1/3 miles** in all. The runners were to come in through the royal entrance, beneath the box itself.

Then their entrance moved to the far side of the stadium. Wilcock found no announcement of the change, but he reasons that the royal entrance had been raised and decorated for carriages, and that runners using it would have kept the Queen and other dignitaries from leaving for two hours or so. The road route was adjusted to keep 26 miles to the new entrance, and the runners were sent round the track in the reverse of the usual direction so that, Wilcock reasons, they would pass two-thirds of the crowd. The full lap shrank to **385 yards**.

> "There was no royal influence on the determination of the distance." - Bob Wilcock, [*Journal of Olympic History*](https://digital.la84.org/digital/collection/p17103coll10/id/3877), 2008

Wilcock suggests the reversed direction confused the first runner to arrive. Dorando Pietri of Italy came onto the cinder path "almost unconscious" and "turned to his right instead of his left," the [Official Report](https://archive.org/details/the-fourth-olympiad-london-1908/page/78/mode/1up) says. He collapsed, was helped by doctors and attendants, and "staggered past the tape" ahead of Johnny Hayes of the United States. An American objection was upheld, and the race was given to Hayes.

## The nursery window has no contemporary source

The nursery story is unverified: no contemporary source supports it, and nothing on it has been found in the Royal Archives, Wilcock reports. The organisers were already seeking permission for a start in the castle grounds in November 1907, and [Olympedia](https://www.olympedia.org/results/56369), the Olympic results database, notes that there were no requests from the royal family.

The children who saw the start were not watching from a window. On race day the Princess of Wales arrived at the East Terrace by carriage with her children. The family had moved to Frogmore, on the far side of the Windsor estate, the day before the race, and had a mile's drive to the terrace. The [Official Report](https://archive.org/details/the-fourth-olympiad-london-1908/page/74/mode/2up) says the princess gave the signal to start and "was present with her children." Her diary, as [Wilcock quotes it](https://digital.la84.org/digital/collection/p17103coll10/id/3877), reads: "At 2 we went to see the start for the Marathon Race from the East Terrace."

The finish in front of Queen Alexandra needed no royal request either. It was in the plan from the outset; every race in the stadium finished in front of the Royal Box.

Olympedia's writers, who once repeated the nursery story themselves ("including by our group," [it says](https://www.olympedia.org/results/56369)), now cite Wilcock against it.

## Herodotus's runner went to Sparta before the battle

The ancient half of the story has a gap of its own. [Herodotus](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2456/pg2456-images.html), the main source for the battle of Marathon in [490 BC](https://www.livius.org/articles/battle/marathon-490-bce/), does describe a long run, but from Athens to Sparta, before the fighting. The Athenian generals sent "Pheidippides an Athenian and for the rest a runner of long day-courses" to ask the Spartans for help, and he "was in Sparta on the next day after that on which he left the city of the Athenians," in Macaulay's translation.

Sparta agreed to come but would not march until the moon was full. Its [2,000 soldiers](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2456/pg2456-images.html) arrived too late for the battle. After the victory, [Herodotus](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2456/pg2456-images.html) has the Athenian army hurry back to defend the city, arriving "before the Barbarians came."

The lone messenger who dies with the news first appears in surviving writing more than five centuries later. Plutarch, who lived from [about AD 45 to 120](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/plutarch/), wrote in his essay [On the Glory of Athens](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Moralia/De_gloria_Atheniensium*.html) that Thersippus brought the news, "as Heracleides Ponticus relates; but most historians declare that it was Eucles who ran in full armour, hot from the battle." Eucles could only say "Hail! we are victorious!" and "straightway expired."

Lucian, [writing in the second century AD](https://archive.org/details/worksoflucianofs01luciuoft/page/n9/mode/1up), gave the run to "Philippides the dispatch-runner" in [an essay on greetings](https://archive.org/details/worksoflucianofs02luciuoft/page/36/mode/1up). His runner told the waiting archons "Joy, we win!" and "died upon his message."

The 1896 Olympic race was created because of that legend, [Olympedia](https://www.olympedia.org/results/56017) says, and it ran from Marathon to Athens, about **40 kilometres**. The event takes its name from Marathon, where that first course began.

## Olympic marathons kept changing length until 1924

London's distance did not stick at once. Olympedia's results pages give these lengths for the early Olympic marathons:

| Games | Distance |
|---|---|
| [1896 Athens](https://www.olympedia.org/results/56017) | About 40 km (24.85 miles) |
| [1900 Paris](https://www.olympedia.org/results/56066) | 40.26 km (25.01 miles) |
| [1904 St Louis](https://www.olympedia.org/results/56131) | 40 km (24.85 miles) |
| [1908 London](https://www.olympedia.org/results/56369) | 42.195 km (26 miles 385 yards) |
| [1912 Stockholm](https://www.olympedia.org/results/56648) | 40.2 km (24.98 miles) |
| [1920 Antwerp](https://www.olympedia.org/results/57135) | 42.75 km (26.56 miles) |
| [1924 Paris](https://www.olympedia.org/results/57408) | 42.195 km (26 miles 385 yards) |

[Olympedia](https://www.olympedia.org/results/56369) says the IAAF declared 26 miles 385 yards the international distance in **1921**, the year [World Athletics' own page](https://worldathletics.org/disciplines/road-running/marathon) gives, and it records the [**1924** race in Paris](https://www.olympedia.org/results/57408) as the first Olympic marathon over that distance since London. [Antwerp's 42.75 kilometres](https://www.olympedia.org/results/57135) in 1920 was the longest in Olympic history.

Had the runners kept the royal entrance and run the full lap, the 1908 race would have ended a third of a mile past mile 26, not a fifth. Strictly FYI.
