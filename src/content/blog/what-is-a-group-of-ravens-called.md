Turn to the leaf signed **f iiij** in a book printed at St Albans in **1486** and a hunting poem in rhyming couplets simply stops. Beneath it the printer set a colophon, "[Explicit Dam Iulyans Barnes in her boke of huntyng](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/71266)", which closed the treatise and left him seven blank pages to fill before the next section could begin.

He filled them with the beasts of the chase, the names of hounds, the points of a good greyhound and a good horse, proverbs, a folk rhyme about two wives in one house, and a long list of words. Partway down that list, between [a fflight of Doues and a Clateryng of choughes](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/71266), sits the line the internet has quoted ever since: **an vnkyndenes of Rauenes**.

## What is a group of ravens called?

A group of ravens is called an **unkindness**. The term is older than the book that made it famous. An unkindness of ravens already appears in a fifteenth-century manuscript word list that John Hodgkin [dated to about 1452](https://archive.org/details/transact071000philuoft), and in a list [Caxton printed](https://caxtonandbeyond.dmu.ac.uk/editions/16_Lyd_Hor.html) around 1476, a decade before the Book of St Albans. In the wild, the birds that gather are mostly young non-breeders, and [non-breeding individuals may occupy communal roosts](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Corvus_corax/) while settled adults keep to themselves.

## The list was printed to fill seven blank pages

William Blades reproduced the whole 1486 edition [in facsimile in 1881](https://archive.org/details/cu31924031031184) and worked out how the page came to exist. Each treatise was set to end inside its own gathering of leaves, so that it could stand alone, and the hunting rhymes ran out early.

> The same idea controlled the arrangement of "The Book of Hunting," which, beginning on sig. e j, ends with Dame Juliana's "Explicit" on the recto of sig. f iiij. This left the last seven pages of the quaternion to be filled up.

Blades adds that early printers filled such gaps with "[such common household aphorisms or popular rhymes as came easily to the memory](https://archive.org/details/cu31924031031184)", and that the word list "was evidently copied from some MS." The printer had paper to use up and reached for a list already in circulation.

The colophon carrying Juliana Barnes's name sits **before** the list, so crediting her with the unkindness of ravens credits her with a page printed outside the one section her name is attached to. Blades noted that the name was "altered by degrees" into Dame Juliana Berners and credited with the whole volume, wrote that "[what is really known of the Dame is almost nothing](https://archive.org/details/cu31924031031184)", and found that it does not appear in the lists of fifteenth-century prioresses of Sopwell, the nunnery she is said to have run.

## Fourteen lists carry the raven term

In 1909 John Hodgkin printed transcripts of every such list he could find, for the Philological Society. The Egerton manuscript, "presumably the oldest list" in Hodgkin's words, heads its list "[Termys of venery](https://archive.org/details/transact071000philuoft)", says the words are there so that a young gentleman "fayle not in hys propyr termys", and gives "A Vnkyndenys of rauynnys" with "A mursher of Crowys" on the next line, a word Hodgkin thought was "intended possibly to represent the noise of crows".

Caxton had already [printed a version](https://caxtonandbeyond.dmu.ac.uk/editions/16_Lyd_Hor.html) around 1476, a few leaves after the end of Lydgate's poem The Hors, the Shepe and the Ghoos, with "[an vnkindnes of rauons](https://archive.org/details/transact071000philuoft)" and "a Murther of crowes" side by side. In Hodgkin's combined table of the lists, manuscript and print, the raven term appears **fourteen times**, spelled more than ten ways, and no rival word for ravens appears beside it.

## The list taught gentlemen the proper terms

The St Albans version has [164 numbered entries in Hodgkin's transcript](https://archive.org/details/transact071000philuoft), and Blades called it "[the list of proper terms to be used by gentlemen and those curious in their speech](https://archive.org/details/cu31924031031184)".

Many entries are animals. The rest include **a Cast of Brede**, **a Clustre of Grapys**, **a Couple or a payer of botillis** and, under a heading about beasts and fowls, **a Rage of the teethe**. The list also covers people, often mockingly: a superfluity of nuns, a melody of harpers, a subtlety of sergeants, an abominable sight of monks, a disworship of Scots and an uncredibility of cuckolds.

Not one of the entries carries an explanation. The book never says why ravens are an unkindness, or what a rage of the teeth is.

## The crow line is just as old

The word **murder** appears nowhere in the 1486 text. Crows turn up twice in its hawking treatise, once as quarry beside rooks and ravens and once as food for a hawk after its moult, while [rooks got "a beldyng of Rookes" and starlings "a Murmuracion of stares"](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/71266). Egerton's and Caxton's lists carry a crow line; the St Albans list has none.

The table sets the famous terms against the Egerton manuscript and the 1486 print, as Hodgkin transcribed them.

| Term | Egerton MS 1995, fifteenth century | Book of St Albans, 1486 |
|---|---|---|
| unkindness of ravens | A Vnkyndenys of rauynnys | an vnkyndenes of Rauenes |
| crows (now "murder") | A mursher of Crowys | absent |
| building of rooks | A Byldynge of Rookys | a beldyng of Rookes |
| murmuration of starlings | A murmuracyon of Starys | a Murmuracion of stares |
| conspiracy of ravens | absent | absent |

The conspiracy, the treachery and the parliament of ravens printed beside unkindness online appear in neither list, and their origin is undocumented. James Lipton's An Exaltation of Larks, a collection of terms "both real and created by Lipton himself", [was first published in 1968](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Lipton). Penguin, whose edition dates from 1993, describes it as "[a brilliant compendium of more than 1,100 resurrected or newly minted contributions](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/298626/an-exaltation-of-larks-by-james-lipton/9780140170962/)" to English, running from old terms to new ones such as a slouch of models and a pocket of quarterbacks.

## Unkind may not have meant cruel

The Old English adjective [un-cynde is defined simply as "Unnatural"](https://bosworthtoller.com/32736) in Bosworth and Toller's Anglo-Saxon dictionary. The older sense was still live in 1486: the hunting rhymes have a roebuck that bells "[by kynde](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/71266)", and Blades glosses "kyndeli" in this book as "natural".

No list says which sense the compiler meant. Ravens may have been unkind as in unnatural, or unkind as in cruel.

## The neglectful-parent story is old lore and bad biology

The cruelty reading does have a medieval source, though not one tied to the word. The Aberdeen Bestiary, [made in England around 1200](https://www.abdn.ac.uk/bestiary/about.php), says of the raven that "[this bird does not feed them fully until it sees that they have black feathers similar to its own](https://www.abdn.ac.uk/bestiary/ms24/f37r)". None of the word lists cites that belief, so linking it to unkindness is a later guess.

The biology runs the other way. The University of Michigan's Animal Diversity Web records that [both parents care for the young once they have hatched](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Corvus_corax/), and that the young leave the nest between **5 and 7 weeks** of age.

## The crowd you see is made of teenagers

Modern raven research supplies what the word lists skip. In a 2009 PLOS ONE paper, Sasha Dall and Jonathan Wright set out how ravens feed: "[Carcasses are usually discovered and defended by the local resident territorial pair of adult birds, and it normally requires groups of floating non-territorial juveniles to displace them](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2646834/)."

The crowd is the cohort without property. Non-breeding juveniles gather at communal winter roosts that [appear to function as 'information centers'](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2646834/) about where food has turned up. At the Newborough roost on Anglesey, Dall and Wright cite [a main roost of 500 to 1500 birds](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2646834/), with sub-roosts of about **30 birds each** whose members search and feed together as a gang.

Numbers matter at a carcass: [it takes about 7 birds to overcome the resource defense](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2646834/) of the breeding pair whose territory it is on. Outside the winter crowd, the Animal Diversity Web notes that [most commonly ravens occur alone or in pairs](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Corvus_corax/).

So the word for a group of ravens is older than the book that made it famous, it travelled with a word for crows that the 1486 list lacks, and the birds it now describes are mostly adolescents loitering near a carcass because they do not yet own anywhere better to be. Strictly FYI.
