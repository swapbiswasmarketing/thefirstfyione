The sailfish's famous top speed, **68 miles an hour**, was worked out from [100 yards in 3 seconds](https://archive.org/details/sim_country-life_1941-09-19_90_2331), timed with a stopwatch. The 100 yards were fishing line, running off a reel with a hooked sailfish on the other end.

Tail beats recorded by accelerometers and high-speed video put hunting sailfish at [under a third of that speed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25898843/). Tests of the fish's swimming muscle put its likely top speed not much higher.

## What is the fastest fish?

The sailfish is the usual answer, credited with [**68 mph**](https://archive.org/details/sim_country-life_1941-09-19_90_2331), though some lists give the black marlin [**80 mph**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fastest_animals). Both figures come from hooked fish pulling line off a reel. Sailfish muscle puts its top speed nearer **19 mph** (8.3 m/s), and [a 2016 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5087677/) noted that no earlier study had unequivocally tested the top speed of billfishes, the group that includes sailfish and marlins.

Each figure comes from a different method:

- **68 mph (109 km/h):** a hooked fish's run off a reel, timed with a stopwatch, in print by 1937.
- **About 19 mph (8.3 m/s):** the theoretical maximum set by the twitch time of its mid-body swimming muscle, from a 2016 study.
- **About 16 mph (7 m/s):** bursts estimated from tail beats recorded with accelerometers and high-speed video on hunting sailfish, from a 2015 study.
- **22 to 34 mph (10 to 15 m/s):** the fastest any swimmer near the surface can go before its tail fin cavitates, from a 2008 analysis.

## The 68 mph was clocked on fishing line

The reel story was in print by January 1924, when the [Mexia Daily News](https://archive.org/details/mexia-daily-news-1924-01-08) in Texas passed on a claim from Hamilton M. Wright in McClure's Magazine: the sailfish "has been known to run out 100 yards of line in less than three seconds."

Lionel Walford's 1937 book [*Marine Game Fishes of the Pacific Coast from Alaska to the Equator*](https://archive.org/details/bwb_W6-AUX-200) reported a speed for such runs. Frank Lane quoted it in [Country Life](https://archive.org/details/sim_country-life_1941-09-19_90_2331) on September 19, 1941, in an article titled "How Fast Do Fish Swim?" Asking whether any living animal could swim 70 miles an hour, Lane thought the answer was "Yes" and offered Walford's passage "as proof":

> "In the Atlantic the runs of hooked specimens have been timed with a stop watch, and the speed attained has been reckoned at as much as 100 yards in 3 seconds or, roughly, 68 miles an hour." - Lionel Walford, quoted by Frank Lane in *Country Life*, 1941

The same article shows how fish were clocked in that era. A ["fish-o-meter,"](https://archive.org/details/sim_country-life_1941-09-19_90_2331) a motorcycle speedometer with a cable and pulley mounted on a rod and reel, registered **44 miles an hour** when its co-designer, H. Earl Thompson of Liverpool, Nova Scotia, hooked a 60-pound tuna.

In 1958 Robert L. Wisner of the Scripps Institution of Oceanography repeated the number in [Pacific Science](https://archive.org/details/biostor-233944): "A hooked sailfish was clocked at 100 yards in three seconds, or approximately 68 miles per hour (Walford, 1937)."

Hamilton M. Wright, the writer behind the newspaper claim, is also the source for where the timing took place. Gerald Wood's [*Guinness Book of Animal Facts and Feats*](https://archive.org/details/guinnessbookofan0000wood) of 1976, citing Wright as quoted by Hunt in 1935, places the run in "speed trials carried out with a stopwatch at the Long Key Fishing Camp, Florida, USA, between 1910 and 1925." Neither that passage nor the one Lane quoted names the angler or the day. That leaves the famous figure as an unverified angling report about a fish on a line.

## Muscle twitches put the sailfish's top speed near 19 mph

In February 2015, Morten Svendsen and ten co-authors examined four sailfish caught on rod and reel off Cancun, Mexico, along with barracuda, little tunny and dorado. Within five minutes of each fish's death, they triggered its swimming muscle with an electrical stimulus and used the shortest contraction time from at least eight twitches, as they reported in [Biology Open](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5087677/) in 2016 under the subtitle "a myth revisited."

A fish's top speed is set by how fast its muscle can drive the tail and how far each tail beat carries it. Sailfish muscle near mid-body took **68.5 milliseconds** to twitch, [enough for about 7.4 tail beats a second](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5087677/), and wild sailfish move about 0.76 of their length per beat. For fish averaging 1.47 meters from the tip of the mouth to the tail fork, leaving out the bill, that works out to **8.3 meters per second**, about 19 mph. That made sailfish the fastest of the four species in absolute terms, but they were also 50 to 80 centimeters longer than the others, and corrected for size they came last.

The authors call the figure a theoretical maximum, because the muscle was tested unloaded, in fish taken out of the water, and, [as they write](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5087677/), "in water, fish muscles are loaded and so lower speeds are more likely." Using the fastest-twitching muscle instead, 15 percent of the way back from the head, [the same method gives 11.45 meters per second](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5087677/), and the authors conclude that sailfish are unlikely to swim faster than 10 to 15 m/s.

Early estimates of [35 meters per second](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5087677/) for sailfish and black marlin rest on "fishermen's records of hooked specimens," the authors write, and are "most likely overestimations." Reaching that speed would take about 3.4 body lengths per tail beat, yet swimming fish "have only rarely been observed to have a stride length exceeding 1 body length."

## Hunting sailfish burst at about 16 mph

Stefano Marras and colleagues recorded sailfish hunting with high-frequency accelerometers and high-speed video, estimated their speeds from tail-beat frequencies, and titled their 2015 paper ["Not So Fast"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25898843/). Their results suggest the fish burst at about **7 meters per second** and do not exceed 10 m/s.

## Bubbles on the tail fin cap speed near the surface at about 34 mph

As water speeds up around the leading edge of a tail fin, its pressure drops, and at high enough speed it falls so low that the water turns to vapor bubbles. That is cavitation, and bubbles collapsing on a fin can damage its surface, Iosilevskii and Weihs explain in [a 2008 analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2607394/).

They put the [maximum cavitation-free speed](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2607394/) for any swimmer near the surface at **10 to 15 meters per second**, about 22 to 34 mph, and found speeds above 15 m/s "hardly possible at a shallow depth." Tunas and mackerels, which lack pain receptors in their tail fins, may cross the limit briefly, and fin damage from it has been observed. Dolphins, the authors suggest, probably cannot cross it without pain.

The famous reel figure works out to [about 30 meters per second](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10952363/), twice the top of that range.

## A cheetah beats the sailfish's muscle estimate about three to one

On paper the sailfish wins, since its reel figure beats the **58 mph** (25.9 m/s) that GPS and motion-sensor collars recorded on wild cheetahs in Botswana, the top speed across [367 runs by five animals](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23765495/) reported in Nature in 2013. Set against the muscle tests, the cheetah's 25.9 m/s is about three times the sailfish's estimated top speed.

## The black marlin and tuna records lean on reels too

The black marlin's [80 mph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fastest_animals) (129 km/h) rests on the claim that "a hooked black marlin has been recorded stripping line off a fishing reel" at that rate. The list that gives it cites a 2008 BBC wildlife clip and tags the figure "dubious."

Tuna figures split by method as well. A rod-and-reel measurement published in 1964 gave a yellowfin tuna **20.7 meters per second**, while muscle twitch times put an Atlantic bluefin's maximum at 15 m/s, [as Svendsen's team summarizes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5087677/).

Tags, which ride on the fish themselves, record far lower numbers. In a 2023 study, bio-loggers on two [shortfin mako sharks](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10952363/) caught a top burst of **5.02 m/s**, a speed the authors place "among the highest so far directly measured among sharks, tunas and billfishes." A 1992 study using acoustic tags on three [blue marlin](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.166.1.267) recorded short bursts of up to 2.25 m/s.

| Fish | Top figure | Method (year published) |
|---|---|---|
| Black marlin | 80 mph (129 km/h) | Hooked fish stripping line off a reel (undated; cited to a 2008 BBC video) |
| Sailfish | 68 mph (109 km/h) | Hooked fish's run timed with a stopwatch (1937) |
| Yellowfin tuna | 46 mph (20.7 m/s) | Rod and reel (1964) |
| Atlantic bluefin tuna | 34 mph (15 m/s) | Muscle twitch times (1989) |
| Sailfish | 19 mph (8.3 m/s) | Muscle twitch times (2016) |
| Shortfin mako shark | 11 mph (5.02 m/s) | Bio-loggers on two sharks, top burst recorded (2023) |
| Blue marlin | 5 mph (2.25 m/s) | Acoustic tags on three fish (1992) |

Sorted by speed, the table also sorts by method: reels at the top, muscle tests in the middle, tags at the bottom. The tag figures show only the bursts the tags happened to catch, and the mako study's authors judge that their own top speed ["may underestimate the maximum speed achievable by shortfin mako."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10952363/)

Sailfish do not hunt by top speed anyway. In high-speed video of Atlantic sailfish attacking schooling sardines, Paolo Domenici and colleagues saw them slide their bills into a school ["without eliciting an evasive response,"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24759865/) then slash sideways with "one of the highest accelerations ever recorded in an aquatic vertebrate." The fish famous for its speed catches its food by sneaking up on it. Strictly FYI.
