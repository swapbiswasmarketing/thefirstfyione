The sailfish's famous top speed, **68 miles an hour** (110 km/h), was worked out from **100 yards in 3 seconds**, timed with a stopwatch. The 100 yards were fishing line, running off a reel with a hooked sailfish on the other end.

Accelerometers and high-speed video have caught hunting sailfish at under a third of that speed. A 2016 study of sailfish muscle put the fish's limit in the same range, and its authors subtitled their paper "a myth revisited."

## What is the fastest fish?

The sailfish is the usual answer, credited with **68 mph** (110 km/h), though some lists give the black marlin **80 mph**. Both figures come from hooked fish pulling line off a reel. Sailfish muscle puts its top speed nearer **19 mph** (8.3 m/s), and a 2016 study noted no earlier study had unequivocally tested a sailfish's or marlin's top speed.

Each figure comes from a different method:

- **68 mph (110 km/h):** a hooked fish's run off a reel, timed with a stopwatch, in print by 1937.
- **About 19 mph (8.3 m/s):** the limit set by the twitch time of its swimming muscle, from a 2016 study.
- **About 16 mph (7 m/s):** bursts recorded with accelerometers and high-speed video on sailfish hunting sardines, from a 2015 study.
- **22 to 34 mph (10 to 15 m/s):** the fastest any swimmer near the surface can go before its tail fin cavitates, from a 2008 analysis.

## The 68 mph was clocked on fishing line

The reel story was in print by January 1924, when the [Mexia Daily News](https://archive.org/details/mexia-daily-news-1924-01-08) in Texas passed on a claim from Hamilton M. Wright in McClure's Magazine: the sailfish "has been known to run out 100 yards of line in less than three seconds."

Lionel Walford's 1937 book *Marine Game Fishes of the Pacific Coast* reported a speed for such runs. Frank Lane quoted it in [Country Life](https://archive.org/details/sim_country-life_1941-09-19_90_2331) on September 19, 1941, in an article titled "How Fast Do Fish Swim?" Asking whether any animal could swim 70 miles an hour, Lane answered "Yes" and offered Walford's passage "as proof":

> "In the Atlantic the runs of hooked specimens have been timed with a stop watch, and the speed attained has been reckoned at as much as 100 yards in 3 seconds or, roughly, 68 miles an hour." - Lionel Walford, quoted by Frank Lane in *Country Life*, 1941

The same article shows how fish were clocked in that era. A "fish-o-meter," a motorcycle speedometer with a cable and pulley mounted on a rod and reel, registered **44 miles an hour** for a hooked 60-pound tuna.

In 1958 Robert L. Wisner of the Scripps Institution of Oceanography repeated the number in [Pacific Science](https://archive.org/details/biostor-233944): "A hooked sailfish was clocked at 100 yards in three seconds, or approximately 68 miles per hour (Walford, 1937)."

Wikipedia's [list of the fastest animals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fastest_animals) places the timing at a fishing camp at Long Key, Florida, and concedes that the speed "includes leaps out of the water." It tags its version "citation needed" and "dubious." The passage Lane quoted names no camp, no date and no angler. That leaves the 68 mph as an unverified angling report about a fish on a line.

## Muscle twitches put the sailfish's limit near 19 mph

In February 2015, Morten Svendsen and ten co-authors examined four sailfish caught on rod and reel off Cancun, Mexico, along with barracuda, little tunny and dorado. Within five minutes of each fish's death, they triggered its swimming muscle with an electrical stimulus and timed the fastest twitch, as they reported in [Biology Open](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5087677/) in 2016.

A fish's top speed is set by how fast its muscle can drive the tail and how far each tail beat carries it. Sailfish muscle near mid-body took **68.5 milliseconds** to twitch, enough for about 7.4 tail beats a second, and wild sailfish move about 0.76 of their length per beat. For fish averaging 1.47 meters, that works out to **8.3 meters per second**, about 19 mph.

The figure is a ceiling. The muscle was tested unloaded, in fish taken out of the water, and "in water, fish muscles are loaded and so lower speeds are more likely," the authors write. To reach the **35 meters per second** (about 78 mph) of early estimates for sailfish and black marlin, a sailfish would have to cover about 3.4 body lengths per tail beat, yet swimming fish "have only rarely been observed to have a stride length exceeding 1 body length."

Sailfish were the fastest of the four species in absolute terms, but they were also 50 to 80 centimeters longer than the others, and corrected for size they came last.

## Hunting sailfish burst at about 16 mph

Stefano Marras and colleagues recorded sailfish hunting schooling sardines with high-frequency accelerometers and high-speed video, and titled their 2015 paper ["Not So Fast"](https://doi.org/10.1093/icb/icv017). The fish burst at about **7 meters per second** and stayed under 10 m/s.

Instruments can still miss a record. Recordings like these cover short stretches of time, Svendsen's team cautions, which lowers the odds of catching the rare moments when an animal goes flat out.

## Bubbles on the tail fin cap speed near the surface at about 34 mph

As water speeds up around the leading edge of a tail fin, its pressure drops, as the air's does over [a plane's wing](/blog/how-do-planes-fly/), and at high enough speed it falls so low that the water turns to vapor bubbles. That is cavitation, and bubbles collapsing on a fin can damage its surface, Iosilevskii and Weihs explain in [a 2008 analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2607394/).

They put the fastest cavitation-free speed for any swimmer near the surface at **10 to 15 meters per second**, about 22 to 34 mph, and found speeds above 15 m/s "hardly possible at a shallow depth." Tunas, which lack pain receptors in their tail fins, may cross the limit briefly, and fin damage from it has been observed. Dolphins, the authors suggest, probably cannot cross it without pain.

The famous 68 mph is about 30 meters per second, twice the top of that range.

## A cheetah beats the sailfish about three to one

On paper the sailfish wins: 68 mph against the **58 mph** (25.9 m/s) that GPS and motion-sensor collars recorded on wild cheetahs in Botswana, the top speed across 367 runs by five animals, [reported in Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature12295) in 2013. On the measurements, the cheetah's 25.9 m/s is about three times the sailfish's estimated limit of 8.3 meters per second.

"We can't beat most animals in a sprint, but we best many of them in endurance running," the Harvard anthropologist Daniel Lieberman [told the *Harvard Gazette*](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2004/11/running-paced-human-evolution/) in 2004, making the still-disputed case that the human body [evolved to run long distances](/blog/when-was-running-invented/).

## The black marlin and tuna records lean on reels too

Wikipedia's list gives its top fish speed to the black marlin, **80 mph** (129 km/h), noting that "a hooked black marlin has been recorded stripping line off a fishing reel" at that rate. It cites a 2008 BBC video clip and tags the figure "dubious." Svendsen's team says the early estimates of about 35 meters per second rest on "fishermen's records of hooked specimens" and are "most likely overestimations," and notes that "no previous study has unequivocally tested the maximum speed attainable in billfishes."

Tuna figures split by method as well. A rod-and-reel measurement published in 1964 gave a yellowfin tuna **20.7 meters per second**, while muscle twitch times put an Atlantic bluefin's maximum at 15 m/s, as Svendsen's team summarizes.

Tags, which ride on the fish themselves, record far lower numbers. In a 2023 study, bio-loggers on two [shortfin mako sharks](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10952363/) caught a top burst of **5.02 m/s**, a speed the authors place "among the highest so far directly measured among sharks, tunas and billfishes." A 1992 study using acoustic tags on three [blue marlin](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.166.1.267) recorded short bursts of up to 2.25 m/s.

| Fish | Top figure | Method (year published) |
|---|---|---|
| Black marlin | 80 mph (129 km/h) | Hooked fish stripping line off a reel (undated; cited to a 2008 BBC video) |
| Sailfish | 68 mph (110 km/h) | Hooked fish's run timed with a stopwatch (1937) |
| Yellowfin tuna | 46 mph (20.7 m/s) | Rod and reel (1964) |
| Atlantic bluefin tuna | 34 mph (15 m/s) | Muscle twitch times (1989) |
| Sailfish | 19 mph (8.3 m/s) | Muscle twitch times (2016) |
| Shortfin mako shark | 11 mph (5.02 m/s) | Bio-loggers on two sharks, top burst recorded (2023) |
| Blue marlin | 5 mph (2.25 m/s) | Acoustic tags on three fish (1992) |

Sorted by speed, the table also sorts by method: reels at the top, muscle tests in the middle, tags at the bottom. The tag figures are the fastest bursts the tags happened to catch, not maximums. Better measurements have shrunk a famous animal number before: the sloth's [15 hours of sleep a day](/blog/why-are-sloths-slow/), recorded in captivity, lost more than six hours when wild sloths wore the recorders.

Sailfish do not hunt by top speed anyway. In high-speed video of sailfish attacking sardines off Cancun, Paolo Domenici and colleagues saw them slide their bills into a school ["without eliciting an evasive response,"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4043100/) then slash sideways with "one of the highest accelerations ever recorded in an aquatic vertebrate." The ocean's most famous speedster catches its food by sneaking up on it. Strictly FYI.
