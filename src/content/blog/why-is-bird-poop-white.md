Park a car under a tree for an afternoon and the mess on the roof is usually built the same way: a chalky white splash with a smaller dark blob near its centre. Two colours, one splat.

Almost nobody stops to ask why one animal would fire out two different substances at once. The white is the bird's urine. The dark speck is the actual droppings. And in one modern X-ray study, the compound the standard explanations credit for the colour did not show up in a single sample. In another, it did.

## Why is bird poop white?

The white part of a bird dropping is [urine, not feces](https://www.jsg.utexas.edu/news/2019/09/bird-droppings-defy-expectations/). Birds have no bladder, so urine from the kidneys drains straight into the cloaca, the single exit that also carries waste from the gut. Water is reclaimed on the way, leaving a pale paste that lands at the same moment as the darker feces.

A 2026 review of comparative renal physiology in Poultry Science states the anatomy without hedging: in birds, [a urinary bladder is absent](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13136738/), and the kidneys feed ureters that empty directly into the urodeum of the cloaca.

## Birds have no bladder, so the gut finishes the job

The same review sets the two plumbing systems side by side. Cattle excrete much of their nitrogen as urea, a highly soluble compound that passes out in urine through a bladder and urethra. Birds take the opposite route: protein nitrogen is turned into uric acid in the liver, and uric acid barely dissolves, so the waste can leave as a semi-solid paste with very little water going with it. The review calls that chemistry [a water-saving adaptation crucial for flight and arid environments](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13136738/).

The finishing work happens after the kidneys. Ureteral urine drops into the cloaca, and some of it is then pushed backwards by reverse peristalsis into the colon and the ceca. A 2022 study of nitrogen recycling in broiler chickens, also in Poultry Science, puts it in [one line](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8866718/): "Reflux from the cloaca through the colon selectively delivers fluids, including urine, to the ceca."

How much goes back is not fixed. Estimates of the share of urinary nitrogen that reaches the ceca [range from 5 to 48 percent](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8866718/), depending on whether the bird has eaten and how much protein its diet holds.

The same paper notes, citing earlier work, that those backward contractions are controlled by how well hydrated the bird is, which [suggests](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8866718/) their "main purpose is to maintain water balance of the bird." It adds that the reflux is also thought to conserve protein from the urine. The water it pulls back is part of how liquid urine ends up as a crust on your paintwork.

## The 2019 X-ray found no uric acid in any sample

In 2018 Nicholas Crouch, a postdoctoral researcher at the University of Texas at Austin, had a conversation with Jackson School professor Bob Folk, who claimed that bird waste contains no uric acid. Crouch decided to check. With Vincent Lynch, a UT chemist, and Jackson School professor Julia Clarke, he collected fresh excreta from six species, [most of it from birds kept at the Austin Zoo](https://www.jsg.utexas.edu/news/2019/09/bird-droppings-defy-expectations/) and one sample from a neighbour's backyard chicken flock, then ran the urine fraction through X-ray diffraction. The species ran from the Great Horned Owl to the domestic chicken and, by the university's account, took in the three major groupings of birds.

**None of the samples produced a diffraction pattern consistent with uric acid.** What came back instead was **ammonium urate**, **struvite** (magnesium ammonium phosphate) and **two compounds the authors could not identify**. The paper appeared in the Journal of Ornithology in 2019, and [the authors' own archived data record](https://datadryad.org/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.d39b44r) states it flatly: "None of the analyzed samples were found to contain uric acid."

Crouch offers a bacterial culprit as a likely cause rather than a demonstrated one: [he says](https://www.jsg.utexas.edu/news/2019/09/bird-droppings-defy-expectations/) the compounds are probably what is left after bacteria inside the bird's gut break the uric acid down before it is excreted. The paper points the same way, citing earlier research showing that waste [may be modified in the ceca immediately prior to it being expelled](https://datadryad.org/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.d39b44r).

## The uric acid answer is the one Audubon and BBC Wildlife give

[Audubon's page on the question](https://www.audubon.org/news/why-bird-poop-white), credited to BirdNote, tells readers that "birds, unlike mammals, don't produce urine. Instead they excrete nitrogenous wastes in the form of uric acid, which emerges as a white paste." At BBC Wildlife, [Mike Toms writes](https://www.discoverwildlife.com/animal-facts/birds/why-is-bird-poo-white) that "whitish crystals of uric acid are deposited after some of the water has been reabsorbed in the cloaca." His page also mentions urates, the salts of uric acid, which sits closer to what the Austin team found.

Neither page invented the idea. The 2019 authors acknowledge that it has [long been concluded from chemical analyses, and more recently from genetic studies, that uric acid is the principal constituent of urine in Aves](https://datadryad.org/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.d39b44r). Their X-ray work was a test of that hypothesis, and in their six species it failed.

## Folk challenged the uric acid answer in 1969, and the argument is still running

Folk had already run the experiment once. [In the 1960s he examined samples from 17 species](https://www.jsg.utexas.edu/news/2019/09/bird-droppings-defy-expectations/), using X-ray diffraction and solubility tests. His paper, ["Spherical urine in birds: petrography"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17742853/), appeared in Science in 1969 and describes the white material as "microscopically uniform spheres 2 to 8 micrometers in diameter."

Its abstract ends on a pun. X-ray analysis, Folk wrote, shows that bird urine ["does not consist largely of uric acid, as has frequently been parroted."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17742853/) That is more cautious than what he told Crouch decades later, when he said bird waste held no uric acid at all.

Biologists pushed back fast. A 2025 study in the Journal of the American Chemical Society describes Folk's paper as ["a direct challenge to consensus opinion that elicited a strong response from prominent biologists."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12593373/) One reply, printed in Science in October 1970, ran under the title ["Uric acid: the main nitrogenous excretory product of birds."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5452903/)

In 1971 Science printed an X-ray challenge. Kathleen Lonsdale and D.J. Sutor ran X-ray diffraction on [the white excrement from a budgerigar](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5573570/) and concluded that it "consists mainly of uric acid dihydrate" (Science, volume 172, May 1971).

The argument did not end there. The 2025 study notes that Lonsdale and Sutor saw [the same single intense X-ray peak](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12593373/) that Folk had reported, and that a urate from a greater rhea gave a similar pattern. Its authors match that peak to uric acid monohydrate, a form of uric acid, and call Folk's position ["the erroneous claim that birds do not excrete uric acid."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12593373/)

> Birds route their nitrogen through uric acid; that part is not in dispute. What form it is in by the time it reaches your windscreen is the part the X-ray studies still disagree on.

## The ostrich pees first, and separately

The idea that birds cannot urinate at all runs into trouble with the ostrich. Gary Duke, A. Allan Degen and James Reynhout followed urine through the lower colon and cloaca of ostriches for [The Condor in 1995](https://academic.oup.com/condor/article/97/1/165-173/5126302). In their birds no urodeum was evident. Urine was stored mainly in the proctodeum, which the authors [describe](https://academic.oup.com/condor/article/97/1/165-173/5126302) as acting as a "bladder" that changed size dramatically: in eight-week-old chicks it measured 6 to 8 cm across before urination and 1 to 2 cm after.

Their abstract leaves no room on the order: urine and feces ["were excreted separately (urine first)"](https://academic.oup.com/condor/article/97/1/165-173/5126302). An ostrich urinates, then defecates. The combined splat is a habit of most birds, not a limit on all of them.

Ostriches also skip most of the gut's finishing work. The study saw no backward flow from the cloaca into the colon on X-ray, and concludes that ostriches ["rely mainly on kidney function for final composition of voided fluid."](https://academic.oup.com/condor/article/97/1/165-173/5126302)

| The standard answer | What the research found |
|---|---|
| Birds do not produce urine | Birds produce urine; they have no bladder to store it in |
| The white paste is uric acid | Disputed: none found in one study of six species; a later study read a rhea sample as uric acid monohydrate |
| Every bird voids both wastes together | The ostrich urinates before it defecates |

So the white streak on the car roof is bird urine, with the water the bird could not spare pulled back before it left. Whether its nitrogen is still uric acid by then, or ammonium urate and struvite, depends on whose X-ray you trust. Those compounds also turn up in human kidney stones: in [a 2019 to 2023 series of kidney stones from a national referral centre in Iran](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11840317/), uric acid accounted for 44.7 percent of stones analysed, struvite for 2.6 percent and ammonium urate for 0.4 percent. What you are scraping off the windscreen is, mineralogically, a kidney stone the bird had the sense not to keep. Strictly FYI.
