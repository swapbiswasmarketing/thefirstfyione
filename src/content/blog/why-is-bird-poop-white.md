Park a car under a tree for an afternoon and the mess on the roof is always built the same way: a chalky white splash with a smaller dark blob near its centre. Two colours, one splat.

Almost nobody stops to ask why one animal would fire out two different substances at once. The white is the bird's urine. The dark speck is the actual droppings. And the compound every encyclopedia credits for the colour did not show up in a single sample the last time anyone checked.

## Why is bird poop white?

The white part of a bird dropping is urine, not feces. Birds have no bladder, so urine from the kidneys drains straight into the cloaca, the single exit that also carries waste from the gut. Most of the water is reclaimed on the way, leaving a pale paste that lands at the same moment as the darker feces.

A 2026 review of comparative renal physiology in Poultry Science states the anatomy without hedging: in birds, [a urinary bladder is absent](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13136738/), and the kidneys feed ureters that empty directly into the urodeum of the cloaca.

## Birds have no bladder, so the gut finishes the job

Mammals dissolve nitrogen waste in water as urea and flush it away. That costs water, which is why a bladder earns its keep. Birds took the opposite route. The liver converts nitrogen into uric acid, which barely dissolves, so the waste can leave as a semi-solid paste with very little water going with it. That same review calls the insoluble route a water-saving adaptation.

The finishing work happens after the kidneys. Ureteral urine drops into the cloaca and is then pushed backwards by reverse peristalsis into the colon and the ceca, where water is pulled back out of it. A 2022 study of nitrogen recycling in broiler chickens, also in Poultry Science, puts it in [one line](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8866718/): "Reflux from the cloaca through the colon selectively delivers fluids, including urine, to the ceca."

That reclaiming step is what turns liquid urine into the crust on your paintwork.

## The 2019 X-ray found no uric acid in any sample

In 2018 Nicholas Crouch, a postdoctoral researcher at the University of Texas at Austin, was told by Jackson School professor Bob Folk that bird waste contains no uric acid. Crouch decided to check. With chemist Vincent Lynch and Julia Clarke of the Jackson School, he collected fresh excreta from six species, [most of it from birds kept at the Austin Zoo](https://www.jsg.utexas.edu/news/2019/09/bird-droppings-defy-expectations/) and one sample from a neighbour's backyard chicken flock, then ran the urine fraction through X-ray diffraction.

The species ran from the Great Horned Owl to the domestic chicken and covered all three major groupings of birds. **None of the samples produced a diffraction pattern consistent with uric acid.** What came back instead was **ammonium urate**, **struvite** (magnesium ammonium phosphate) and **two compounds nobody has identified**. The paper appeared in the Journal of Ornithology in August 2019, and [the authors' own archived data record](https://datadryad.org/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.d39b44r) says the same thing: no analysed sample contained uric acid.

> Every step of the textbook answer holds up until the last one. Birds do route their nitrogen through uric acid, and then something inside the bird converts it again before it ever reaches your windscreen.

Crouch offers a bacterial culprit as a likely cause rather than a demonstrated one: he says the compounds are probably what is left after bacteria inside the bird's gut break the uric acid down before it is excreted. Microbes doing unbilled chemistry is a recurring answer in biology, which is part of why [the argument over whether the world could spare mosquitoes](/blog/do-we-need-mosquitoes/) keeps refusing to settle.

## The uric acid line is still what the biggest pages teach

[Audubon's page on the question](https://www.audubon.org/news/why-bird-poop-white), credited to BirdNote, tells readers that "birds, unlike mammals, don't produce urine. Instead they excrete nitrogenous wastes in the form of uric acid, which emerges as a white paste." At BBC Wildlife, [Mike Toms writes](https://www.discoverwildlife.com/animal-facts/birds/why-is-bird-poo-white) that "whitish crystals of uric acid are deposited after some of the water has been reabsorbed in the cloaca."

This one did not begin as folklore. It came out of a textbook simplification that hardened into a fact, and the pages ranking highest on the question are the ones still repeating it. Birds do produce urine, and the last time the white fraction went under an X-ray beam, uric acid was not among the compounds that came back.

A confident one-line explanation can outlive its accuracy by decades. The reason [Venus runs hotter than the planet sitting closer to the Sun](/blog/why-is-venus-hotter-than-mercury/) gets flattened in much the same way, into a single word that skips the step doing the work.

| The standard answer | What the X-ray work found |
|---|---|
| Birds do not produce urine | Birds produce urine; they have no bladder to store it in |
| The white paste is uric acid | Ammonium urate, struvite and two unidentified compounds |
| Every bird voids both wastes together | The ostrich urinates before it defecates |

## The correction was published in 1969 and lost by 1971

Folk had already run the experiment once. In the 1960s he collected samples from 17 species, found no sign of uric acid in them, and published the result in Science in 1969 as "Spherical urine in birds: petrography". His abstract describes the white material as microscopically uniform spheres 2 to 8 micrometres across, and reports that the X-ray examination contradicts the assumption that uric acid is its main component.

Two years later Science printed the rebuttal. Kathleen Lonsdale and D.J. Sutor ran the same technique on the white excrement of a single budgerigar and concluded that it "consists mainly of uric acid dihydrate" (Science 172, pages 958 to 959, May 1971). One parrot outvoted seventeen species, and the textbooks went with the parrot. Durable animal explanations are a genre of their own; [the clip of a cat leaping away from a cucumber](/blog/why-are-cats-afraid-of-cucumbers/) has had a comparable career.

## The ostrich pees first, and separately

The second casualty here is the idea that birds cannot urinate at all. Gary Duke, A. Allan Degen and James Reynhout tested that on ostriches for [The Condor in 1995](https://academic.oup.com/condor/article/97/1/165-173/5126302). An ostrich cloaca has no urodeum. Urine collects instead in the proctodeum, which the authors say acted as a "bladder" and changed size dramatically: in eight-week-old chicks it measured 6 to 8 cm across before urination and 1 to 2 cm after.

Their abstract leaves no room on the order: urine and feces "were excreted separately (urine first)". An ostrich urinates, then defecates. The combined splat is a habit of most birds, not a limit on all of them.

So the white streak on the car roof is bird urine, dried into ammonium urate and struvite after the bird pulled back the water it could not spare. Both minerals also grow inside people: in [a 2019 to 2023 series of kidney stones from a national referral centre in Iran](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11840317/), struvite accounted for 2.6 percent of stones analysed and ammonium urate for 0.4 percent. What you are scraping off the windscreen is, mineralogically, a kidney stone the bird had the sense not to keep. Strictly FYI.
