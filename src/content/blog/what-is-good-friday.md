Good Friday, the popular story goes, was once God's Friday, and the holy name wore down into one that sounds wrong for the day Jesus died. The story has been in print a long time. "Some say it is from 'God's Friday' (Gottes Freitag)," the Catholic Encyclopedia told its readers in a [1909 article on Good Friday](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/06643a.htm), one sentence after conceding that "the origin of the term Good is not clear."

The idea still circulates. An article on the name at [Crosswalk](https://www.crosswalk.com/special-coverage/easter/why-is-it-called-good-friday.html), a Christian site, quotes Justin Holcomb: "some believe it developed from an older name, 'God's Friday.'" The linguists Slate consulted reject it. "The origin from God is out of the question," Anatoly Liberman, a University of Minnesota professor who studies the origins of English words, told [Slate](https://slate.com/culture/2017/04/why-is-good-friday-called-good-friday-the-etymology-and-origins-of-the-holidays-name.html) in an article first published in 2014, and the lexicographer Ben Zimmer told the same reporter that "None of the early examples in the Oxford English Dictionary imply that it started off as God's rather than Good."

The [American Heritage Dictionary](https://www.ahdictionary.com/word/search.html?q=Good+Friday) derives the name from good in a sense it marks obsolete: pious, holy.

## What is Good Friday?

Good Friday is the Friday before Easter Sunday, [the Christian holy day](https://www.ahdictionary.com/word/search.html?q=Good+Friday) that marks the **crucifixion and death of Jesus**. The good in its name is [an old sense of the word](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Good_Friday), **holy**, as in the Good Book. Before English settled on Good Friday, it called the day **Long Friday**, and Danish, Norwegian, Swedish and Finnish still do.

The Gospels tie Jesus's death to a day of the week: [Mark's Gospel](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+15&version=KJV) sets it on "the preparation, that is, the day before the sabbath," and counts its hours. "It was the third hour, and they crucified him," the [King James text](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+15&version=KJV) reads, and darkness lay over the land "until the ninth hour," when Jesus cried out and died.

Because the day falls two days before Easter Sunday, its date moves with [the date of Easter](/blog/why-does-easter-change-every-year/), between **March 20 and April 23** in Western churches.

## Good meant holy, as in the Good Book

Jesse Sheidlower, then president of the American Dialect Society, gave [Slate](https://slate.com/culture/2017/04/why-is-good-friday-called-good-friday-the-etymology-and-origins-of-the-holidays-name.html) the short answer: "The answer seems pretty clearly to be that it's from good 'holy.'" The sense survives in relatives that the [Wiktionary entry](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Good_Friday) lists: good tide, once a name for Christmas or Shrovetide; Good Wednesday, for the Wednesday of Holy Week; and the Good Book, for the Bible.

The [Crosswalk article](https://www.crosswalk.com/special-coverage/easter/why-is-it-called-good-friday.html) also gives a theological answer: "It is a good day because he traded places for you and for me." That is a statement of belief about the day, and it explains good by the word's current sense. Forrest Wickman, who put the question to linguists for [Slate](https://slate.com/culture/2017/04/why-is-good-friday-called-good-friday-the-etymology-and-origins-of-the-holidays-name.html), granted that it is "certainly how many Christians today understand the name," but "not where the name originally comes from."

## The God's Friday story has a model in goodbye

The swap the story imagines did happen in another word. [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/goodbye) traces goodbye to God be with you, and [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/goodbye) puts the change of God to good down to "confusion with good morning, good night." Merriam-Webster gives Good Friday no such history: its [entry for the name](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Good%20Friday) derives it "from its special sanctity."

## Old English called it Long Friday

The Old English name was **langa frigedaeg**. The [Bosworth-Toller Anglo-Saxon Dictionary](https://bosworthtoller.com/21146) defines Langa-Frigedaeg as Good Friday and cites a reading note, at the start of chapter 18 in an Old English copy of John's Gospel, that assigns the reading of the Passion to that day.

The same entry quotes a church rule by Aelfric, an English abbot who [died around 1010](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%86lfric_of_Eynsham), in the [dictionary's translation](https://bosworthtoller.com/21146): "the eucharist must not be consecrated on Good Friday, for Christ suffered for us on that day." The rule is **canon 36** in the letter of canons Aelfric addressed to a bishop, and in [Benjamin Thorpe's 1840 translation](https://archive.org/details/ancientlawsandi00commgoog) it sets out the day's service: readings, the passion and prayers, a kiss for "the rood of God" and communion from bread hallowed on Thursday.

In his [Swedish etymological dictionary](https://runeberg.org/svetym/0522.html), Elof Hellquist traced Swedish [langfredag](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/l%C3%A5ngfredag), written with a ringed a, through Old Swedish langa freadagher to Old English langa frigedaeg, noting that English says Good Friday instead. Danish and Norwegian spell it [langfredag](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/langfredag) without the ring, and [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/pitk%C3%A4perjantai) calls Finnish pitkaperjantai, written with a dotted a, a calque of the Late Old Swedish name.

Why the day was long is less certain. [Den Store Danske](https://lex.dk/langfredag), the Danish encyclopedia, suggests that it perhaps felt long, as a day of fasting and abstinence or as a day of sorrow. The [same 1909 article](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/06643a.htm) that reported God's Friday recorded the older name:

> "Sometimes, too, the day was called Long Friday by the Anglo-Saxons; so today in Denmark." - Thomas Gilmartin, Catholic Encyclopedia, 1909

## Other languages call it holy, great or sorrowful

The [translations Wiktionary gathers](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Good_Friday) name the day for its length, its holiness, its greatness or its grief:

| Language | Name for the day | Literal meaning |
|---|---|---|
| English | Good Friday | Holy Friday, in an old sense of good |
| Dutch | Goede Vrijdag | Good Friday |
| Danish, Norwegian, Swedish | langfredag (Swedish with a ringed a) | Long Friday |
| Finnish | pitkaperjantai (with a dotted a) | Long Friday |
| German | Karfreitag | Friday of mourning |
| French, Spanish, Italian | vendredi saint, Viernes Santo, Venerdi Santo | Holy Friday |
| Greek | Megali Paraskevi | Great Friday |

The Kar of [Karfreitag](https://www.dwds.de/wb/Karfreitag) goes back to Old High German **kara**, mourning or lament, a word that has died out of modern German except as the Kar- in the names of Holy Week and its days, such as Karwoche, Karfreitag and [Karsamstag](https://www.dwds.de/wb/Karsamstag), Holy Saturday. The DWDS dictionary relates it to Old English caru and English care, which [in Old English](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/care) also meant sorrow and grief, and the [1909 encyclopedia](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/06643a.htm) rendered the German name as Sorrowful Friday. Zimmer turned the same fact against the God's Friday story when [Slate](https://slate.com/culture/2017/04/why-is-good-friday-called-good-friday-the-etymology-and-origins-of-the-holidays-name.html) asked him: the German for the day is Karfreitag, not Gottes Freitag, the German form the encyclopedia set beside God's Friday.

Greek kept the Gospel's own word. Paraskeue, preparation, meant "the preparation that was made on the sixth day for the Sabbath," the [Catholic Encyclopedia](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/06643a.htm) explains, and "the Greeks retained this use of the word as applied to every Friday," while Latin kept it for one. [Paraskevi](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CE%A0%CE%B1%CF%81%CE%B1%CF%83%CE%BA%CE%B5%CF%85%CE%AE) is still the Greek for Friday.

## No Mass is said, and the service starts near three

Aelfric's rule still holds in the Catholic Church. "On this day, in accordance with ancient tradition, the Church does not celebrate the Eucharist," says [Paschale Solemnitatis](https://www.catholicculture.org/culture/library/view.cfm?id=10848), a 1988 circular letter from the Vatican's Congregation for Divine Worship, which sets the celebration of the Lord's passion "in the afternoon, at about three o'clock." The service it prescribes has the parts Aelfric listed, "the liturgy of the word, the adoration of the cross, and Holy Communion," in an order that, [the letter says](https://www.catholicculture.org/culture/library/view.cfm?id=10848), "stems from an ancient tradition of the Church."

Three o'clock is the Gospel's ninth hour, which the [New International Version](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+15&version=NIV) renders as "three in the afternoon," the time Jesus cried out. Some churches fill the hours before it with the Three Hours' Agony, or Tre Ore, a service running "from noon until 3 o'clock," as [Bishop Robert Barron's Word on Fire](https://www.wofdigital.org/tre-ore-the-three-hours-agony) describes it, with "a series of homilies on the seven last words spoken by Christ."

Orthodox churches normally celebrate no Eucharist that day either: "There is no Divine Liturgy on Good Friday," the Orthodox Church in America says on [its page for Holy Friday](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/worship/the-church-year/holy-friday), "for the same obvious reason that forbids the celebration of the eucharist on the fasting days of lent."

**Canon 1251** of the [Code of Canon Law](https://www.vatican.va/archive/cod-iuris-canonici/eng/documents/cic_lib4-cann1244-1253_en.html) makes it a day of fasting and abstinence for Catholics, ordering "Abstinence from meat, or from some other food as determined by the Episcopal Conference" on Fridays and adding that "Abstinence and fasting are to be observed on Ash Wednesday and Good Friday." Canon 1252 binds abstinence on "those who have completed their fourteenth year" and fasting on adults "until the beginning of their sixtieth year," and [canon 1253](https://www.vatican.va/archive/cod-iuris-canonici/eng/documents/cic_lib4-cann1244-1253_en.html) adds that "the conference of bishops can determine more precisely the observance of fast and abstinence."

Good Friday is a [bank holiday](https://www.gov.uk/bank-holidays) in Britain and a [general holiday](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/L-2/section-166.html) under the Canada Labour Code. The United States leaves it off its list of [federal holidays](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/pay-leave/federal-holidays/), but the [New York Stock Exchange](https://www.nyse.com/markets/hours-calendars) closes for it.

English lent the north its Long Friday, by Hellquist's account, and then gave the name up, so a Swede who says langfredag is keeping alive the name Old English used. Strictly FYI.
