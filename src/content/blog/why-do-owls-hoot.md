The owl that says "twit-twoo" is usually two owls. Shakespeare gave the whole call to one bird. The winter song at the end of [*Love's Labour's Lost*](https://shakespeare.mit.edu/lll/full.html) runs, "When blood is nipp'd and ways be foul, / Then nightly sings the staring owl, Tu-whit; / Tu-who, a merry note, / While greasy Joan doth keel the pot."

The call belongs to the tawny owl, Britain's most widespread owl, and the [British Trust for Ornithology](https://www.bto.org/learn/about-birds/birdfacts/tawny-owl) (BTO) splits it between a female and a male:

> "It is the source of the familiar 'too-whit-too-woo' call which is actually made by two birds - the female is responsible for the 'too-whit' part when she answers to the male's hooting 'too-woo'."

Her part is a sharp "kee-wick." The hooted "twoo," says the BTO's [calling survey page](https://www.bto.org/get-involved/volunteer/projects/completed/tawny-owl-calling-survey), "is usually made by the male and is a territorial call."

## Why do owls hoot?

Owls hoot mainly **to claim a territory and to attract or keep a mate**. A hoot tells other owls that its territory is taken, and mated pairs call back and forth to stay in touch. Hooting peaks when territories and pairs are being settled, which for the tawny owl means autumn and late winter.

- **Territory:** tawny owls use their calls "to show ownership of a breeding territory," the BTO says.
- **Courtship:** the same calls serve in "attracting a mate and reinforcing a pair bond."
- **Warning off newcomers:** settled tawny owl pairs call most in autumn, when young owls go looking for territories of their own.
- **Not every owl hoots:** barn owls screech and hiss, and eastern screech-owls mostly trill and whinny.

## A tawny owl's territory is usually for life

Young tawny owls typically establish a territory by late autumn and are "most unlikely to leave, for the rest of their lives," according to the [Barn Owl Trust](https://www.barnowltrust.org.uk/barn-owl-facts/uk-owl-species/tawny-owl-facts/), which puts a territory at **12 to 20 hectares (30 to 50 acres)**. The trust gives a typical lifespan of about 4 years, but [BTO ringing data](https://www.bto.org/learn/about-birds/birdfacts/tawny-owl) show tawny owls can live for more than 20 years, often in the same territory and even the same nest hole.

Pairs normally mate for life and defend their shared territory "from other owls all year round," says the [Woodland Trust](https://www.woodlandtrust.org.uk/trees-woods-and-wildlife/animals/birds/tawny-owl/). The hoot announces the claim, as [the crow does for a rooster](/blog/why-do-roosters-crow/).

Each male's hoot is also his own. A [2019 case study in Animal Cells and Systems](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6440519/) of five male tawny owls in South Korea found their territorial calls "individually identifiable," well enough to trace individual birds within the population over two years, much as [a hyena's giggle carries its caller's identity](/blog/why-do-hyenas-laugh/).

## Autumn is the tawny owl's loudest season

Tawny owls are "at their most vocal" in autumn, the BTO says, "as pairs on established territories seek to deter dispersing juveniles from settling." The chicks become independent roughly three months after fledging, **from August into November**, and "territory establishment and calling activity peak from October onwards," Mike Toms writes in a [BBC Wildlife Magazine Q&A](https://www.discoverwildlife.com/animal-facts/birds/facts-about-tawny-owls/).

A call that seems to be missing its final part probably comes from one of those youngsters, still looking for a territory, Toms adds. Tawny calls "take time to develop fully," and a juvenile's pitch differs from an adult's. The owls call all year, but particularly in autumn and again in late winter and early spring, ahead of an average UK first egg date of **23 March**.

North America's great horned owl can nest earlier than the tawny. In Missouri, "breeding occurs in late January or early February, following a few months of hooting," says the [Missouri Department of Conservation](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/great-horned-owl) (MDC). The [Owl Research Institute](https://www.owlresearchinstitute.org/great-horned-owl) describes two kinds of male hoot: a series of evenly spaced, low-pitched hoots during breeding, and a soft double hoot to contact other owls or defend territory.

## Females call too, and pairs answer each other

Either sex can make either half of the twit-twoo. "The male and female may also use the 'keewik' contact call, though this is rare for the male during the breeding season," says [Discover Wildlife's tawny owl guide](https://www.discoverwildlife.com/animal-facts/birds/facts-about-tawny-owls/), and "Females may very occasionally hoot, though theirs is less distinct than the males'." The same guide renders a male's territorial hoot on its own as "the gentle 'tu-wit, tu who' followed by a 'huhuhuhooo'," so a single male can produce something like the whole call. The classic twit-twoo exchange is usually, not always, a pair.

The female great horned owl is the bigger bird, at about **1,509 grams (3.3 pounds)** to the male's **1,304 grams (2.9 pounds)**, yet her voice is "higher pitched than males," according to the Owl Research Institute. Among [crickets, only the males chirp](/blog/why-do-crickets-chirp/); among great horned owls, both sexes call.

Barred owl pairs often hoot together. Their classic series is "also described as 'Who cooks for you, who cooks for you all?'" in [MDC's guide](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/barred-owl), and "members of a pair often will call back and forth to each other," says [Audubon's field guide](https://www.audubon.org/field-guide/bird/barred-owl).

## The screech-owl mostly trills, and the barn owl screeches

"Despite its name, the screech-owl's most common call is not a screech," MDC says of the [eastern screech-owl](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/eastern-screech-owl). Its usual calls are an even-pitched trill and a descending whinny, sometimes given "in a duet between mates," and "When screech-owls screech, it is usually only in defense."

The screeching belongs to the American barn owl, whose vocabulary MDC calls "a set of truly unnerving screeches, screams, and shrieks." Britain's [Barn Owl Trust](https://www.barnowltrust.org.uk/barn-owl-facts/) describes "eerie screeching and hissing noises" and says that if you regularly hear an owl hooting, "it is likely to be a Tawny Owl."

| Owl | What you hear |
|---|---|
| Tawny owl | The male's hoot and the female's sharp "kee-wick," sometimes as a duet |
| Great horned owl | Deep, booming hoots, higher pitched from females |
| Barred owl | "Who cooks for you" hoots, with pairs often calling back and forth |
| Eastern screech-owl | An even trill and a descending whinny, with a screech mainly in defense |
| Barn owl | Screeches, screams, shrieks and hisses |

## Owls hoot at night, and less in the rain

The hooting owls are mostly night birds. Tawny owls are "night owls in every sense," the [RSPB](https://www.rspb.org.uk/birds-and-wildlife/tawny-owl) says, "so you're more likely to hear their hoots than see their faces." Great horned owls are nocturnal too, hunting in the dark by ear and eye, and their call is a run of [low, far-carrying hoots](https://www.audubon.org/field-guide/bird/great-horned-owl).

Daytime calls are the exception. Tawny owls give only very occasional calls in broad daylight, while the barred owl, though mostly active at night, "will also call and even hunt in the daytime," says Audubon.

The hour is not the only thing that matters. From a survey of 3,465 volunteers in 2005-06, the BTO learned that "the time of day, the moon cycle, weather and distance to woodland impact the chances of hearing a Tawny Owl."

Rain cuts a hoot's reach. In a [2002 paper in the Proceedings of the Royal Society B](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1691141/), Lengagne and Slater reported "a **69-fold** advantage in area reached by the call" of the tawny owl "in dry versus rainy conditions." They also found "a marked reduction in the calling of tawny owls in rainy conditions."

A hoot in the dark has been read as an omen for a long time, and the reading is folklore. Pliny the Elder's *Natural History* already called the horned owl "especially funereal" and "the monster of the night," with a voice like "a sort of shriek," in [Bostock and Riley's 1855 translation](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.02.0137:book=10:chapter=16). To the owls listening, the message is plainer: this territory is taken.

In Britain, a tawny owl hooting after dark may be holding a territory it will keep for life, and the kee-wick that answers is most likely a female. Shakespeare's staring owl probably had company. Strictly FYI.
