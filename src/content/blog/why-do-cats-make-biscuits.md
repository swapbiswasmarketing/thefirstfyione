A nursing kitten pumps its front paws against its mother's belly **mostly when the milk is coming more slowly than it can drink**, according to [Bonnie Beaver's veterinary textbook *Feline Behavior*](https://archive.org/details/felinebehavior00bonn/page/173/). An adult cat kneading a lap is making the same motion with no milk to hurry.

## Why do cats make biscuits?

Cats make biscuits because kneading begins as a feeding instinct. Nursing kittens push their front paws against the mother's mammary glands to stimulate milk flow, and pet cats can keep the habit as adults, when it usually signals a relaxed, contented cat. The scent-marking and nest-making reasons repeated on pet-care sites remain untested.

## A veterinary textbook calls it the milk tread

In [Beaver's chapter on feeding](https://archive.org/details/felinebehavior00bonn/page/173/), the **milk tread** "consists of rhythmic alternate movements of the forepaws against the mammary gland." It may first help push the mother's skin away from a newborn's nose, "but soon it becomes more important to help stimulate milk flow," [the chapter says](https://archive.org/details/felinebehavior00bonn/page/173/), and kittens usually alternate it with nonrhythmic sucking.

[Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-do-cats-knead-like-theyre-making-biscuits/) put the question to several specialists in 2023. Leticia Fanucchi, an animal behaviour scientist at Oklahoma State University, said kneading is instinctual and never has to be learned.

The nickname fits the motion. The authors of a [2017 case report in *Cureus*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5805320/) define kneading as "the rhythmic motion that cats make with their paws when they repeatedly push them in and out of a soft object," often with the claws going in and out too, which is roughly what a baker's hands do to dough.

## Feral cats tend to stop kneading

Lauren Finka, a cat behaviour expert at Cats Protection, told [BBC Science Focus](https://www.sciencefocus.com/nature/why-do-cats-knead) that free-living cats not socialised to people mostly drop kneading along with other kitten behaviours. Such cats "don't tend to perform these behaviours when they leave the nest," she said.

It is "not unusual for domesticated animals to retain juvenile traits into adulthood," [Scientific American notes](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-do-cats-knead-like-theyre-making-biscuits/). The pattern is called neoteny, and the magazine says it also explains why dogs look and act more like wolf cubs than adult wolves.

The same article notes that a kneading cat is often purring at the same time, and [Beaver's chapter](https://archive.org/details/felinebehavior00bonn/page/173/) records that **[purring](/blog/why-do-cats-purr/) by both mother and kittens** often accompanies nursing. A [2024 review in *Animals*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11240355/) puts the two together with meowing: adult house cats use "neotenized signals, such as meowing, kneading, and purring," to communicate with people.

> Kneading, meowing and purring are "all things that humans unknowingly encourage cats to maintain in their repertoire," Lauren Finka of Cats Protection told [BBC Science Focus](https://www.sciencefocus.com/nature/why-do-cats-knead).

## Scent marking and nest-making are still hypotheses

Beyond nursing, the usual candidates are contentment, scent marking, nest-making by wild ancestors and stretching. "Some of these are just theories because we don't know," Susan Nelson, a veterinarian at Kansas State University, told [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-do-cats-knead-like-theyre-making-biscuits/). "Cats don't get the funding for studies like dogs do."

Finka told [BBC Science Focus](https://www.sciencefocus.com/nature/why-do-cats-knead) that kneading "hasn't been researched in-depth scientifically."

**Scent marking** has real anatomy behind it. Cats have sweat glands in the pads and between the toes, and their secretions have been suggested as scent marks, according to a [2022 review in *Frontiers in Veterinary Science*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9334771/). A [2023 trial in *PLOS One*](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0292188) describes cats leaving secretions from sebaceous glands "located in their interdigital and facial areas" when they scratch and rub their heads.

The evidence for marking comes from those behaviours. Hilary Feldman's [1994 study of semi-feral cats](https://doi.org/10.1139/z94-147) in the *Canadian Journal of Zoology* recorded urine spraying, scratching of tree bark and rubbing against objects, and found no clear evidence that the cats used their faeces and urine to mark territory. **Kneading is not among the methods its abstract reports.**

Fanucchi told Scientific American that kneading activates the paw glands, and Finka told [BBC Science Focus](https://www.sciencefocus.com/nature/why-do-cats-knead) that "it may be that they knead us to deposit their scent." Neither article cites a study that measured it.

**Nest-making** has less. The [Cureus case report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5805320/) lists it among proposed explanations, as a way to prepare "an area for sleep or delivery similar to wild cats patting down tall grass and fallen leaves," in a sentence that gives no reference. It is a popular explanation with no published test behind it.

**Stretching** fares no better. The idea that kneading feels good to a cat the way a stretch does is just as untested. Fanucchi's remark that kneading stretches the muscles describes an effect of the movement rather than a tested motive.

## A lap can stand in for a mother

Kristyn Vitale, a certified applied animal behaviorist then at Unity Environmental University, told [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-do-cats-knead-like-theyre-making-biscuits/): "It's possible that some of these juvenile behaviors we see that used to be directed at the mother are now directed at the owner." She added that kneading on a favourite person "can also be that affiliative, social behavior that can help build a bond," and that it usually means a cat feels happy, friendly and safe.

Finka was part of a team that measured kneading, among other behaviours, in shelter cats meeting strangers. In a [2021 study at Battersea Dogs and Cats Home](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8381768/), the researchers filmed **100 shelter cats** meeting 120 members of the public in five-minute sessions, before and after the volunteers were taught to let the cat choose and to touch it mainly at the base of the ears and around the cheeks. After the training, the cats kneaded or treaded with their front paws for longer.

A 2021 guide to feline emotions in the [Irish Veterinary Journal](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7995744/) reads it the same way, listing "kneading/treading paws" among the actions of a contented cat, alongside stretching, yawning and grooming.

## Kneading has turned up in medical case notes

Claws are why kneading has a dermatology case report. In that [2017 *Cureus* paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5805320/), Philip Cohen, a dermatologist at the University of California, San Diego, and Douglas Ramsay of the University of Washington described a **56-year-old woman** with clusters of red punctures on her upper abdomen. Her cat, Bella, had kneaded her as she lay on the couch, pushing its claws through her clothing and into the skin and pulling them out again.

The authors proposed a name for the injury, **felis punctatis**. The patient noticed the punctures came more readily through a thin shirt than a thick one, and the paper suggests trimming the sharp tip of each claw or fitting nail guards. Beside the milk, nest and scent explanations, its list of proposed reasons for kneading includes a fourth: a female coming into heat signalling to males that she is ready to mate. It tests none of them.

A kneading-like motion can also be a symptom. A [2026 study of paroxysmal dyskinesia](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13448754/), a movement disorder that recurs in episodes, reviewed the records and videos of **25 cats** from several hospitals and recorded kneading digits in **5 of them** during attacks. The authors describe the toes of the front paws flexing and extending while the forelimbs were held stiffly out, and count it among the features that set the feline disorder apart from the canine one.

## The weaning evidence is about other behaviours

Another claim gets attached to cats that knead a lot: that they were taken from their mothers too young. It is usually repeated without a source.

A University of Helsinki team collected questionnaire data on **5,726 cats from 40 breeds** and [reported in 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5583233/) that cats weaned before 8 weeks were more likely to behave aggressively toward strangers than cats weaned at 12 to 13 weeks. The paper mentions wool sucking, which "is thought to arise from the motivation to suckle." It **never mentions kneading**.

| Claim | What supports it | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Nursing holdover | The milk tread in a veterinary textbook; adult pets can keep it | Documented in kittens; best-supported for adults |
| Contentment | Listed under contentment in a 2021 veterinary emotion guide; more kneading in a 2021 handling study | Expert reading, some data |
| Scent marking | Paw glands exist; the marking evidence is from scratching | Untested in kneading |
| Nest-making by wild ancestors | Listed in a 2017 case report with no reference | Untested |
| Stretching | One expert says it stretches the muscles; the motive is unsourced | Untested as a reason |
| Weaned too early | A weaning study that did not measure it | Unsupported |

The name comes from bread, but the movement was built for milk. The scent and nest stories rest on less: a set of paw glands and a guess about grass. Strictly FYI.
