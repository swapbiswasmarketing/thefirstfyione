When archaeologists opened ancient Egyptian tombs, the story goes, they found pots of honey thousands of years old that were still good to eat. A [2013 Smithsonian article](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/the-science-behind-honeys-eternal-shelf-life-1218690/) describes "pots of honey, thousands of years old, and yet still preserved." It names no tomb, no excavator and no one who tasted the stuff.

The chemistry behind the story is real, and it explains why a jar in your cupboard keeps for years. The tomb part is shakier, and one documented find ended in a disappointment.

## Why does honey almost never spoil?

Honey resists spoilage because it holds too little available water for most bacteria, yeasts and molds to grow, it is acidic, and bees add an enzyme that makes hydrogen peroxide when honey is diluted, the [main factors a 2019 review lists](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6963415/) alongside other antimicrobial compounds. Kept sealed, so it cannot [soak up moisture from the air](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/the-science-behind-honeys-eternal-shelf-life-1218690/), honey does not go bad in the sense of growing microbes, though in long-term storage, especially above 20 degrees C, it [darkens and its sugars slowly break down](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12371870/).

In a test described in the US Department of Agriculture's [1980 beekeeping handbook](https://archive.org/details/Beekeeping.in.the.United.StatesD7), nine common disease-causing bacteria were added to honey, and "All the bacteria died within a few hours or days."

## A tomb find of 1905 turned out to be natron

In February 1905, Theodore M. Davis and his workmen found the Egyptian tomb of Yuya and Tjuyu (Iouiya and Touiyou in the [1907 book on the tomb](https://archive.org/details/tombofiouiyatou00davi)), the parents of Queen Tiyi. Near a chariot in the burial chamber stood two alabaster vases, and shreds of mummy cloth hung from the neck of one, where a robber had torn off the cover; in [his own account](https://archive.org/details/tombofiouiyatou00davi), Davis wrote that the vase "contained only a liquid which was first thought to be honey, but which subsequently proved to be natron."

> "Three thousand years thereafter I looked into the vase with like expectation; both of us were disappointed." - Theodore M. Davis, 1907

Natron is a salt mixture the Egyptians used in mummification because it [absorbs water and behaves as a drying agent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natron).

Honey that really was left in a jar for millennia has been found, and it took modern chemistry to recognize it. In 1954 an underground shrine at Paestum, in southern Italy, yielded bronze jars holding "an orange-brown sticky substance," and over the next [30 years three teams](https://chem.web.ox.ac.uk/node/4580731) concluded it was fat "contaminated with pollen and insect parts." Oxford chemists reported in [2025](https://chem.web.ox.ac.uk/node/4580731) that it held sugars, organic acids and royal jelly proteins, the first biomolecular evidence that the residue once contained honey, probably as honeycomb, and suggested that copper from the bronze may have protected the sugar markers "from microbial decay."

## Spoilage needs water, and honey keeps very little

When the USDA analysed [490 samples of American honey](https://archive.org/details/usda-composition-american-honeys-1962) from the 1956 and 1957 crops, the average moisture was **17.2 percent**. Nearly all the rest is sugar, mostly the [two simple sugars the survey calls levulose and dextrose](https://archive.org/details/usda-composition-american-honeys-1962), better known as fructose and glucose.

That much sugar makes most of the remaining water unavailable to microbes. Food scientists measure the usable share as water activity, and honey's typically runs from **0.5 to 0.65**, according to a [2022 review in Frontiers in Nutrition](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9359632/), below the levels most bacteria (0.90), yeasts (0.80) and molds (0.70) need to grow.

A microbe that lands in honey also loses water to it. The sugar creates [high osmotic pressure](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9359632/), drawing water out of microorganisms and stopping their growth.

Bees get honey that dry partly by flapping their wings to dry out the nectar, as the [Smithsonian piece](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/the-science-behind-honeys-eternal-shelf-life-1218690/) describes, and some foragers [start evaporating water from their mouthparts while still on the flower](/blog/how-do-bees-make-honey/).

## Honey is acidic, and its peroxide waits for water

The 1962 survey put the [average pH of honey at 3.91](https://archive.org/details/usda-composition-american-honeys-1962), and a [2013 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3758027/) gives the range as 3.2 to 4.5, acidity "low enough to inhibit the growth of most microorganisms."

Bees add an enzyme called **glucose oxidase** to nectar from glands in their heads, and [in diluted honey](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9359632/) it turns glucose into gluconic acid, the main acid in honey, and **hydrogen peroxide**.

That last detail matters. Honey's antibacterial activity was [linked to an unidentified "inhibine" in 1937](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6963415/), and its identity stayed unknown until [Jonathan W. White Jr. and two colleagues showed in 1963](https://doi.org/10.1016/0006-3002(63)90359-7) that it was hydrogen peroxide made by honey's glucose oxidase. The peroxide's [antimicrobial effect increases upon dilution](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6963415/), as the enzyme binds glucose more readily, so in thick, ripe honey the scarce water and the acidity do most of the guarding.

## Wet honey ferments

Honey pulls moisture from the air: normal honey with 18.8 percent water or less will absorb it from [air above 60% relative humidity](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3758027/).

The yeasts that spoil honey are sugar-tolerant species that occur in it naturally, so the [USDA handbook](https://archive.org/details/Beekeeping.in.the.United.StatesD7) judges the risk mainly by water content and yeast count: honey with less than 17.1 percent water "will not ferment in a year, irrespective of the yeast count," while honey above 19 percent can be expected to ferment with even one spore per gram.

| Water content | Safe for a year with |
|---|---|
| Below 17.1% | any yeast count |
| 17.1 to 18% | 1,000 spores per gram or fewer |
| 18.1 to 19% | 10 spores per gram or fewer |
| Above 19% | expect fermentation |

Fermenting honey [foams, may smell of sweet wine](https://archive.org/details/Beekeeping.in.the.United.StatesD7) and can taste sour, because the alcohol the yeasts make can break down into acetic acid.

## Crystallized honey has not spoiled

Honey that turns grainy and pale has crystallized: the glucose forms monohydrate crystals, and [the honey turns lighter in color because the glucose crystals are white](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3758027/).

Crystallized honey is still honey, and [careful heating can return it to liquid](https://archive.org/details/Beekeeping.in.the.United.StatesD7), but the liquid left between the crystals holds more water, which favors fermentation. A crystallized jar is worth keeping tightly closed.

## Honey is not for babies under 1

"Occasionally, honey contains bacteria that can produce toxins in a baby's intestines, leading to infant botulism," the [NHS says](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/weaning-and-feeding/foods-to-avoid-giving-babies-and-young-children/), and its advice is to wait until a child is over 1 year old. The [CDC gives the same age limit](https://www.cdc.gov/botulism/prevention/index.html) and adds that infant botulism can happen when the spores get into an infant's intestines, where they grow and produce the toxin. Anyone with a question about a particular child should ask a doctor.

## Bees store honey to outlast lean seasons

In the hive, bees cap ripe honey under a thin layer of wax for long-term storage, [to carry the colony through nectar shortages](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9359632/) when foraging is impossible. The jar in the cupboard keeps for the same reason a cell in the comb does: the bees left it too little free water for microbes to use. Strictly FYI.
