It takes dead oxen to collect cinnamon, or so [Herodotus](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2707/pg2707-images.html) was told in the [fifth century BCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Histories_(Herodotus)). Large birds carry "those dried sticks which we have learnt from the Phenicians to call cinnamon" to nests of clay on cliffs "which man can find no means of scaling," so the Arabians cut up oxen, asses and other pack animals that have died into "pieces as large as convenient" and leave them near the nests. The birds carry the meat up, the nests give way under the weight, and the men gather the cinnamon from the ground.

The passage, in [book three of his *Histories*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2707/pg2707-images.html), picks up the story with the sticks already dried. The Arabians "are not able to tell" where cinnamon grows, Herodotus concedes, except for "a probable account" placing it where Dionysos was brought up.

## How is cinnamon made?

Cinnamon is made by **peeling bark off Cinnamomum trees**, usually just the [inner layer](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2025-12-23-bark-baking-cinnamons-journey-your-table), and drying it until it **curls into quills**. Workers cut shoots from coppiced bushes or strip bark from older cassia trunks. Whole quills become cinnamon sticks, and broken pieces are ground into powder. Almost all of it comes from **China, Vietnam, Indonesia and Sri Lanka**.

## Pliny called the birds a pricing trick

Pliny the Elder, the Roman encyclopedist, took up Herodotus's stories in his [Natural History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_History_(Pliny)), published about five centuries later. Herodotus had a [cassia legend](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2707/pg2707-images.html) too: gatherers wrapped in cowhide cut the spice from a shallow pool guarded by "winged beasts nearly resembling bats."

In [book 12](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.02.0137:book=12:chapter=42), Pliny named Herodotus and dismissed both stories: **"All these tales, however, have been evidently invented for the purpose of enhancing the prices of these commodities."** He then got the answer wrong himself: cinnamon, he wrote, grows "in the country of the Aethiopians." Later in the same chapter he put cinnamon's former price at "as much as **a thousand denarii per pound**."

Two books earlier, in [his volume on birds](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.02.0137:book=10:chapter=50), he had told a nest story straight: the Arabian cinnamolgus "builds its nest with sprigs of cinnamon; and the natives knock them down with arrows loaded with lead, in order to sell them." Arrows loaded with lead are one of the versions he later [files among the inventions](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.02.0137:book=12:chapter=42).

> In the [same chapter](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.02.0137:book=12:chapter=42) that calls the bird stories a price-raising invention, Pliny says the Aethiopians must ask a god's permission to gather cinnamon, offering "the entrails of forty-four oxen, goats, and rams."

## Phoenician flasks point to cinnamon 3,000 years ago

Both men were late to the trade. A 2015 review in [Radiocarbon](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/radiocarbon/article/abs/on-the-beginnings-of-south-asian-spice-trade-with-the-mediterranean-region-a-review/57B7230DFD38F6B035420F1D11A93210) by Ayelet Gilboa and Dvory Namdar sums up residue analyses of small Phoenician clay flasks from the **11th to late 10th century BCE**. Several, the authors concluded, had held cinnamon, "which in this period could only have originated in South/Southeast Asia."

The Greek word itself, *kinnamomon*, corresponds to the Hebrew *qinnamon*, according to the [Liddell-Scott Greek lexicon](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.04.0057:entry=kinna/mwmon).

## Sri Lankan peelers loosen the bark with a brass rod

Until Dutch experimental plantings in Colombo between 1765 and 1785, Sri Lankan cinnamon was harvested from the wild, says a [history](https://cinnamon.gov.lk/about-cinnamon/history/) published by the country's Department of Cinnamon Development. Cultivated trees are usually coppiced, cut back to the ground to force new shoots, so trees that can grow 30 to 50 feet tall stay shrubs of about 6 to 8 feet, according to the [University of Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2025-12-23-bark-baking-cinnamons-journey-your-table). [PROSEA](https://plantuse.plantnet.org/en/Cinnamomum_%28PROSEA_Spices%29), a Southeast Asian plant reference, reported two exceptions: Indonesian cassia was still harvested mainly by felling whole trees, and Saigon cassia was usually harvested at 10 to 12 years old, its bark stripped from standing trees, sometimes from bamboo scaffolding.

For Ceylon cinnamon, the department's [post-harvest guide](https://cinnamon.gov.lk/about-cinnamon/post-harvest-technology/) puts the first harvest **two to three years after planting**. The guide sets the optimal time to cut for when the rainy season's new leaves have turned green and the bark "peels more easily," and a well-managed plantation can be cut **two to three times a year**.

The brown outer bark is scraped off first. Each stem is then **rubbed with a [brass rod](https://cinnamon.gov.lk/about-cinnamon/post-harvest-technology/)** "until the juice oozes out and loosens the bark from the wood," and a small knife lifts the inner bark away in sheaths that curl as they dry in the shade.

Peelers stack the sheets and fill them with small pieces to make quills **42 or 21 inches** long, then [dry the quills](https://cinnamon.gov.lk/about-cinnamon/post-harvest-technology/) for 4-7 days, until moisture falls to 14% or below. The thinnest of about 13 grades, Alba, is 6 millimetres across, and 45 of those 42-inch quills weigh a kilogram.

## The four major species are all native to Asia

Cinnamon comes from four major species of Cinnamomum. **Ceylon cinnamon** is native to Sri Lanka, southern India and southern Myanmar, and the three **cassia** species to a band running from southern China and Vietnam to Indonesia.

| Sold as | Species | Native to | Named in US regulations as |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ceylon cinnamon | [*C. verum*](https://npgsweb.ars-grin.gov/gringlobal/taxon/taxonomydetail?id=70183) | Sri Lanka, southern India, southern Myanmar | Cinnamon, Ceylon |
| Chinese cassia | [*C. cassia*](https://npgsweb.ars-grin.gov/gringlobal/taxon/taxonomydetail?id=70182) | Southern China | Cassia, Chinese and Cinnamon, Chinese |
| Saigon cinnamon | [*C. loureiroi*](https://npgsweb.ars-grin.gov/gringlobal/taxon/taxonomydetail?id=10583) | Vietnam | Cassia, Saigon and Cinnamon, Saigon |
| Indonesian cassia | [*C. burmanni*](https://npgsweb.ars-grin.gov/gringlobal/taxon/taxonomydetail?id=10577) | Southern China to Indonesia | Cassia, Padang or Batavia |

A 2014 study cited by the USDA's [GRIN database](https://npgsweb.ars-grin.gov/gringlobal/taxon/taxonomydetail?id=70183) calls these "the four major species of cinnamons." Ranges are from GRIN, which files Chinese cassia as *C. aromaticum*; names are from [21 CFR 182.10](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/182.10), which uses the older names *C. zeylanicum* and *C. loureirii*.

[FAO's figures](https://www.fao.org/faostat/en/#data/QCL) for 2024 put **China** first, with about **94,200** of the world's 244,800 tonnes, though the China and Vietnam numbers are FAO estimates rather than official reports. China, Vietnam, Indonesia and Sri Lanka together grew **more than 98 percent** of the total.

## The fake-cinnamon claim gets cassia wrong

The modern myth says cassia is fake cinnamon and only Ceylon is real. Calling Ceylon "true" cinnamon is a naming habit, common in much of the world according to [Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2025-12-23-bark-baking-cinnamons-journey-your-table), but cassia is the bark of a Cinnamomum tree too. The [federal list](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/182.10) of spices generally recognized as safe names *Cinnamomum cassia* twice, **as "Cassia, Chinese" and as "Cinnamon, Chinese."**

[Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2025-12-23-bark-baking-cinnamons-journey-your-table) does describe a real difference in the jar: Ceylon quills "composed of several thin layers" against cassia's "thick, hard quills" and "a bold, intense flavor." If the Ceylon version sounds unfamiliar, it adds, that is probably because most cinnamon sold in the United States is cassia.

## Ground cinnamon is bark, not sawdust

The claim that cinnamon is basically sawdust mixes up the bark and the wood. Sri Lanka's department [defines ground cinnamon](https://cinnamon.gov.lk/about-cinnamon/post-harvest-technology/) as **dried, ground inner bark**, and it warns that stems left too long between harvests can end up with "a higher wood percentage" but not necessarily much more bark, which can make top grades such as Alba harder to produce.

[Pliny](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.02.0137:book=12:chapter=42) drew the same line: "the upper part of the tree is preferred," he wrote, "there being the greatest proportion of bark there," while the wood, xylocinnamum, "is held in no esteem at all."

Bark peeled off a bush would have had a going rate. Sticks that had to be tricked out of a nest on a cliff face with a dead ox could cost whatever the seller said. Strictly FYI.
