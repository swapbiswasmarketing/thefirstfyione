[Only two countries produced more rapeseed oil than Germany in 2023](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapeseed_oil): China, and Canada, where a new rapeseed was bred and renamed canola. Germany's output of **4.2 million tonnes** ran [close behind Canada's 4.4 million](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rapeseed_oil), in the UN's FAOSTAT figures.

Across the European Union, farmers harvested [**16.6 million tonnes** of rape and turnip rape seed in 2024](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Agricultural_production_-_crops), about twice their sunflower crop. The oil sits on European shelves under its older name, and [BBC Food](https://www.bbc.co.uk/food/rapeseed_oil) gives British cooks the translation: "In Canada and the US, rapeseed oil is known as canola."

## Can you buy canola oil in Europe?

Canola oil is not banned in Europe, which grows, presses and sells the same kind of oil as **rapeseed oil**. Canola is rapeseed bred low in both erucic acid and glucosinolates, a change [Canadian plant breeders made in the decades after the Second World War](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/canadian-canola-fields-145517). EU law [caps erucic acid in vegetable oils](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32023R0915) at **20 grams per kilogram**, or 2 percent, the [Codex level for low-erucic rapeseed oil](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32019R1870).

What the EU regulates is erucic acid, one of the oil's fatty acids, and [the rule covers nearly every vegetable oil](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32019R1870), not rapeseed alone. Camelina, mustard and borage oils get a looser limit of [50.0 grams per kilogram](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32023R0915), partly because none of those plants has varieties low enough in erucic acid to meet the stricter one and partly because those oils are "of less significance for the human exposure than other vegetable oils," as the [European Commission explained in 2019](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32019R1870).

## Canola is a Canadian name for double-low rapeseed

Rapeseed became a Canadian crop during the Second World War, when its oil "was urgently needed as a lubricant for the rapidly increasing number of marine engines in naval and merchant ships," according to the [Canola Council of Canada](https://www.canolacouncil.org/canola-encyclopedia/history-of-canola-seed-development/). As a food, it drew doubts early: as early as 1956, [the council says](https://www.canolacouncil.org/canola-encyclopedia/history-of-canola-seed-development/), "the nutritional aspects of rapeseed oil were questioned, especially the high eicosenoic and erucic fatty acid contents."

Breeders answered with Oro, developed at Saskatoon in 1968 as "the first low erucic acid" selection of *Brassica napus*, and in 1974 Baldur Stefansson of the University of Manitoba developed **Tower**, "the first low erucic acid, low glucosinolate cultivar," in [the same history](https://www.canolacouncil.org/canola-encyclopedia/history-of-canola-seed-development/). The new quality in the seed, oil and meal "needed a name to distinguish the commodity from common rapeseed," [it goes on](https://www.canolacouncil.org/canola-encyclopedia/history-of-canola-seed-development/), and the name adopted was canola, derived from Canadian oil.

The council's [About Canola page](https://www.canolacouncil.org/about-canola/) calls the word a contraction of Canada, "where canola was developed," and ola, referring to oil. To earn the name, seed must yield oil with "less than 2% erucic acid in its fatty acid profile" and meal with under 30 micromoles of certain glucosinolates per gram, [under that page's standard](https://www.canolacouncil.org/about-canola/).

European rapeseed often passes that test. Winter rapeseed, "widely grown in parts of Europe and Asia," keeps the old name, but many of its cultivars have erucic acid and glucosinolate levels like Canada's and "fit the canola definition," [the council adds](https://www.canolacouncil.org/canola-encyclopedia/history-of-canola-seed-development/).

The Western Canadian Oilseed Crushers trademarked these "double low" rapeseeds as canola in 1978, according to [NASA's Earth Observatory](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/canadian-canola-fields-145517).

American law still files canola under the older name. The Food and Drug Administration's rule for the oil begins with rapeseed:

> "Low erucic acid rapeseed oil, also known as canola oil, is the fully refined, bleached, and deodorized edible oil obtained from certain varieties of Brassica Napus or B. Campestris of the family Cruciferae." - US Code of Federal Regulations, [21 CFR 184.1555](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/184.1555)

## The toxic-oil rumor misreads a limit on one fatty acid

One version of the ban story has the European Union classifying canola oil as toxic and carcinogenic, and [Green Matters, looking into it in 2023](https://www.greenmatters.com/food/why-is-canola-oil-banned-in-europe), called both claims false. EU law does something narrower. Its contaminants regulation lists erucic acid [under the heading plant toxins](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32023R0915) and caps it in vegetable oils, rapeseed oil among them.

The cap dates from [1976, when maximum limits were set for erucic acid](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/161109) in vegetable oils and fats, the European Food Safety Authority notes. The [directive of the Council of the European Communities](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/31976L0621) cited undesirable effects of large doses of colza oil in experimental animals, added that "it has not been demonstrated that such effects can occur in man," and restricted erucic acid as a precaution: no more than **10 percent** from July 1977, and from July 1979 no more than **5 percent** of the fatty acids in the fat.

Natural rapeseed and mustard carry [over 40% of their fatty acids as erucic acid](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/161109), but in rapeseed grown for food the level is typically [below 0.5%](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/161109), according to EFSA. Its 2016 review set a tolerable daily intake of 7 milligrams per kilogram of body weight per day and [found erucic acid](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/press/news/161109) "not a safety concern for most consumers," though it "may be a long-term health risk for children up to 10 years of age who consume high amounts of foods containing this substance." Citing that opinion, the Commission in 2019 [lowered the ceiling](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32019R1870) for most vegetable oils to the figure in force today, which it called "the level established by Codex Alimentarius for low-erucic acid rapeseed oil."

| Rule | Applies to | Erucic acid ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| Canola definition, [Canola Council of Canada](https://www.canolacouncil.org/about-canola/) | Seed sold as canola | Less than 2% of the fatty acids |
| US rule, [21 CFR 184.1555](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/184.1555) | Low erucic acid rapeseed oil (canola oil) | No more than 2% of the fatty acids |
| EEC directive of 1976, [76/621/EEC](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/31976L0621) | Oils and fats for human consumption, from 1979 | 5% of the fatty acids |
| EU regulation in force, [2023/915](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32023R0915) | Vegetable oils and fats except camelina, mustard and borage oil | 20.0 grams per kilogram (2%) |

## Hexane and genetically modified rapeseed imports are regulated in the EU, not banned

In the method most Canadian crushers use, the seed is heated, flaked and pressed to remove most of the oil, and the leftover cake is then soaked in hexane, a solvent that [the Canola Council of Canada says](https://www.canolacouncil.org/about-canola/processing/) "carries nearly all of the remaining oil away" and that is recovered and reused; only a "small proportion of Canadian canola is processed without solvent extraction." EU law allows the same step: the [extraction solvents directive](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/02009L0032-20230216) permits hexane for the "production or fractionation of fats and oils" and caps what remains at **1 milligram per kilogram** of fat or oil.

Almost all the canola grown in Canada is herbicide-tolerant, and two of its three herbicide-tolerant types are genetically modified, according to [The Canadian Encyclopedia](https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/canola), which lists the European Union among the main importers of Canadian canola.

The EU authorizes some genetically modified oilseed rape for food and feed: in December 2025 the European Commission [renewed its authorization](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32025D2542) of MON 88302, a glyphosate-tolerant oilseed rape, for every use "with the exception of cultivation." Food made from a genetically modified crop has to carry words such as "produced from genetically modified" on the label, under the [EU's regulation on genetically modified food](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32003R1829).

## Rapeseed is named for the turnip

The rape in rapeseed comes from the Latin [rapum, meaning turnip](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/rapeseed), and [its relatives include the turnip, rutabaga, cabbage, cauliflower and mustard](https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/canola). [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/rapeseed) traces the English word to Middle English *rape sede* and calls the verb "etymologically unrelated." The [1976 directive](https://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/31976L0621) also calls the oil colza oil, and [colza](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/colza) came into English from French, which took it from the Dutch koolzaad, cabbage seed.

Canada bred its rapeseed low in erucic acid and glucosinolates and renamed it canola. Europe kept the old name, grew the crop by the million tonnes and, in 2019, wrote the canola-grade limit on erucic acid into its own law. Strictly FYI.
