A mood ring on your finger rarely holds one colour for long. It sits at blue for a while, then after a cold drink or a winter walk it slides through green and amber toward black.

The usual chart reads those colours as moods, with blue for relaxed and black for stressed. The physics reads them as temperatures, and they run backwards from the way colours feel: blue, the colour we call cool, sits toward the warm end of the stone's range, and black is a crystal too cold to show any colour.

## How do mood rings work?

A mood ring is **a thermometer for the skin of your finger**. Its stone holds a thermochromic liquid crystal whose molecules stack in a helix that tightens as it warms, moving the reflected colour from **red through green to blue**. Anything that cools that skin, stress included, sends the colour back toward black.

## The helix tightens as the finger warms

A liquid crystal flows like a liquid but keeps some of a crystal's order. In the type used for mood rings, called cholesteric, rod-shaped molecules lie in thin layers, each turned slightly from the one below, so their direction traces a helix, the shape of a corkscrew, through the stack.

White light entering the helix gets sorted. The length of one full turn, called the pitch, sets which band of wavelengths is reflected, and the rest passes through.

Warming widens the angle between neighbouring layers, which shortens the pitch and moves the reflection toward blue. The [Handbook of Thermochromic Liquid Crystal Technology](https://spotsee.io/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/RT006-Rev01-USA-Thermochromic-Handbook.pdf), a technical guide from the manufacturer Hallcrest, finds the pitch shrinking with heat in "almost all pure cholesteric and chiral nematic compounds studied to date".

> Change the angle between layers by "about one tenth of one degree", the Hallcrest handbook says, and the reflected colour moves "from one end of the visible spectrum to the other".

## Black means the crystal has stopped reflecting colour

In the handbook's account, a thermochromic liquid crystal shows colour only inside a set band of temperatures: colourless below it, then red, orange, yellow, green, blue and violet as it warms, then colourless again above it. These crystals are meant to be viewed against black, which shows their colours best, so colourless reads as black.

A [patent filed in 1976 (granted 1980)](https://patents.google.com/patent/US4220016A/en) for stick-on "skin jewelry" with its own mood chart describes a colour-changing stone that is black when "the jewel is the coldest", below about **80 F (27 C)**, and dark blue above about **90 F (32 C)**.

A crystal can also fail outright. A [University of Rochester lesson plan on liquid crystals](https://spie.org/Documents/Students/LC-Mood-patch-Lesson-Plan.pdf) warns that moisture that gets into the crystal layer "will destroy its properties".

## Stress does cool the finger a ring sits on

The mood half of the idea has something real under it. In a [2013 study in the journal Stress](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23790072/), Vinkers and colleagues gave volunteers the Trier Social Stress Test, a speech and mental arithmetic in front of an audience. Against a calm control task, their skin held steady below the collarbone but cooled at the fingertip and at the **base of the finger**, the exact spot a ring sits on.

The route is blood flow. A [1998 study of fingertip temperature](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9641246/) found that stimuli which trigger the sympathetic nervous system constrict the small vessels in fingertip skin almost at once, and about **15 seconds** later the fingertip temperature dips. Blood vessels that narrow fast are also thought to play a part in [brain freeze](/blog/the-science-of-brain-freeze/).

A cold drink in the hand cools the same skin, and the ring cannot tell the two causes apart. Kelly Boyer Sagert, who researched the fad for her book *The 1970s*, told [HISTORY](https://www.history.com/articles/mood-ring-colors-1970s) the old rings were easy to game: "You just had to rub your hands to get warm and be happy." The 1976 filing even proposed moving the stone off the hand, because fingers "tend to change temperature more than other body parts".

## There was never one colour chart

The charts are marketing, and they disagree. Green means "mixed emotions" in the 1970s chart HISTORY describes, "normal or average" on a [chemistry lab handout](http://www.chymist.com/Calibration%20of%20a%20mood%20ring.pdf) and "active" on [one mood-ring seller's page](https://bestmoodrings.com/pages/how-mood-rings-work). They agree on two readings, black for stressed and blue for relaxed, and on the order the physics sets: black at the cold end, blues and violets toward the warm end.

The temperatures behind each colour vary as well.

| Material | Colour and temperature |
|---|---|
| Westinghouse thermal-imaging film, patent filed 1960 | Red at about 21 C (70 F), green at about 23 C (73 F) |
| "Skin jewelry" stone, patent filed 1976 | Black below about 80 F (27 C), dark blue above about 90 F (32 C) |
| Rochester classroom "mood patch" blend | Red to blue between 89 F and 94 F (32 C to 34 C) |
| Hallcrest mixture R35C1W | Red at 35 C (95 F), blue at 36 C (97 F) |

Green can mean about 23 C on one material and between 35 C and 36 C on another, so a colour stands for a temperature only once that mixture has been checked against a thermometer. The [chirp-rate formula for the snowy tree cricket](/blog/why-do-crickets-chirp/) comes with the same fine print: calibrate your local crickets against one first.

## The name comes from cholesterol

This family of liquid crystal is still called cholesteric because, the Hallcrest handbook explains, the first chemicals to show it "were esters of cholesterol". In **1888** the Austrian botanist Friedrich Reinitzer found that one of them, cholesteryl benzoate, seemed to melt twice, into a cloudy liquid at about 145 C and a clear one at about 179 C. He is usually credited with discovering the liquid crystal state. Names taken from a first example tend to stick, and [American football](/blog/why-is-american-football-called-football/) still carries the name of the kicking game it started as.

For decades there was little commercial interest. Then in 1960 Westinghouse filed James Fergason's first [patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/US3114836A/en), for a thermal-imaging film, and by the early 1970s cholesteric crystals were selling in volume for temperature indicators. A [review of Fergason's work](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1310.7569) counts "even a 1970s pop culture icon, the 'mood ring'" among the later uses of that patent.

## Two claimants, neither patented it

HISTORY credits Josh Reynolds and Maris Ambats with bringing the mood ring into American stores in 1975, as a **$45** silver-plated ring introduced at Bonwit Teller in New York. In the same account, jewellery designer Marvin Wernick says he got there first, after watching a doctor use temperature-sensing tape on a patient.

The credit is still disputed, and neither Reynolds nor Wernick applied for a patent. The idea itself was already on file. A [patent granted to Bill G. James in 1974](https://patents.google.com/patent/US3802945A/en) for a "heat sensitive novelty device" describes colour-changing liquid crystal sealed inside a transparent body, and lists "the setting for a ring" among its uses.

Within months of the launch, HISTORY says, the market "was flooded with poor quality knockoffs", and in 1977 Reynolds filed for bankruptcy.

The stone can show a mood only after the mood has reached the skin, and stress gets there by cooling the fingers. Strictly FYI.
