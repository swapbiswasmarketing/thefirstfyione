In the middle of July, the [Medici court physician](https://archive.org/details/experimentsonge00bigegoog/page/n14/mode/2up) Francesco Redi sealed a snake, some fish, eels from the Arno and a slice of veal in four large flasks, and left four more open. Flies went in and out of the open ones, which soon turned wormy, while in the [sealed ones](https://archive.org/details/experimentsonge00bigegoog/page/n41/mode/2up) he "did not see a worm."

Sealing also shut out air, so he tried again with meat and fish in a large vase "closed only with a fine Naples veil," [set inside a frame](https://archive.org/details/experimentsonge00bigegoog/page/n44/mode/2up) covered with the same net. He [reported](https://archive.org/details/experimentsonge00bigegoog/page/n44/mode/2up) that he "never saw any worms in the meat," though flies [landed on the outer net](https://archive.org/details/experimentsonge00bigegoog/page/n45/mode/2up) now and then, some leaving six or seven worms at a time.

Redi [published these tests](https://archive.org/details/experimentsonge00bigegoog/page/n18/mode/2up) in **1668** in *Experiments on the Generation of Insects*. They are quoted here from [Mab Bigelow's 1909 translation](https://archive.org/details/experimentsonge00bigegoog) of the 1688 edition. He was testing "the dictum of ancients and moderns, and the popular belief," that [rotting flesh breeds worms](https://archive.org/details/experimentsonge00bigegoog/page/n35/mode/2up).

## Where do maggots come from?

Maggots are fly larvae. A female fly lays her eggs on moist, decaying matter such as carrion, food waste or animal droppings, and maggots hatch from them; blow fly eggs hatch in about **24 hours**, according to [NC State Extension](https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/blow-fly-biology-management). Many flesh flies skip egg-laying and deposit live maggots instead. Rotting food never turns into maggots on its own.

House flies lay batches of about **100 to 150** eggs in "warm, moist, organic materials such as manure, garbage, lawn clippings, decaying vegetables and fruits," according to [UC IPM](https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/flies/), the University of California's pest program.

## Blow flies can arrive within 10 minutes

The first to arrive at a body are usually blow flies, and in some cases they have "been known to turn up within just **10 minutes**," London's [Natural History Museum](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/murder-maggots-forensic-entomology.html) reports. A female secondary screwworm, one of the commonest blow flies on remains in the southern United States, lays a loose mass of about **50 to 200** eggs, and several females laying together can leave masses of thousands on one carcass, a University of Florida [profile](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/IN149) reports.

Blow fly eggs "look like small grains of rice," says NC State Extension's [blow fly factsheet](https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/blow-fly-biology-management), and are typically concentrated on open wounds or in crevices. In another of Redi's trials, six [open boxes](https://archive.org/details/experimentsonge00bigegoog/page/n38/mode/2up) of meat, from two snakes to a sheep's heart, "all became wormy in little more than twenty-four hours."

## Some flies deposit live maggots

Some maggots arrive already hatched. Many flesh flies "deposit maggots directly on a corpse instead of eggs," a [2018 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6197121/) of forensically important flesh flies explains, and a female can set down **20 to 40** at one sitting, according to the University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee's [field station](https://uwm.edu/field-station/bug-of-the-week/flesh-fly/).

> "It is true that some kinds of flies bring forth live worms and some others eggs, as I have proved by experiment," Redi [wrote](https://archive.org/details/experimentsonge00bigegoog/page/n45/mode/2up).

## Maggots turn into flies inside their own skin

A maggot feeds and molts through three larval stages, then converts its last larval skin into a puparium, "a hardened shell within which the pupa develops," [UC IPM](https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/flies/) explains, and the adult "pops off the end of the puparium and emerges." Warmth speeds development: under ideal temperatures, blow flies can go from egg to adult in as little as **7 days**, [UC IPM says](https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/flies/).

| Stage | What happens | Blow fly timing |
|---|---|---|
| Egg | Laid in batches on carrion, typically on wounds or in crevices | Hatches in about 24 hours |
| Maggot | Feeds and molts through three larval stages | About 1 week |
| Pupa | Develops inside the puparium, the hardened last larval skin | **About 8 days** |
| Adult fly | Emerges, then mates and lays hundreds of eggs on carrion | Roughly 16 days after laying |

The timings are [NC State's](https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/blow-fly-biology-management), totaled in the bottom row. The [museum](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/murder-maggots-forensic-entomology.html) gives the blow fly *Calliphora vicina* about **19 days** from egg to adult, and says half of that time can be spent in the puparium.

## A closed lid is not always a barrier

Maggots in a closed container still came from a fly, though the fly may never have got in. Fruit flies, a [University of Kentucky factsheet](https://entomology.mgcafe.uky.edu/ef621) warns, lay eggs under the lid of a container that is not well sealed, "and the tiny larvae will enter the container upon hatching."

Redi noticed the occasional maggot on the [paper covers](https://archive.org/details/experimentsonge00bigegoog/page/n41/mode/2up) of his sealed flasks seeking "some crevice by which to enter."

Blow fly maggots also travel the other way, leaving their food to find a drier place to pupate. That is why, [UC IPM notes](https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/flies/), many homeowners have seen "a mass emergence of maggots crawling from their backyard trash cans" when kitchen waste went in without a sealed plastic bag.

The reverse surprise, flies with no maggots ever seen, has a hidden source too. Maggots "may go unnoticed until after they have pupated and emerged as adult flies," [NC State notes](https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/blow-fly-biology-management), and a sudden swarm of blow flies indoors often traces back to a dead animal in a wall void, crawlspace or attic.

## Spontaneous generation, from Aristotle to Kircher

The belief Redi was testing is at least as old as [Aristotle (384-322 BC)](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle/), whose [*History of Animals*](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/history_anim.5.v.html) says some insects "are not derived from living parentage, but are generated spontaneously," in dew, mud, dung, timber, hair and "the flesh of animals."

Aristotle knew that flies mate and that their mating produces grubs, but [he wrote](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/history_anim.5.v.html) that "from these issues the parent-species is never reproduced." In his account, the grubs that became new flies arose in the dung on their own: flies, in [D'Arcy Thompson's translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/history_anim.5.v.html), "grow from grubs in the dung that farmers have gathered up into heaps."

In Redi's century, Father Athanasius Kircher, whom Redi called "a man worthy of esteem," described [breeding flies](https://archive.org/details/experimentsonge00bigegoog/page/n42/mode/2up) from dead flies soaked in honey-water. The honey-water, Redi [replied](https://archive.org/details/experimentsonge00bigegoog/page/n43/mode/2up), only "serves to attract the living flies to breed in the corpses of their comrades."

[Hunters and butchers](https://archive.org/details/experimentsonge00bigegoog/page/n41/mode/2up) knew better, Redi noted: they "protect their meats in Summer from filth by covering them with white cloths." For microbes the dispute outlived him by nearly two centuries. Louis Pasteur's work "had not been accepted as final" by its critics, and the physicist John Tyndall's experiments of the 1870s came "at the opportune time to help swing the balance of opinion," a [1942 history](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7942451/) of his work argues.

## Redi still believed oak trees made insects

Redi's experiments are "regarded as a key contribution to the downfall of belief in spontaneous generation," Emily Parke wrote in a [2014 paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.shpsc.2013.12.005). Parke argues he treated living hosts as a separate question: whether insects can come from living things that are not their parents.

He had once "believed, or rather suspected," that a fly [slit a young oak twig](https://archive.org/details/experimentsonge00bigegoog/page/n100/mode/2up) in spring and hid an egg there. Then he changed his mind, "especially as every gall or growth has its own peculiar kind of worm, gnat, or fly, which never varies," as [his account](https://archive.org/details/experimentsonge00bigegoog/page/n102/mode/2up) of galls puts it. He [credited the insects](https://archive.org/details/experimentsonge00bigegoog/page/n100/mode/2up) instead to "that soul or principle which creates the flowers and fruits of living plants." He thought [tapeworms](https://archive.org/details/experimentsonge00bigegoog/page/n124/mode/2up) arose inside living bodies the same way.

His first guess was nearer the truth, though on oaks the egg-layer is often a wasp rather than a fly. Oak gall wasps "lay their eggs on oak tissues, and this action induces the formation of a gall structure around the eggs," a [2024 article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11060654/) in *Plant Physiology* explains.

No worm reached Redi's veiled meat, though only because he picked off the ones that [worked through the fine meshes](https://archive.org/details/experimentsonge00bigegoog/page/n45/mode/2up) of the outer net. Some of his flies, [he noted](https://archive.org/details/experimentsonge00bigegoog/page/n45/mode/2up), did not even wait for the net: they "dropped them in the air before reaching the net." Every maggot comes from a fly, and some leave their mother already hatched. Strictly FYI.
