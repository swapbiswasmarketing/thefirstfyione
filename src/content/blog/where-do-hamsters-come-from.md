The farmer looked on in dismay as the dirt piled up over his young wheat. A local hunter named Tah'an and a crew of villagers were digging into his field, near Aleppo in northern Syria, after a small golden animal. It was [April 12, 1930](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/the-untold-story-of-the-hamster-aka-mr-saddlebags-1223774/), and Israel Aharoni, a biologist from Jerusalem, had come to catch one.

Eight feet down they reached a nest holding a mother and ten pups, in Rob Dunn's account of the expedition for [Smithsonian magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/the-untold-story-of-the-hamster-aka-mr-saddlebags-1223774/). Placed in a box, the mother turned on her litter. "I saw the [mother] hamster harden her heart and sever with ugly cruelty the head of the pup that approached her most closely," Aharoni wrote in his memoir, and Tah'an killed her in a cyanide jar.

The pups left behind still had their eyes closed when Aharoni took them back to Jerusalem.

## Where do hamsters come from?

Hamsters come from Europe and Asia, where [wild species](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Cricetinae/) live in deserts, steppes and farm fields from central Europe to Korea. The usual pet, the golden or Syrian hamster, is native to farm fields around Aleppo in northern Syria and southern Turkey. Nearly every laboratory golden hamster descends from [one litter captured in Syria in 1930](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7158296/), and Dunn writes that every domestic one does. Dwarf hamsters come from central Asia.

The [IUCN Red List assessment](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/13219/107411865/pdf) places the golden hamster's main range on the "fertile, agricultural and densely populated Aleppinian plateau," **280 to 380 metres** (about 920 to 1,250 feet) above sea level, and lists it as native to just **two countries, Syria and Turkey**. It lives mainly in arable fields "with annual crops such as wheat, barley, chickpeas, lentils, and vegetables." Reports of the species on open steppe, the assessors add, probably come from confusion with its relative the Turkish hamster.

Surveys in northern Syria in 1997 and 1999 by Rolf Gattermann's team, reported in the [Journal of Zoology](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-zoology/article/abs/notes-on-the-current-distribution-and-the-ecology-of-wild-golden-hamsters-mesocricetus-auratus/A57232691258B6DB389C66236CA330A0), mapped 30 burrows and examined 23 of them. No occupied burrow held more than one adult, and depths ran from 36 to 106 centimetres, roughly one to three and a half feet, far shallower than the eight feet reported for Aharoni's dig.

In southern Turkey, a [2008 paper in Biology Letters](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2610053/) found wild females "out of their burrows almost exclusively during daylight hours," between 6 and 8 a.m. and between 4 and 7:30 p.m. In the laboratory the same species is "strictly nocturnal," and the paper calls it "a prime model for studying the mechanisms controlling circadian rhythms." Its caged comparison animals came from wild stock caught in 1999, "roughly five generations removed from their wild progenitors," and they kept to the night as well.

## Three littermates started the laboratory colony

The dig was for Aharoni's colleague Saul Adler, who "sought a laboratory animal susceptible to infection with Leishmania," in the words of a [2015 chapter on hamsters](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7158296/) in the textbook *Laboratory Animal Medicine*. In Dunn's account, a hamster species already used in research in China would not breed in captivity and had to be caught again and again.

The head counts after the dig change from telling to telling. In [Dunn's version](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/the-untold-story-of-the-hamster-aka-mr-saddlebags-1223774/), Aharoni began with 11 hamsters and 9 made it back to Jerusalem, where they were fed with an eye-dropper. Michael Murphy's 1985 history of the capture has Aharoni and his wife raising the ten surviving pups by hand, then an escape and the recapture of all but one, after which "the remaining nine animals were given over to" Haim Ben-Menahem, [he writes](https://archive.org/details/hamsterreproduct0000unse). One night five of them chewed their way out of a wooden cage, and none was recovered alive.

Even the sexes of the four survivors are disputed. Aharoni recorded three males and one female, while papers by B. Aharoni in 1932 and by Adler in 1948 say one male and three females survived and that the male later killed one female, [Murphy notes](https://archive.org/details/hamsterreproduct0000unse). Dunn and the textbook chapter end in the same place: **three littermates, one male and two females**.

Ben-Menahem was director of the Hebrew University's animal house, according to a [1989 history](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2651351/) in the *Israel Journal of Medical Sciences*. [Dunn](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/the-untold-story-of-the-hamster-aka-mr-saddlebags-1223774/) describes him putting one female in a chamber filled with hay and then letting in her brother; when the pair mated, Ben-Menahem credited God, who "nudged a single wheel of the uncountable wheels of nature - and a miracle happened."

> "The laboratory hamsters are descendents of a brother-sister pair mated in 1930." - [Biology Letters, 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2610053/)

The colony numbered 150 within its first year, Chris Henwood wrote in a [1992 history for the British Hamster Association](https://web.archive.org/web/20190403211735/https://www.britishhamsterassociation.org.uk/get_article.php?fname=journal%2Fdiscover_syrian.html), though he noted that other authorities give other figures. Its descendants were "first imported to the United States in 1938," the [textbook chapter](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7158296/) says, and by 1948 Adler, writing from the Hebrew University's department of parasitology, could tell [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/162256b0) that the golden hamster "is now being bred in scientific institutions in Europe and America."

Hamsters reached England in 1931, smuggled in Adler's coat pockets and given to E. Hindle of the Zoological Society of London, in [Henwood's account](https://web.archive.org/web/20190403211735/https://www.britishhamsterassociation.org.uk/get_article.php?fname=journal%2Fdiscover_syrian.html). The pet trade came later. In the United States the great promoter was Albert Marsh of Mobile, Alabama, who "was an unemployed highway engineer when he won his first hamster on a bet," [Murphy writes](https://archive.org/details/hamsterreproduct0000unse), and who "started the first large commercial hamstery."

## The Aaron Abrahams story names the wrong finder

A [Mental Floss article](https://www.mentalfloss.com/animals/pets/where-do-hamsters-come) tells it differently: "in 1930, an archaeologist named Aaron Abrahams happened upon a nest containing a rodent and 12 small babies." The accounts from Jerusalem name someone else. The [1989 history](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2651351/), by J.H. Adler of the Hebrew University-Hadassah Medical School, says Israel Aharoni collected the family "with the aid of a hunter by the name of Georgius," and Dunn's account quotes Aharoni's own memoir. Neither names an Aaron Abrahams, and Aharoni was a biologist, not an archaeologist.

The same [article](https://www.mentalfloss.com/animals/pets/where-do-hamsters-come) says the hamster "was thought to be extinct by the late 1920s," and cites nothing for it. Dunn calls the animal rare and poorly known, and says Tah'an had seen it before. Science had a record of it long before: on April 9, 1839, George Robert Waterhouse exhibited "A new species of Hamster" at the [Zoological Society of London](https://archive.org/details/per_proceedings-of-the-zoological-society-of-londo_proceedings-of-the-zoological-society-o_1839), named it *Cricetus auratus*, gave its home as Aleppo and called it "remarkable for its deep golden yellow colouring."

The line that every pet hamster comes from one litter holds only for golden hamsters, and even among captive golden hamsters it needs a "nearly." Murphy himself took twelve wild animals from Aleppo to the United States in 1971, [Henwood records](https://web.archive.org/web/20190403211735/https://www.britishhamsterassociation.org.uk/get_article.php?fname=journal%2Fdiscover_syrian.html). In their 1997 and 1999 surveys, Gattermann's team caught six females and seven males near Aleppo, and one female gave birth to six pups. All 19 went to Germany, where with three more from the University of Aleppo they formed "a new breeding stock," the team [reported](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-zoology/article/abs/notes-on-the-current-distribution-and-the-ecology-of-wild-golden-hamsters-mesocricetus-auratus/A57232691258B6DB389C66236CA330A0).

## Wild golden hamsters are Endangered, and farmers treat them as pests

The [IUCN](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/13219/107411865/pdf) lists the golden hamster as **Endangered**, in an assessment made in August 2019 and published in 2022; its 2008 rating was Vulnerable, and its 1996 rating Endangered. Its estimated extent of occurrence is **4,743 square kilometres** (about 1,830 square miles), "restricted to a small, fragmented area on the Turkish/Syrian border," and only three localities are known in Turkey. There may be fewer than **2,500** mature animals, though the assessors say more data are needed to confirm it.

Habitat loss, caused by increasing human settlements, is the main threat in Syria, the [assessment](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/13219/107411865/pdf) says. Golden hamsters are also "considered one of the most important agricultural pests," and they are "trapped or poisoned in February as soon as burrow entrances become visible," with farmers applying large quantities of rodenticides provided by the government. The assessment lists the Syrian civil war among possible threats and notes that field research on the species' status has been impossible "for a long time."

The European hamster is in worse shape. "Once abundant across much of Europe and Russia," it became **Critically Endangered** in 2020, the [IUCN announced](https://www.iucn.org/news/species/202007/almost-a-third-lemurs-and-north-atlantic-right-whale-now-critically-endangered-iucn-red-list). Its females averaged more than 20 offspring a year for most of the 20th century and now have 5 to 6 pups a year, the IUCN said.

## Dwarf hamsters come from the dry steppes of central Asia

Dwarf hamsters are a minority of pets, at least among those seen by vets in Britain. Of **16,605** pet hamsters seen at 886 UK veterinary clinics in 2016, **73%** were Syrian, 14% Djungarian and 6% Roborovski's, a [2022 VetCompass study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9796486/) of clinical records found. Those two dwarfs belong to a different genus, *Phodopus*, whose three species live only in the dry steppe and desert of central Asia, according to a [2022 paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9185382/) in *Genome Biology and Evolution*.
