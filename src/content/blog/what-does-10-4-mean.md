**"10-4 Acknowledgement."** It sits fourth on a revised list of **70 police radio signals** printed on [page eight of The APCO Bulletin for January 1940](https://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.ssl.cf1.rackcdn.com/1940-01-p008-200.pdf), and the page says nothing more about it. No signal on the list means yes. The nearest thing to one is the radiotelegraph quiz printed beside it, where answers "in the affirmative" count as promises.

The rest of the list is stranger. In [the same January 1940 list](https://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.ssl.cf1.rackcdn.com/1940-01-p008-200.pdf), 10-32 is **"Is drunkometer available?"**, 10-14 is **"Convoy or escort"** and 10-82 is "Reserve room with bath at hotel for officer," followed by a blank for his number. 10-12, "Officials or visitors present," was later drafted into [the folklore about why police are called 12](/blog/why-are-police-called-12/).

## What does 10-4 mean?

**10-4 means "message received,"** the wording of [APCO's 1957 standard list](https://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.ssl.cf1.rackcdn.com/1957-04-pp007-200.pdf). It is one of the police radio ten signals that the Associated Police Communication Officers, or APCO, [registered and officially adopted in 1940](https://archive.org/details/nationaltraining025505mbp/page/n46/mode/1up), and its meaning there was **"Acknowledgement"**: I heard you, not yes. APCO later stretched it toward "okay": the brevity code from [its Project 14 in 1974](https://www.apcointl.org/community/projects/) defines 10-4 as "Affirmative (Ok)."

## The ten is an alert and the number is the message

APCO set out its reasoning in a 1967 operating manual, reprinted in [a 1968 federal training manual](https://archive.org/details/nationaltraining025505mbp) for police radio personnel: "The purpose of the TEN SIGNALS is two-fold: to achieve reliability and speed."

Reliability comes from the front half. In [the manual's words](https://archive.org/details/nationaltraining025505mbp/page/n46/mode/1up), the "10-" is a euphonic alert "attesting to the fact that information is about to follow." The number is the information content, several words squeezed into one figure, and numbers "are more easily read through the noise that is ever present in a two-way radio system."

> In APCO's own account, 10-4 has two parts. The "10-" only tells the listener to pay attention, and the 4 carries the entire message.

[The manual](https://archive.org/details/nationaltraining025505mbp/page/n46/mode/1up) also sets a grammar rule: "A TEN SIGNAL is complete in itself." The correct form is **"10-4, Rockford."** The incorrect one is **"I am 10-4 on that information, Rockford."** Anyone who has answered "I'm 10-4" has made the manual's textbook error.

## The warm-up story has no source attached

The popular explanation is mechanical. [Wikipedia's article on ten-codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten-code) says 1930s police radios ran on a motor-generator called a dynamotor, which "took from 1/10 to 1/4 of a second to 'spin up' to full power," so saying "ten" first gave the transmitter time to reach full strength. That passage cites no source of its own.

Neither the 1940 Bulletin pages cited here nor the 1967 manual mentions a warm-up. Neither Bulletin page gives a reason for the prefix, and the manual's alert sentence points back to [its section on calling procedure](https://archive.org/details/nationaltraining025505mbp/page/n40/mode/1up), which dates the custom of a calling station naming itself first to a time when "static and noise on the low frequencies then in use made it necessary for the called station to be alerted that something was coming through the racket." Until a period source mentions the dynamotor, the warm-up story is plausible but unsourced.

## APCO itself turned 10-4 into a yes in 1974

The standard list printed in [the APCO Bulletin of April 1957](https://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.ssl.cf1.rackcdn.com/1957-04-pp007-200.pdf), prepared by the National Operating Procedure Committee in 1955, gave **10-4 as "Message received."** [The 1967 manual's list](https://archive.org/details/nationaltraining025505mbp/page/n58/mode/1up) went back to "Acknowledgement," moved "Message received" to 10-69, ran to 10-99 and gave **10-74 to "Negative"**, with no affirmative at all. [Its glossary](https://archive.org/details/nationaltraining025505mbp/page/n128/mode/1up) defines acknowledgement as the act by which one operator "signifies to another that a message has been received."

A February 1968 Bulletin column, [reprinted in the training manual](https://archive.org/details/nationaltraining025505mbp/page/n130/mode/1up), coached operators to keep the word "I" off the air: "instead of 'I will do it,' substitute '10-4.' Instead of 'I don't think so,' say '10-74.'" With [APCO's Project 14 in 1974](https://www.apcointl.org/community/projects/) the pairing became official: [its brevity code](https://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.ssl.cf1.rackcdn.com/1974-proj14-apr-060.200.pdf) lists **10-4 as "Affirmative (Ok)"** and moves "Negative" to 10-10.

[UPI's 1986 obituary of Broderick Crawford](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1986/04/27/Oscar-winner-Broderick-Crawford-dies-at-74/1450514962000/) said the actor "probably was best remembered for barking 'ten-four, ten-four' into his police car radio" on Highway Patrol, "the prototype 1950s police television drama." When C.W. McCall's CB trucker anthem "Convoy" [topped the charts in 1976](https://www.npr.org/2017/06/06/531749486/the-legacy-of-convoy-how-a-trucker-s-protest-anthem-became-a-70s-hit), 10-4 had been in APCO's Bulletin since [the Illinois list it printed in 1939](https://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.ssl.cf1.rackcdn.com/1939-05-p006-200.pdf), and [the list reprinted in April 1940](https://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.ssl.cf1.rackcdn.com/1940-04-p012-200.pdf) had already given 10-14 to "Convoy or escort."

## Four states were using a version of the list before APCO adopted it

The name usually attached to the codes is **Charles "Charlie" Hopper**. [The same article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten-code) makes him communications director for Illinois State Police District 10 at Pesotum and credits him with the invention, citing a 2006 Law and Order article by James Careless that never mentions him in [its archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20160316230251/http://www.hendonpub.com/resources/article_archive/results/details?id=3615). APCO's magazine, PSC, ran a 2024 article headlined ["Charles Hopper and the 10-Codes."](https://www.pscmagazine-digital.com/pscs/0524_september_october_2024/MobilePagedArticle.action?articleId=2009613)

APCO's own Bulletins do put a Charles L. Hopper in the Illinois State Police in 1939, though at Springfield rather than Pesotum. "Hopper, Charles L." of Springfield, Ill., appears among [the paid-up members as of February 15](https://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.ssl.cf1.rackcdn.com/1939-02-p004-200.pdf), "C. L. Hopper, Ill. S. P., Springfield" is on [the October convention roll](https://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.ssl.cf1.rackcdn.com/1939-10-p014-200.pdf), and the November issue named him to [the Operating Procedure Committee](https://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.ssl.cf1.rackcdn.com/1939-11-p003-200.pdf) for three years. None of those entries credits him with the signals.

In May of that year the Bulletin printed [Illinois' 17 Operators' Ten Signals](https://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.ssl.cf1.rackcdn.com/1939-05-p006-200.pdf), 10-4 "Acknowledgement" among them, noting that "many requests are received from time to time by Illinois for a copy." [APCO's 1976 Bulletin](https://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.ssl.cf1.rackcdn.com/1976-01-pp014-200.pdf) says the first ten signals it published, "17 of them," appeared in 1939, and that a year later they were "revised and enlarged to 70 signals."

The 1940 revision came from a standards committee, chaired by T.M.B. Lowe of Springfield, Illinois, which made "numerous changes" to "the list that has been in use in Iowa, Illinois, Missouri and Minnesota for the past several years," [the January 1940 Bulletin reported](https://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.ssl.cf1.rackcdn.com/1940-01-p008-200.pdf). By April [the Bulletin reported](https://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.ssl.cf1.rackcdn.com/1940-04-p012-200.pdf) that the list "seems to have met with general approval at the Chicago meeting of the committee" and that the signals would "go into effect in Iowa, Minnesota, Wisconsin, Missouri, Indiana and Illinois at once."

APCO's dates disagree. [The 1967 manual](https://archive.org/details/nationaltraining025505mbp/page/n46/mode/1up) says the signals were originally "formulated by Illinois APCO members in 1935," the same year [it gives](https://archive.org/details/nationaltraining025505mbp/page/n18/mode/1up) for APCO's own founding, while [APCO's project history](https://www.apcointl.org/community/projects/) puts the establishment of the 10 Codes in 1937. Neither the manual nor the project history names an inventor.

## Federal doctrine wants it off multi-agency radio

The Department of Homeland Security's SAFECOM program, in [its guide to making the switch](https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/publications/PlainLanguageGuide.pdf), says codes answered two problems: by the 1940s two-way radio "began to overload early single channel radio systems," and officers wanted privacy on channels that could be monitored.

Agencies also wrote their own lists. [SAFECOM's example](https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/publications/PlainLanguageGuide.pdf) is **10-88**, which "may mean 'present phone number' in one agency and 'officer needs help' in another." [The 1940 list](https://28011b0082f55a9e1ec0-aecfa82ae628504f4b1d229bd9030ae1.ssl.cf1.rackcdn.com/1940-01-p008-200.pdf) had already reserved its "80" series "for assignment by nets for local use," suggesting 10-88 as a request for the phone number at which to call an officer.

FEMA's [National Incident Management System](https://www.fema.gov/sites/default/files/2020-07/fema_nims_doctrine-2017.pdf) now states the rule directly: "Using plain language and clear text, not codes, in incident management is a matter of public safety." [SAFECOM notes](https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/publications/PlainLanguageGuide.pdf) that NIMS requires it only for mutual aid and strongly encourages it for day-to-day operations. James Careless predicted in [Law and Order](https://web.archive.org/web/20160316230251/http://www.hendonpub.com/resources/article_archive/results/details?id=3615) in 2006 that "soon the only appropriate place for 10-codes will be on CB radios and old reruns of 'Highway Patrol.'"

Federal doctrine now presses police to drop 10-4 on the radios it was written for, though APCO's own code long ago gave it the meaning its first lists never had: yes. Strictly FYI.
