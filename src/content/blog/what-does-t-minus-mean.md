The countdown clock at Kennedy Space Center read ten minutes to go, and it was not moving. Four astronauts were sealed inside the Orion spacecraft on top of the Artemis II rocket when, at 6:19 p.m. EDT on 1 April 2026, NASA [announced](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/missions/2026/04/01/live-artemis-ii-launch-day-updates/) that its launch team had decided "to extend the T-10 minute hold".

The launch window opened at **6:24**. The terminal count began at 6:25, and the rocket lifted off at **6:35 p.m.** The clock had first started at 4:44 p.m. on the Monday, [49 hours and 40 minutes before the planned launch](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/missions/2026/03/30/nasas-artemis-ii-launch-mission-countdown-begins/), and it had since been stopped three times, each stop written into NASA's published timeline.

## What does T-minus mean?

T-minus is the time remaining before **T-zero**, the scheduled moment of an event such as a rocket's liftoff, so T-minus 45 seconds means 45 seconds to go. On NASA launches the T clock follows a sequence of events and **stops during planned holds**, while a second count, **L-minus**, counts real time to the scheduled liftoff.

Until the last planned hold is over, a T-minus reading is shorter than the real time left to liftoff. NASA and NOAA pages say the T stands for "time", though a NASA shuttle launch director said "test". After zero the count runs upward as [mission elapsed time](https://www.nasa.gov/blogs/missions/2026/04/01/live-artemis-ii-launch-day-updates/), with the Artemis II rocket due to clear its launch tower at +00:00:07.

## A NASA countdown keeps two times, and only one of them stops

NASA's [published Artemis II countdown](https://www.nasa.gov/general/nasa-releases-artemis-ii-moon-mission-launch-countdown/) defines both in two sentences: "The 'L minus' indicates how far away liftoff is in hours and minutes. The 'T minus' time is a sequence of events built into the launch countdown." During planned holds, it adds, "the countdown clock is intentionally stopped and the T- time also stops. The L- time, however, continues to advance."

NOAA's satellite service says the L- time [is "synced to the clock on the wall"](https://www.nesdis.noaa.gov/news/top-5-terms-you-should-know-satellite-launch). The holds are built in, NASA's countdown page says, "to allow the launch team to target a precise launch window, and to provide a cushion of time for certain tasks and procedures without impacting the overall schedule."

| Entry in NASA's Artemis II timeline | Time to scheduled liftoff (L-) | The T clock |
|---|---|---|
| Countdown clock begins | L-49H40M | Starts |
| 2-hour 45-minute built-in hold | L-12H35M to L-9H50M | Stopped |
| Resume T-Clock from T-8H10M | L-9H50M | Restarts at 8 hours 10 minutes |
| 1-hour 10-minute built-in hold | Starts at L-5H10M | Stopped |
| 30-minute built-in hold | Starts at L-40M | Stopped at 10 minutes |

> When NASA's Artemis II timeline restarts the T clock at T-8H10M, nine hours and fifty minutes remain before the scheduled liftoff. The missing hour and forty minutes is exactly the two built-in holds still to come, one of 1 hour 10 minutes and one of 30.

Even after the last built-in hold, the T clock can be wound back. If the team needs more than **3 minutes** of hold between T-6 minutes and T-1 minute 30 seconds, the same Artemis II countdown page says, "the countdown would recycle back to T-10."

Built-in holds go back at least to Apollo. For Apollo 16, NASA's [history office records](https://www.nasa.gov/history/50-years-ago-countdown-begins-for-apollo-16-the-fifth-moon-landing-mission/), "the terminal countdown began on April 14 at the T-minus 28-hour mark and included several built-in holds." The launch was on **16 April 1972**, two days later.

## NASA has explained the T two different ways

One answer is time. A 2010 [NASA release](https://web.archive.org/web/20101109013350/http://www.nasa.gov/mission_pages/epoxi/epoxi20101004.html) about the EPOXI comet flyby said T-minus "translates to 'Time-minus'". NOAA's 2016 glossary and a [2022 post from NASA's Launch Services Program](https://x.com/NASA_LSP/status/1498769148767318017) carry the same sentence: "The 'T' stands for time."

The other answer came from the firing room. In a [NASA podcast published on 28 June 2006](https://web.archive.org/web/20230506045011/https://www.nasa.gov/multimedia/podcasting/ksc_leinbach_transcript.html), shuttle launch director **Mike Leinbach** said he had put the question to his elders when he joined the space programme about 20 years earlier, "and it turns out that T stands for test. Because it's not always related to time." The T, he explained, could mark the start of a test, "independent of the time of day."

[PolitiFact](https://www.politifact.com/factchecks/2023/may/05/instagram-posts/affinity-for-the-devil-no-nasas-t-minus-countdown/), ruling in 2023 on an Instagram claim that NASA's T-minus countdown was a mocking reference to Satan, concluded that the letter "stands simply for 'time' or 'test.'"

## A 1929 silent film is credited with the launch countdown

Fritz Lang's *Frau im Mond* (Woman in the Moon) opened in Berlin on **15 October 1929**. The rocket pioneer Hermann Oberth was Lang's scientific advisor and, according to a [Cinematheque francaise dossier](https://www.cinematheque.fr/zooms/femmelune/en/telechargement/zoom-frau_textes_en.pdf), created a model of the film's rocket.

A 2019 dpa report in the German magazine [Forschung & Lehre](https://www.forschung-und-lehre.de/zeitfragen/wie-das-kino-die-welt-lehrte-rueckwaerts-zu-zaehlen-2205) describes the film's title cards running down from "Noch 20 Sekunden" (20 seconds to go) to a final "JETZT", capital letters for "now".

Lang, the Cinematheque says, claimed the countdown "as his own idea", and a 2012 [DGA Quarterly photo essay](https://web.archive.org/web/20220225041959/http://www.dga.org/-/media/Files/DGAQ-Photo-Essays/1203PhotoEssayOuterLimits.ashx?la=en&hash=BD4D89ABA192F2949613BD4B38C5110D5EE25F4E) says the film "invented the countdown before the launch of a rocket". The noun came more than 20 years later: [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/countdown) dates *countdown* to about **1952**, and [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/countdown) to 1953, American English, "in early use especially of launches of rockets or missiles".

How the count reached real launch pads is less certain. Wolfgang Jung, head of launch services at DLR, Germany's aerospace centre, said in the dpa report that he knew of no official countdown before the film, and that German engineers such as Wernher von Braun, a former student of Oberth's, knew Lang's film and carried the backward count into NASA's programme. The line of descent is plausible, but the report rests it on his word alone.

## A novel and a boat race counted down before the film

In Jules Verne's 1889 novel [*Sans dessus dessous*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/12533/pg12533-images.html), the Gun Club fires a giant cannon dug into Kilimanjaro at midnight, and Barbicane counts the minutes on his chronometer: "Minuit moins cinq! - Moins quatre! - Moins trois! - Moins deux! - Moins une!" The seconds follow, from "Plus que vingt secondes!" (only twenty seconds left) to "Plus qu'une!" (only one), and Barbicane shouts "Feu!" (fire).

*Minuit moins cinq*, literally "midnight [minus](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/moins) five", is everyday French for five to midnight, and an [1890 English translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/10547/10547-h/10547-h.htm) gives the count as "Five minutes to twelve, four minutes, three minutes, two minutes, one minute to twelve".

An older count had already appeared in English. William Everett's 1865 book [*On the Cam*](https://archive.org/details/oncamlectureson00everiala/page/192/mode/2up) describes timekeepers at a Cambridge college boat race counting the crews down to the starting gun: "Ten, nine, eight, seven, six, five, four, three, two, one, - gun." As with [the first video game](/blog/what-was-the-first-video-game/), which count came first depends on the test, and the film's credit is the narrower one: the first countdown to a rocket launch.

On 1 April, the ten minutes on the Artemis II clock marked a place in NASA's sequence of events rather than a stretch of time: when NASA made its 6:19 p.m. announcement, those minutes had not begun to run, and the rocket left at 6:35. Like [the medieval moment, which stretched in summer and shrank in winter](/blog/how-long-is-a-moment/), a T-minus reading is named for a length it does not always keep. Strictly FYI.
