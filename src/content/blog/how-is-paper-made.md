Rene-Antoine Ferchault de Reaumur read his [*History of Wasps*](https://archive.org/details/histoiredelacad19acad/page/n385/mode/1up) to a public assembly of the Royal Academy of Sciences in Paris in 1719 without knowing where wasps got the material for their paper nests. The [printed memoir](https://archive.org/details/histoiredelacad19acad/page/n404/mode/1up), which the Academy dates to 15 November 1719 but did not publish until 1721, admits as much, then tells how he found out.

The answer came after he had stopped looking. A wasp landed beside him on the frame of his open window and began cutting very fine pieces of wood from it. "The wasp did not swallow what she had thus detached," Reaumur wrote, in translation. She added each piece to a little mass held between her legs, and he caught her in the act.

The memoir also carries a proposal: wasps make their paper from wood, and they "seem to invite us to try whether we could not succeed in making fine, good paper by using certain woods directly," [it says](https://archive.org/details/histoiredelacad19acad/page/n407/mode/1up). At the time, Europe made its paper from rags.

## How is paper made?

Mills make paper by breaking wood or other plant material into cellulose fibres, mixing them with water into pulp, and draining the pulp on a **moving wire screen** so the fibres mat into a sheet. Presses squeeze out more water, steam-heated rollers dry the sheet, and heavy rolls smooth it before it is wound onto a reel.

1. **Debark and chip.** Logs are stripped of bark and cut into chips, typically about 20 mm long and 4 mm thick.
2. **Pulp.** The chips are cooked in chemicals, or the wood is ground, until the cellulose fibres come apart.
3. **Wash and bleach.** Spent cooking liquor, knots and uncooked chips are washed and screened out, and pulp for white paper is bleached in stages.
4. **Form.** The pulp is refined, mixed with fillers such as clay and spread on the moving wire belt of a paper machine, most commonly a Fourdrinier, where gravity and vacuum drain the water.
5. **Press.** Rollers squeeze more water out of the wet sheet and compress the fibres.
6. **Dry.** Steam-heated rollers dry the sheet, and the fibres bond together.
7. **Calender and reel.** Heavy rolls press the paper thinner and smoother, and it is wound onto large rolls.

The US Environmental Protection Agency's [profile of the pulp and paper industry](https://archive.epa.gov/sectors/web/pdf/pulppasn.pdf) sums up the process in one sentence: paper is made "by applying a watery suspension of cellulose fibers to a screen which allows the water to drain and leaves the fibrous particles behind in a sheet." Brown grades skip the bleach: unbleached pulp typically goes into "boxboard, linerboard, and grocery bags."

Recycled paper starts in a pulper, a large tank of water where a rotor churns used paper back into fibres. Strings, wires and heavier debris are pulled or spun out, ink can be removed, and the cleaned pulp goes on to the paper machine.

## Grinding keeps the lignin in, and cooking dissolves it

Wood fibres are bound by lignin, which the EPA calls "the glue-like substance" holding them together. Mills free the fibres by grinding the wood or by cooking it, and the paper they want decides which.

| | Mechanical pulp | Kraft pulp |
|---|---|---|
| How the fibres are freed | Grinding or refining | Cooking in sodium hydroxide and sodium sulfide |
| Share of the wood that becomes pulp | Up to 95 percent | About 50 percent |
| Fibres | Short and weak | Long and strong |
| Lignin | Stays in | Up to 90 percent removed |
| Typical paper | Newsprint | Strong or bright-white grades |

Kraft is German for "strength," the EPA notes. Kraft pulping made about **83 percent** of US wood pulp tonnage in 2000, while mechanical pulp goes "principally for newsprint and other nonpermanent paper goods." That pulp keeps its lignin, which "promotes acid hydrolysis," a reaction that degrades paper, according to the [Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/preservation/care/deterioratebrochure.html). Rag papers, by contrast, "tend to remain strong and durable," the library says.

## Before wood, paper came from rags, hemp and bark

Western paper was made from "cotton and linen clothing rags" until the **mid-19th century**, the [Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/preservation/care/deterioratebrochure.html) says. Early Chinese paper was apparently made from "a suspension of hemp waste in water," says Georgia Tech's [Robert C. Williams Museum of Papermaking](https://paper.gatech.edu/early-papermaking). European mills used cotton and linen "collected mostly from recycled clothing," the museum's [history of papermaking](https://paper.gatech.edu/sites/default/files/2021-04/History%20of%20Papermaking%20Around%20the%20World.pdf) adds, sold to them by rag pickers who bought old clothes. Some paper still keeps to the rag era's fibres: the paper of a US [dollar bill](/blog/illuminati-on-the-dollar-bill/) is [**75 percent cotton and 25 percent linen**](https://www.bep.gov/currency/how-money-is-made).

Reaumur had seen a rag shortage coming. Wasps showed that paper could be made from plant fibres without their first having been "linen and rag," he wrote, and rags were getting scarce:

> "The consumption of paper increases every day, while that of linen stays about the same." - Rene-Antoine Ferchault de Reaumur, *History of Wasps*, translated

Foreigners were carrying off France's "wretched rags" for their own mills, he added. By the time a young Charles Fenerty of Nova Scotia took up the problem, demand from "printing-shops and newspaper offices in Europe and North America was outstripping the availability of rags," according to the [Dictionary of Canadian Biography](https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/fenerty_charles_12E.html).

## Two wood-pulp papers appeared in 1844

Wood had been tried before. In London in 1801, Matthias Koops printed an appendix to his history of writing materials on "[Paper made from Wood alone](https://archive.org/details/historicalaccou00koop/page/n266/mode/1up), the produce of this country, without any intermixture of rags." The first method to take hold started at a grindstone.

Friedrich Gottlob Keller, a master weaver in Hainichen, Saxony, got papermaking fibre out of spruce by grinding it on a wet grindstone, says his entry in the [*Neue Deutsche Biographie*](https://www.deutsche-biographie.de/gnd118561073.html). In **1844**, with his wife's help, he ground 100 kg of wood pulp, and a mill at Alt-Chemnitz made it into printing paper with 40 percent rag pulp, according to the older *Allgemeine Deutsche Biographie* on the same page.

A Saxon patent followed on **26 August 1845**, applied for in his name and that of Heinrich Voelter, technical director of the Bautzen paper mills. The first grinding machines appeared after 1848, and Voelter carried on without Keller and made a great profit from it.

Keller's own account, as the older biography tells it, credits two observations: wasps building their nests from wood fibre, and the fibre that always turned up in the grinding water when, as a child, he ground cherry stones held in thin wooden boards into beads. Around 1840, it says, he kept reading about the rag shortage.

On **26 October 1844**, the *Acadian Recorder* printed a letter from Fenerty with a sample he called "as firm in its texture, as white, and to all appearances as durable as the common wrapping paper," yet "actually composed of spruce wood reduced to pulp." His Dictionary of Canadian Biography entry calls the likeliest source of his idea the fuzz rubbed off the wooden saw frames at his family's sawmills, though wasp nests have been suggested too. "No commercial advantage was taken in Nova Scotia of Fenerty's discovery," it says.

## Cai Lun gets the traditional credit, but paper is older

The usual answer to who invented paper is Cai Lun, a Chinese court official, in **105 CE**, the year the Georgia Tech museum gives. His biography in the [*Book of the Later Han*](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%BE%8C%E6%BC%A2%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B778) says he conceived the idea of making paper from tree bark, hemp ends, old rags and fishing nets and presented it to the emperor, after which everyone used it and called it "Marquis Cai's paper."

That is the record of a report to the throne, not proof that paper began there. The museum says excavations "place the actual invention of papermaking some 200 years earlier," and that paper pieces from the Xuanquanzhi ruins near Dunhuang "apparently were made during the period of Emperor Wu," who reigned from 141 to 87 BCE.

Egypt's papyrus, developed as early as 3000 BCE, is older still but counts as a different material. Its makers laid strips of the plant side by side, added a crosswise layer and pounded the two "together into a laminate," while paper, in the museum's definition, is "pulped plant fiber" formed into a sheet on a screen.

The Academy's own summary of Reaumur's memoir, in the same 1719 volume, [opens](https://archive.org/details/histoiredelacad19acad/page/n24/mode/1up) with a poor opinion of wasps. They are enemies of the [honey bees](/blog/how-do-bees-make-honey/) "we love," it says, and "the work they do is absolutely useless to us." Most paper is now made the way their nests are, from wood fibre matted into sheets. Strictly FYI.
