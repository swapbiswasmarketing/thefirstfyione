Rene-Antoine Ferchault de Reaumur read his [*History of Wasps*](https://archive.org/details/histoiredelacad19acad/page/n385/mode/1up) to a public assembly of the Royal Academy of Sciences in Paris in 1719 without knowing where wasps got the material for their paper nests. The [printed memoir](https://archive.org/details/histoiredelacad19acad/page/n404/mode/1up), which the Academy dates to 15 November 1719 but did not publish until 1721, admits as much, then tells how he found out.

The answer came after he had stopped looking. A mother wasp, as he called her, of the ground-nesting kind landed beside him on the frame of his open window and began cutting very fine pieces of wood from it, and she did not swallow what she detached, [the memoir says](https://archive.org/details/histoiredelacad19acad/page/n404/mode/1up). She added each piece to a little mass held between her legs, and he caught her in the act.

The memoir also carries a proposal. Wasps make their paper from wood fibre, and they seem to invite us to try whether fine, good paper could be made by using certain woods directly, [Reaumur wrote](https://archive.org/details/histoiredelacad19acad/page/n407/mode/1up), in a passage given here in translation. At the time, as the same page says, French paper was made from rags.

## How is paper made?

Mills make paper by breaking wood or other plant material into cellulose fibres, mixing them with water into pulp, and draining the pulp on a **moving wire screen** so the fibres mat into a sheet. Presses squeeze out more water, steam-heated rollers dry the sheet, and heavy rolls smooth it before it is wound onto a reel.

1. **Debark and chip.** Logs are stripped of bark and cut into chips, usually about [20 mm long and 4 mm thick](https://archive.epa.gov/sectors/web/pdf/pulppasn.pdf).
2. **Pulp.** The chips are cooked in chemicals, or the wood is ground, until the cellulose fibres come apart.
3. **Wash and bleach.** Spent cooking liquor, bark fragments and uncooked chips are washed and screened out, and pulp for white paper is bleached in stages.
4. **Form.** The pulp is refined, mixed with fillers such as clay and spread on the moving wire belt of a paper machine, most commonly a Fourdrinier, where gravity and vacuum drain the water.
5. **Press.** Rollers squeeze more water out of the wet sheet and compress the fibres.
6. **Dry.** Steam-heated rollers dry the sheet, and the fibres bond together.
7. **Calender and reel.** Heavy rolls press the paper thinner and smoother, and it is wound onto large rolls.

The US Environmental Protection Agency's [profile of the pulp and paper industry](https://archive.epa.gov/sectors/web/pdf/pulppasn.pdf) sums up the process in one sentence: paper is made "by applying a watery suspension of cellulose fibers to a screen which allows the water to drain and leaves the fibrous particles behind in a sheet." Brown grades skip the bleach, and unbleached pulp typically goes into "boxboard, linerboard, and grocery bags," the profile says. Recycled paper starts in a pulper, a large tank of water where a rotor churns used paper back into fibres, while strings, wires and heavier debris are pulled or spun out.

## Grinding keeps the lignin in, and cooking dissolves it

Wood fibres are bound by lignin, which the [EPA calls](https://archive.epa.gov/sectors/web/pdf/pulppasn.pdf) "the glue-like substance" holding them together. Mills free the fibres by grinding the wood or by cooking it, and the paper they want decides which.

| | Mechanical pulp | Kraft pulp |
|---|---|---|
| How the fibres are freed | Grinding or refining | Cooking in sodium hydroxide and sodium sulfide |
| Share of the wood that becomes pulp | Up to 95 percent | 45 to 50 percent, as for other chemical pulps |
| Fibres | Short and weak | Long and strong |
| Lignin | Stays in | Up to 90 percent removed |
| Typical paper | Newsprint | Strong or bright-white grades |

Kraft is German for "strength," the [EPA notes](https://archive.epa.gov/sectors/web/pdf/pulppasn.pdf), and kraft pulping made about **83 percent** of US wood pulp tonnage in 2000, while mechanical pulp goes "principally for newsprint and other nonpermanent paper goods." That pulp keeps its lignin, which "promotes acid hydrolysis," a reaction that degrades paper, according to the [Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/preservation/care/deterioratebrochure.html). Rag papers, by contrast, "tend to remain strong and durable," the library says.

## Before wood, paper came from rags, hemp and bark

Western paper was made from "cotton and linen clothing rags" until the **mid-19th century**, the [Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/preservation/care/deterioratebrochure.html) says. Early Chinese paper was apparently made from "a suspension of hemp waste in water," says Georgia Tech's [Robert C. Williams Museum of Papermaking](https://paper.gatech.edu/early-papermaking). European mills used cotton and linen "collected mostly from recycled clothing," the museum's [history of papermaking](https://paper.gatech.edu/sites/default/files/2021-04/History%20of%20Papermaking%20Around%20the%20World.pdf) adds, sold to them by rag pickers who bought old clothes. Some paper still keeps to the rag era's fibres: the paper of a US dollar bill is [**75 percent cotton and 25 percent linen**](https://www.bep.gov/currency/how-money-is-made).

Reaumur had seen a rag shortage coming. Wasps showed that paper could be made from plant fibres without their first passing through linen and rag, and the masters of the paper mills knew only too well that rags were getting scarce, [he wrote](https://archive.org/details/histoiredelacad19acad/page/n407/mode/1up):

> "The consumption of paper increases every day, while that of linen stays about the same." - Rene-Antoine Ferchault de Reaumur, *History of Wasps*, translated

Foreigners were also buying up France's poor old rags for their own mills, he added. By the time a young Charles Fenerty of Nova Scotia took up the problem, demand from "printing-shops and newspaper offices in Europe and North America was outstripping the availability of rags," according to the [Dictionary of Canadian Biography](https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/fenerty_charles_12E.html).

## Two wood-pulp papers appeared in the 1840s

Wood had been tried before. In London in 1801, Matthias Koops printed an appendix to his history of writing materials on what he called "[Paper made from Wood alone](https://archive.org/details/historicalaccou00koop/page/n266/mode/1up)," grown in Britain, with no rags, waste paper, bark or straw mixed in. The method that took hold started at a grindstone.

Friedrich Gottlob Keller, a master weaver in Hainichen, Saxony, got papermaking fibre out of spruce by grinding it on a wet grindstone, says his entry in the [*Neue Deutsche Biographie*](https://www.deutsche-biographie.de/gnd118561073.html). In **1844**, with his wife's help, he ground 100 kg of wood pulp, and a mill at Alt-Chemnitz made it into printing paper with 40 percent rag pulp, according to the older [*Allgemeine Deutsche Biographie*](https://www.deutsche-biographie.de/gnd118561073.html) on the same page.

Keller could not get a patent on the grinding method on his own: the Saxon privilege he received in **1845** was for making tanning extract and bark paper from conifer bark, says the [*Saechsische Biografie*](https://saebi.isgv.de/biografie/Friedrich_Gottlob_Keller_%281816-1895%29). Heinrich Voelter, technical director of the Bautzen paper mills, got in touch with him in 1846 and paid him 700 thalers in instalments for a share in that privilege and for instruction in the grinding method, [the same biography](https://saebi.isgv.de/biografie/Friedrich_Gottlob_Keller_%281816-1895%29) says. A five-year patent on the grinding process, which Voelter pursued, was granted in 1847, according to a [2016 history of the invention](https://shb.publia.org/shb/article/download/122/167). Grinding machines came out only after 1848, Keller could not pay his share when the patent renewals fell due in 1852, and Voelter carried on without him at a great profit, the [*Neue Deutsche Biographie*](https://www.deutsche-biographie.de/gnd118561073.html) says.

Keller's own account, as the older biography tells it, credits two observations: wasps building their nests from wood fibre, and the fibre that always turned up in the grinding water when, as a child, he ground cherry stones held in thin wooden boards into beads. Around 1840, [it says](https://www.deutsche-biographie.de/gnd118561073.html), he kept reading about the rag shortage.

On **26 October 1844**, the *Acadian Recorder* printed a letter from Fenerty with a sample he called "as firm in its texture, as white, and to all appearances as durable as the common wrapping paper," yet "actually composed of spruce wood reduced to pulp," his [biography](https://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/fenerty_charles_12E.html) records. According to tradition he had shown a sample to Charles Hamilton, whom the biography calls his wife's brother-in-law, in 1838 or 1839, which would put him ahead of Keller, but he published nothing before the letter. The biography calls the likeliest source of his idea the fuzz rubbed off the wooden saw frames at his family's sawmills, though wasp nests have been suggested too. "No commercial advantage was taken in Nova Scotia of Fenerty's discovery," it says.

## Cai Lun gets the traditional credit, but paper is older

The usual answer to who invented paper is Cai Lun, a Chinese court official, in **105 CE**, the year the [Georgia Tech museum](https://paper.gatech.edu/early-papermaking) gives. His biography in the [*Book of the Later Han*](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%BE%8C%E6%BC%A2%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B778) says writing had long been done on bamboo slips or on silk, which was also called paper, and that silk was costly and bamboo heavy. Cai Lun conceived the idea of making paper from tree bark, hemp ends, old rags and fishing nets and presented it to the emperor, it says, after which everyone used it and called it Marquis Cai's paper.

That is the record of a report to the throne, not proof that paper began there. The [museum says](https://paper.gatech.edu/early-papermaking) archaeology has placed "the actual invention of papermaking some 200 years earlier," and that paper pieces from the Xuanquanzhi ruins near Dunhuang "apparently were made during the period of Emperor Wu," who [reigned from 141 to 87 BCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Wu_of_Han).

Egypt's papyrus, developed as early as 3000 BCE, is older still but counts as a different material. Its makers laid strips of the plant side by side, added a crosswise layer and pounded the two "together into a laminate," while paper, in the [museum's definition](https://paper.gatech.edu/sites/default/files/2021-04/History%20of%20Papermaking%20Around%20the%20World.pdf), is "pulped plant fiber" formed into a sheet on a screen.

The Academy's own summary of Reaumur's memoir, in the same 1719 volume, [opens](https://archive.org/details/histoiredelacad19acad/page/n24/mode/1up) by setting wasps against bees. The work wasps do is absolutely useless to us, it says, and they are enemies of the bees we love, though in the eyes of philosophers the two insects are fairly equal. Since the mid-19th century, paper mills have done what the wasps do, matting wood fibre into sheets. Strictly FYI.
