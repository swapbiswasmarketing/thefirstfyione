Swat one and you feel nothing but satisfaction. Mosquitoes spread malaria, dengue, yellow fever and Zika, according to the [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/vector-borne-diseases), and the WHO's [malaria fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/malaria) estimates that malaria alone killed **610,000 people in 2024**. So it is a fair question, and one biologists have argued about in print: why do we need mosquitoes at all? If we could push a button and erase them, should we?

The honest answer is messier than "yes" or "no." Start with the fact that the insect you picture when you hear "mosquito" is a small slice of what mosquitoes are.

## Most mosquitoes are not out to get you

In 2010 the journal *Nature* put the question to mosquito biologists for a news feature by Janet Fang, ["A world without mosquitoes"](https://web.archive.org/web/20101228225603/http://www.nature.com/news/2010/100721/full/466432a.html), which counted **3,500 named species** of mosquito, "of which only a couple of hundred bite or bother humans." The count has grown since: the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/mosquitoes/about/index.html) now says over 3,700 types of mosquitoes can be found worldwide.

The species behind the major human diseases belong mainly to three genera. The WHO's [vector-borne diseases fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/vector-borne-diseases) lists *Anopheles* mosquitoes for malaria, *Aedes* for dengue, yellow fever and Zika, and *Culex* for Japanese encephalitis and West Nile fever.

**Only female mosquitoes bite, because they need a blood meal to produce eggs**, the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/mosquitoes/about/index.html) explains, while both sexes use the proboscis to feed on flower nectar and fruit juices.

A male could not bite you if he tried, because, in the [CDC's words](https://www.cdc.gov/mosquitoes/about/index.html), "the male's proboscis is not strong enough to pierce skin, and males do not feed on blood."

## The Arctic is where they might be missed most

Fang's feature suggested that losing mosquitoes might make the biggest ecological difference in the Arctic tundra. There, species such as *Aedes impiger* and *Aedes nigripes* hatch after the snow melts, and for a brief period, from northern Canada to Russia, they are so abundant that in some areas they form thick clouds.

The experts she interviewed did not agree on what losing them would mean. One entomologist estimated that the number of migratory birds nesting in the tundra could drop by **more than 50%** without mosquitoes to eat, while a [US Fish and Wildlife Service biologist in Fairbanks](https://web.archive.org/web/20101228225603/http://www.nature.com/news/2010/100721/full/466432a.html) countered that Arctic mosquitoes do not show up in bird stomach samples in high numbers, and that midges are a more important source of food.

## Elsewhere, most predators would find other food

Away from the tundra, the feature found plenty of animals that eat mosquitoes but little evidence that losing them would disrupt ecosystems. Without the larvae, Fang reported, hundreds of species of fish would have to change their diet, and many insects, spiders, salamanders, lizards and frogs would lose a primary food source.

In one study the feature cited, house martins in the Camargue in France produced on average **two chicks per nest** after their park was sprayed with a microbial mosquito-control agent, compared with three at control sites. Most mosquito-eating birds, the feature concluded, would probably switch to other insects, and it suggested bats might not miss mosquitoes at all, since [less than 2% of their gut content is mosquitoes](https://web.archive.org/web/20101228225603/http://www.nature.com/news/2010/100721/full/466432a.html).

That depends on the bat. A [2013 DNA study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3795000/) of Australian bats agreed that mosquitoes generally make up only a small proportion of bat diet, but it found them in the droppings of 55% of the small bats *Vespadelus vulturnus* it sampled, and judged that species likely to be the most sensitive to falls in mosquito numbers.

As larvae, Fang reported, mosquitoes make up substantial biomass in water from temporary ponds to tree holes and old tyres, where they eat decaying leaves, detritus and microorganisms. Whether other filter feeders would step in is the open question, and Illinois State University ecologist Steven Juliano expected they would: ["If you pop one rivet out of an airplane's wing, it's unlikely that the plane will cease to fly."](https://web.archive.org/web/20101228225603/http://www.nature.com/news/2010/100721/full/466432a.html)

The tiny pools inside pitcher plants may be an exception. In the pitchers of *Sarracenia purpurea*, the feature explained, the larvae of the mosquito *Wyeomyia smithii* feed on the waste left after midge larvae chew up drowned insects, which makes nitrogen available to the plant, so the plant's growth might be affected if the mosquito disappeared.

> In many cases, the scientists Nature asked expected the gap left by a missing mosquito to close quickly, as other organisms moved into its niche.

## Mosquitoes pollinate at least one orchid

Because adults run on nectar, mosquitoes visit flowers, and Fang's feature said that thousands of plant species would lose a group of pollinators without them. It also quoted a CDC entomologist who argued that mosquito pollination is not crucial for any crop people depend on.

A [2020 paper in PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6955360/) is more cautious about the wider claim: it notes that in most mosquito-plant interactions studied, mosquitoes contribute little to the plant's reproduction. For at least one wild plant, though, the role is measurable.

The same study found that snow mosquitoes, mainly *Aedes communis* and *Aedes increpitus*, pollinate the orchid *Platanthera obtusata* in northern Washington State, where pollinia, the orchid's pollen masses, stuck to the mosquitoes' eyes as they probed the flowers. Plants left open to visitors produced higher fruit-to-flower ratios and seed sets than plants bagged to keep pollinators out. In cage tests, the yellow fever mosquito *Aedes aegypti*, which is not native to the area, picked up pollinia the same way.

## Why do we need mosquitoes, if they carry so much disease?

We probably do not need the disease-carrying species. Fang's feature concluded that there seem to be few things mosquitoes do that other organisms cannot do just as well, with one possible exception: they move blood, and the microbes in it, from one animal to the next. Its summary asks whether eradicating any organism would have serious consequences for ecosystems, and answers ["Not when it comes to mosquitoes."](https://www.nature.com/articles/466432a)

The case against wiping them out is mostly practical. The same feature reported that the work of Phil Lounibos, an ecologist at the Florida Medical Entomology Laboratory, suggests that eradicating one vector species would be futile, because another would quickly fill its niche. His team found that some female yellow fever mosquitoes collected from Florida scrap yards had been inseminated, and so sterilized, by Asian tiger mosquitoes, which carry diseases of their own.

The tools are also new. In a [2018 study in *Nature Biotechnology*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6871539/), a CRISPR gene drive aimed at the doublesex gene of the malaria mosquito *Anopheles gambiae* spread through caged populations, reaching **100% prevalence within 7-11 generations** and cutting egg production until the populations collapsed. Those were laboratory cages, and a cage result says nothing yet about what removing a species would do in the wild.

## The answer depends on which mosquitoes you mean

The group as a whole, with its thousands of nectar-drinking species, feeds fish, frogs and birds, helps feed at least one pitcher plant and pollinates at least one orchid. The few species that spread malaria and dengue are another matter, and dengue alone causes an estimated [40,000 deaths every year](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/vector-borne-diseases) on top of the malaria toll.

Near its end, Fang's feature made a blunt point: what protects mosquitoes is the limits of the methods for killing them, rather than any lack of human will. A gene drive that works in the wild would remove that protection for the species it targets, which makes the ecological question worth settling before anyone can press the button. Strictly FYI.
