The FDA's case for stopping stevia leaves at the border is one sentence in Import Alert 45-06, under the heading "Charge." It says each shipment is subject to refusal because it ["appears to bear or contain an unsafe food additive within the meaning of section 409."](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cms_ia/importalert_119.html) The goods it covers are stevia leaves, crude extracts of the leaves and foods that contain them.

The leaf it targets is the one people in Paraguay and Brazil have used [to sweeten bitter herbal teas including mate](https://www.henriettes-herb.com/faqs/medi-2-6-stevia.html) for hundreds of years, according to the Herb Research Foundation, which added that "Japanese consumers by the millions" had used extracts of the same plant for nearly 20 years. In plain terms, the FDA had never approved the leaf as a food additive, and it found the safety studies too thin, and too troubling, to change that. The alert has been revised since it first went out, and it is still in force.

Each turn in the story has a document behind it:

| Date | Document | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| 1984 | FDA seizure action against Sunrider | Seized one company's stevia products |
| May 1991 | FDA import alert | Kept stevia out of food at the border |
| 1994 | Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act | Opened a route outside the additive rules |
| 1995 | Revised import alert | Let stevia in as a supplement ingredient |
| June 1999 | EU food committee opinion | Rejected stevioside as a sweetener |
| December 17, 2008 | FDA letters on two GRAS notices | Raised no questions on purified rebaudioside A |
| November 11, 2011 | EU Regulation 1131/2011 | Authorized steviol glycosides in food |

## The FDA stopped stevia because its safety file was thin

**In 1991**, the FDA [banned the importation of stevia and its use in foods](https://www.csmonitor.com/1999/0901/p2s1.html), the Christian Science Monitor reported, saying it had questions about its safety. The agency treated the leaf as an unapproved food additive, and an unapproved additive cannot go into food.

It was not the agency's first move. **In 1984**, the FDA later wrote, it [initiated a judicial seizure action against various stevia products marketed by Sunrider](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20090116124228/http:/www.fda.gov/ohrms/dockets/dockets/95s0316/m000002.pdf) on the grounds that they contained an unapproved food additive, and Sunrider entered a consent decree that barred it from selling products containing stevia.

The Herb Research Foundation, which campaigned for stevia, dated the 1991 step more closely: in **May 1991**, it wrote, the [FDA acted by imposing an import alert](https://www.henriettes-herb.com/faqs/medi-2-6-stevia.html) and began formally warning companies to stop using the herb.

The original order, the Monitor reported, pointed to some studies raising questions about the herb's impact on blood sugar levels and fertility. George Pauli, director of the FDA's division of product policy, [told the paper in 1999 that "the tests weren't very good."](https://www.csmonitor.com/1999/0901/p2s1.html) He added that the agency had expected people to consume a lot of it.

> "It was really the lack of studies that raised alarms." - George Pauli of the FDA, speaking to the Christian Science Monitor in 1999

The agency's worry went beyond the gap. In a 1995 letter to a lawyer for Sunrider, it warned of ["the agency's continued concern over the safety of stevia,"](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20090116124228/http:/www.fda.gov/ohrms/dockets/dockets/95s0316/m000002.pdf) citing published studies suggesting that stevia extracts reduced the fertility of female laboratory animals and others on a possible low-blood-sugar effect in people. "These concerns are currently unresolved," it wrote.

## The NutraSweet story rests on a secondhand remark and a masked file

The popular story says the makers of NutraSweet, the aspartame sweetener then owned by Monsanto, had stevia banned to protect their market. One early written form of it comes from the Herb Research Foundation's Rob McCaleb, who wrote that FDA inspectors began visiting stevia sellers around 1987 and that one inspector [reportedly told a company president](https://www.henriettes-herb.com/faqs/medi-2-6-stevia.html) the agency wanted people to stop using stevia "because Nutra Sweet complained to FDA." The foundation then asked the FDA, under the Freedom of Information Act, for records of contacts between NutraSweet and the agency about stevia, and McCaleb wrote that the identity of the company that prompted the FDA action came back masked.

Both accused parties denied it. The FDA [adamantly denies such assertions](https://www.csmonitor.com/1999/0901/p2s1.html), the Monitor reported, and Etienne Veber of Monsanto's NutraSweet division told the paper the barriers to a new additive were "not from Monsanto, it's just the way the FDA works." A reported remark, a masked file and two denials leave the story where it started: unproven.

## A 1994 law let the leaf back in as a supplement

The way back opened in Congress. In 1994 it passed the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act, and the Herb Research Foundation wrote that the new law [may prevent the FDA from treating stevia and other natural herbs as "food additives."](https://www.henriettes-herb.com/faqs/medi-2-6-stevia.html) In **1995**, FDA staff later wrote, the agency [revised its import alert on stevia leaves and extracts](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30268795/) to allow their use as dietary ingredients in dietary supplements.

The split still governs the leaf. Under the current alert, stevia leaves or crude extracts used in a supplement as a sweetener or flavoring "AND are labeled as such" [are "considered unsafe food additives"](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cms_ia/importalert_119.html) and can be detained. Labeled as a plain dietary ingredient, the same leaf is not detained under the alert, unless its shipper is on the alert's Red List.

The same line once reached a bookshelf. In March 1998, an FDA warning letter told Stevita that the books it stocked, among them James Kirkland's Cooking with Stevia, and its website "rendered the product adulterated." The agency's Dallas office had already [detained a shipment of stevia bound for the company](https://stopthedrugwar.org/chronicle-old/048/bookburning.shtml), which Oscar Rodes ran, that February.

On May 19, 1998, an FDA fax said "an investigator of this office" would be ["available to witness the destruction of the cookbooks, literature and other publications for the purpose of verifying compliance."](https://stopthedrugwar.org/chronicle-old/048/bookburning.shtml) Rodes refused to destroy them, and the investigators who came to his offices in Arlington, Texas, [marked six books so they couldn't be sold](https://www.csmonitor.com/1999/0901/p2s1.html) and left, the Monitor reported the next year.

## The FDA raised no questions about purified extracts in 2008

Two companies filed notices with the FDA in May 2008 saying that rebaudioside A, a purified sweet compound from the leaf, was generally recognized as safe (GRAS). Cargill's notice covered its use [as a general-purpose sweetener in foods, excluding meat and poultry products](https://www.hfpappexternal.fda.gov/scripts/fdcc/index.cfm?set=GRASNotices&id=253), and the Whole Earth Sweetener Company's covered its use [as a sweetener in a variety of food categories, and as a table top sweetener](https://www.hfpappexternal.fda.gov/scripts/fdcc/index.cfm?set=GRASNotices&id=252). On **December 17, 2008**, the FDA closed both with the same answer: "FDA has no questions."

Those compounds, the steviol glycosides, are reported to be [**200 to 400 times** sweeter than table sugar](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/aspartame-and-other-sweeteners-food), the FDA says. Its staff wrote in 2018 that, because of the import alert, the agency had since evaluated [more than 50 GRAS notices](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30268795/) for high-purity steviol glycosides as sweeteners.

The leaf never followed. The FDA's sweeteners page states that ["the use of stevia leaf and crude stevia extracts is not considered GRAS, and their import into the U.S. is not permitted for use as sweeteners."](https://www.fda.gov/food/food-additives-petitions/aspartame-and-other-sweeteners-food) The import alert now exempts, with documentation, extracts of **at least 95% steviol glycosides**, and it still holds that the leaf is [not considered GRAS "due to inadequate toxicological information."](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cms_ia/importalert_119.html)

## Europe said no in 1999 and yes in 2011

Europe took longer. The European Commission's Scientific Committee for Food had turned stevioside down in 1985 and 1989, and in an [opinion adopted on 17 June 1999](https://food.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2020-12/sci-com_scf_out34_en.pdf) it raised questions about the specifications of the extracts tested, the chronic toxicity and carcinogenicity studies and possible effects on male fertility. It added that steviol, a metabolite of stevioside produced by the human microflora, "is genotoxic and induces developmental toxicity," and repeated "that the substance is not acceptable as a sweetener on the presently available data."

Twelve years later the answer changed. [Commission Regulation (EU) No 1131/2011](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2011/1131/oj) of 11 November 2011 added steviol glycosides to the EU's list of permitted food additives. Its preamble records that the European Food Safety Authority had [evaluated their safety in an opinion of 10 March 2010](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2011/1131/oj) and set an acceptable daily intake of **4 mg per kilogram of body weight**, expressed as steviol equivalents.

So stevia was banned as an unapproved food additive, and the leaf, sold as a sweetener, still is. A purified extract of at least 95% steviol glycosides now clears the border with its paperwork, while the leaf it is refined from can still be held there under the [same alert](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cms_ia/importalert_119.html), and the FDA does not permit it as a sweetener in food sold inside the country either.
