Stealing the wine was easy for Jean-Jacques Rousseau; the bread was the hard part. The wine, a slightly cloudy white from Arbois, belonged to Jean Bonnot de Mably, provost of the mounted police in Lyon, who [hired Rousseau in April 1740](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel_Bonnot_de_Mably) to tutor his two eldest sons. Rousseau boasted he could clear it, spoiled only its looks, and began taking bottles to his room.

He could never drink without eating. Sending a footman for bread would give him away, and a gentleman with a sword at his side could not be seen buying a loaf. So, [he writes in the Confessions](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Les_Confessions_%28Rousseau%29/Livre_VI), he remembered the makeshift of a great princess on being told that the peasants had no bread: **"Qu'ils mangent de la brioche."** He bought brioche, though on some outings he crossed the whole town and walked past thirty pastry cooks before he dared go into one.

## What does 'let them eat cake' mean?

"Let them eat cake" means a callous dismissal of hardship by someone too comfortable to understand it, and is now quoted against anyone powerful who seems out of touch. The line is pinned on **Marie Antoinette**, but there is no evidence she said it, and by Rousseau's account it predates her birth.

## Rousseau's scene is fifteen years older than the queen

Rousseau stayed in the Mably house about a year, [by his own count](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Les_Confessions_%28Rousseau%29/Livre_VI), some fifteen years before Marie Antoinette was [born in Vienna on 2 November 1755](https://en.chateauversailles.fr/discover/history/great-characters/marie-antoinette).

He began Books I to VI of the Confessions [in 1765](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ahrf_0003-4436_2002_num_327_1_2564?pageId=T1_39), while she was still a child in Austria, and they were [published in **1782**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confessions_(Rousseau)), four years after his death.

## Alphonse Karr printed the rumour and doubted it

In a [2002 study](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ahrf_0003-4436_2002_num_327_1_2564?pageId=T1_30) in the Annales historiques de la Revolution francaise, the scholars **Veronique Campion-Vincent and Christine Shojaei Kawan** traced the saying's career. They found no written link to the queen before [1843, fifty years after her death](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ahrf_0003-4436_2002_num_327_1_2564?pageId=T1_38), when the journalist Alphonse Karr presented it as a rumour her enemies had spread.

Karr had in fact raised it about a year and a half before that. In **November 1841** his satirical monthly, [Les Guepes](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/42798), recalled "le mot vrai ou faux qu'on rapporte de Marie-Antoinette", the saying, true or false, that is told of her. His target was the Journal des Debats, whose remedy for the misery of workers was that they should put money in the savings bank.

In the 1843 passage he added that he had lately come across [a book dated 1760](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/42798) telling the same saying of a duchess of Tuscany. That seemed to him to prove, more or less, that the queen never said it and that it had been found again and "mis en circulation contre elle", put into circulation against her. He did not say which book.

## Her brother-in-law told it about another queen

On the night of **20 June 1791**, as the royal family made its failed run for Varennes, Louis XVI's brother, the Comte de Provence, [escaped by another route](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_to_Varennes). He later reigned as Louis XVIII, and his account of the journey, [published in Paris in 1823](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ahrf_0003-4436_2002_num_327_1_2564?pageId=T1_40), describes a stop at the isolated post house of Vaurains, between Soissons and Laon.

His companion d'Avaray would not let him step down to stretch his legs, so he proposed they eat. They had a meat pie and some Bordeaux but had forgotten the bread, and eating the crust with the pie, [he writes](https://archive.org/details/relationdunvoyag00loui), they thought of Queen Marie-Therese, who once answered pity for poor people with no bread: "Mais, mon Dieu, que ne mangent-ils de la croute de pate?" Why, good heavens, don't they eat the pie crust?

That queen was Louis XIV's wife, a Spanish Habsburg [born at the Escurial in 1638 and dead since 1683](https://en.chateauversailles.fr/discover/history/great-characters/maria-theresa-austria), whom the French called Marie-Therese d'Autriche. She was not Marie Antoinette's mother, the Empress Maria Theresa, though the [2002 study](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ahrf_0003-4436_2002_num_327_1_2564?pageId=T1_40) suggests his choice of queen may owe something to an analogy between two queens of France from the House of Austria, Marie-Therese and Marie Antoinette. The king's own brother, running from the Revolution, told the joke about a queen dead for more than a century, and his sister-in-law is nowhere in it.

By the time the account was published, though, Marie Antoinette had become a royalist martyr. The study suggests he could no longer pin an unkind remark on her, and notes that as Comte de Provence he had himself helped circulate scandalous songs and pamphlets about her.

## China tells the same story about an emperor

The [Book of Jin](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E6%99%89%E6%9B%B8/%E5%8D%B7004), the official history of China's Jin dynasty, [compiled in 648](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Jin), says that when the empire fell into famine and chaos and the common people were starving to death, [Emperor Hui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Hui_of_Jin), who reigned from 290 to 307, asked why they did not eat meat porridge. His obliviousness, the chronicler adds, was all of this kind.

The same annal tells how, hearing frogs croak in the Hualin park, he asked his attendants whether the croakers were official or private, and was told that those on government land were official and those on private land private.

The plot even has a catalogue number. Folklorists file it as [tale type 1446](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ahrf_0003-4436_2002_num_327_1_2564?pageId=T1_30) in the Aarne-Thompson classification, a type named after the cake line itself, although Stith Thompson created the type only in his 1961 revision and none of the texts he listed as examples involves Marie Antoinette or any other queen.

The 2002 study finds versions in [sixteenth-century German texts](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ahrf_0003-4436_2002_num_327_1_2564?pageId=T1_34), including a joke collection by Martin Montanus, where a noblewoman wonders why the starving do not eat bread and cheese.

|---|---|---|---|
| Book of Jin | 648, of a reign from 290 to 307 | Emperor Hui | Meat porridge |
| An unnamed book, per Karr | 1760 | A duchess of Tuscany | Brioche, the same saying |

## The cake is a translation, and the brioche law is unsourced

Rousseau wrote brioche, which [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/brioche) defines as a "light slightly sweet bread made with a rich yeast dough" and first records in English in 1826. Cake is the English tradition, and even one translation cannot settle on a word: the 1903 edition on [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3913), which follows an unsigned English [translation printed in 1790](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_the-confessions-of-j-j_rousseau-jean-jacques_1790_2), renders the princess's line "Then let them eat pastry!" and, a few lines later, calls Rousseau's purchase "the dear little cake".

A rescue story for the queen also circulates. French law supposedly made bakers sell brioche at the price of plain bread whenever plain bread ran out, which would turn the line into sensible advice.

The idea is not new: in 1934 the novelist Henry de Montherlant suggested that Marie Antoinette perhaps simply believed brioche cost the same as bread, [the 2002 study notes](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ahrf_0003-4436_2002_num_327_1_2564?pageId=T1_47). [CulinaryLore](https://culinarylore.com/food-history:did-marie-antoinette-say-let-them-eat-cake/) now states that "by law, they were required to sell the more expensive brioche at the same price", but it names no ordinance and cites nothing for the brioche rule itself, which leaves the story unsourced.

## The pamphlets that hated her never used the line

By the Revolution, the [Palace of Versailles](https://en.chateauversailles.fr/discover/history/great-characters/marie-antoinette) records, the queen had become "the target of pamphlets, libels and caricatures", her spending "scrutinised and often exaggerated".

> The pamphlets of her own lifetime accused Marie Antoinette of drunkenness, indecency, orgies and incest. Not one line in them, Campion-Vincent and Shojaei Kawan found, gives her the brioche remark.

[Historians of the press of the Revolution](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ahrf_0003-4436_2002_num_327_1_2564?pageId=T1_36) whom Campion-Vincent consulted knew of no brioche story in their sources either, and a survey of French primary-school textbooks from 1833 to 1919 turned up nothing, [the study reports](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ahrf_0003-4436_2002_num_327_1_2564?pageId=T1_37).

Her biographer **Antonia Fraser**, [speaking to NPR in 2006](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/6095949), called it "a royal chestnut" and explained why it attached itself to her anyway: "I think why it stuck to Marie Antoinette is because it confirmed people's prejudices."

Take the queen out and the line goes back to what it was in the Confessions, a joke Rousseau told against himself. Short of bread and too proud to be seen buying it, he followed the princess's advice to the letter and bought brioche. Strictly FYI.
