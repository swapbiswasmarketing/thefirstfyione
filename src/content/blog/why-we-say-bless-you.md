Someone sneezes across the room. A stranger you have never spoken to says "bless you," and you say "thank you," and neither of you has any idea why. It is one of the few automatic rituals left in ordinary life, performed by people who share no religion, no language of origin, and no belief that anything is being blessed.

## Why do we say bless you when someone sneezes?

We say it because people were answering sneezes long before anyone said "bless you," and English filled that old habit with a Christian blessing. Greek soldiers [bowed at a sneeze, taking it as a sign from Zeus](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1170), Romans [saluted a person who sneezed](https://archive.org/details/naturalhistoryof05plinrich), and by the Middle Ages Christians were saying ["God help you"](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/basis/goldenlegend/GoldenLegend-Volume1.asp) instead.

The famous story that Pope Gregory the Great ordered the blessing during a plague is a later legend, and [the contemporary account of that plague](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/basis/gregory-hist.asp) never mentions a sneeze.

## The plague decree, which no witness recorded

The version everyone has heard: during a deadly plague in Rome, **Pope Gregory I** decreed that anyone who sneezed should be blessed at once, because a sneeze was an early sign of the disease. Wikipedia calls it [the most widely repeated origin story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Responses_to_sneezing) for the English phrase, and NPR's health blog has repeated it, saying that because sneezing was a first sign of the plague, Gregory ["commanded people to say 'God Bless You' after a sneeze."](https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2016/08/24/491082028/heres-looking-achoo-debunking-the-sneeze)

The plague and the pope are both real. Gregory [was elected by acclamation to succeed Pelagius II in 590](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Gregory_I), after Pelagius died of the disease.

There is even a witness. Gregory, bishop of Tours, whose [History of the Franks comes down to the year 591](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/basis/gregory-hist.asp), recorded what his own deacon saw in Rome: a plague called inguinaria, a disease of the groin, killed Pelagius, and Gregory ordered the clergy to sing psalms for three days while choirs crossed the city crying "Kyrie eleison." The deacon reported that in a single hour of prayer **"eighty fell to the ground and died."**

Neither that translation nor [the Latin text of the chapter](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/gregorytours/gregorytours10.shtml) says a word about sneezing.

The sneeze arrives centuries later, in the Golden Legend, a collection of saints' lives whose [manuscripts go back as far as 1265](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Legend). Its chapter on the litanies, in a 1900 edition of [William Caxton's English translation of 1483](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/basis/goldenlegend/GoldenLegend-Volume1.asp), says the plague killed people in the middle of a sneeze, "so that when any person was heard sneezing anon they that were by said to him: God help you, or Christ help: and yet endureth the custom."

Even that version has no papal decree about sneezing in it: Gregory institutes the procession, and the bystanders say the words on their own. The decree belongs to the retellings, attached to a story that already looks assembled backwards from a custom that needed explaining.

The decree cannot be where the custom began. People were answering sneezes with a bow or a salute centuries before there was a pope to decree anything.

## The soul-escaping story, which is folklore

The second explanation: people once believed a sneeze could expel your soul, or let a demon in, and the blessing sealed the gap. A companion belief held that [the heart stopped for a moment during a sneeze](https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/bless-you/), which it does not.

Explanations like these are easy to find and impossible to pin down. Surveying them, [Snopes concluded](https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/bless-you/) that "nothing points to any one of them being its origin."

The ancient sources run the logic the other way round. As the Princeton classicist [Elaine Fantham told NPR](https://www.npr.org/2006/05/27/5435812/ancient-sneezing-a-gift-from-the-gods), in the old stories "the blessing was the cause and the sneeze was the result": a god who favoured you made you sneeze, and the sneeze was the proof.

## The ancient record starts with Homer

[Fantham traced the earliest sneeze story](https://www.npr.org/2006/05/27/5435812/ancient-sneezing-a-gift-from-the-gods) to the Odyssey. In [Samuel Butler's translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1727), Penelope is talking about revenge on the suitors when her son Telemachus sneezes so loudly the whole house rings, and she laughs, taking it as a sign that "all the suitors are going to be killed."

The scene in Xenophon's *Anabasis* is more public. The Ten Thousand were Greek mercenaries hired by Cyrus the Younger for [his 401 BC bid for the Persian throne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anabasis_(Xenophon)). Cyrus was killed at Cunaxa, leaving the army stranded deep in Persia, and then their senior officers were [killed or captured by treachery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anabasis_(Xenophon)).

Xenophon was telling them they still had "many a bright hope of safety" when someone sneezed, and in [Henry Graham Dakyns's translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1170), "with one impulse the soldiers bowed in worship." Xenophon moved on the spot that they vow thank-offerings to Zeus the Saviour, "since, even as we spoke of safety, an omen from Zeus the Saviour has appeared," and every hand went up.

Roman manners made it routine. Pliny the Elder, who left most of his Natural History unrevised at [his death in the AD 79 eruption of Vesuvius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_History_(Pliny)), asks in a run of questions about everyday customs why people salute a sneezer, ["an observance which Tiberius Caesar, they say, the most unsociable of men, as we all know, used to exact,"](https://archive.org/details/naturalhistoryof05plinrich) even when riding in his chariot. The same chapter adds that if someone sneezes while a dish is being cleared, bringing it back without tasting it is ["considered highly ominous."](https://archive.org/details/naturalhistoryof05plinrich)

Why a sneeze? Asked why sneezing was a good omen, Fantham said it was seen as ["something humans couldn't engineer"](https://www.npr.org/2006/05/27/5435812/ancient-sneezing-a-gift-from-the-gods), spontaneous and out of the sneezer's control, so it had to come from outside.

> In the ancient stories the gods did the blessing first and sent the sneeze to show it. Now the sneeze comes first, and the blessing follows.

Today, in most non-English-speaking cultures with a customary response, [the phrase is a wish for health or a long life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Responses_to_sneezing), like the German **Gesundheit**, "health," which some Americans and Canadians borrow, while in the religious exchange used in Arabic the sneezer says "All praise is for God" and the listener answers "God have mercy on you."

Not everyone joins in. In Japanese, Korean and Mandarin Chinese it is [not customary to acknowledge another person's sneeze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Responses_to_sneezing), and the sneezer apologises instead.

## Your heart keeps beating, and your eyes stay put

The folklore attached itself to the biology as well. A sneeze is a reflex, usually [set off by chemical or mechanical irritation of the lining of the nose and carried by the trigeminal nerve](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12098519/) to a sneeze-evoking region of the brainstem.

**Your heart does not stop.** Nancy Sweitzer, then chief of cardiology at the University of Arizona's Sarver Heart Center, [told NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2016/08/24/491082028/heres-looking-achoo-debunking-the-sneeze) that the pressure in the chest during a sneeze reduces blood flow into the heart, which can make one beat weaker and feel skipped, while "the electrical activity of the heart is constant through a sneeze."

**Your eyes will not pop out.** A medically reviewed Healthline explainer says sneezing includes [an autonomic reflex that closes your eyes](https://www.healthline.com/health/can-you-sneeze-with-your-eyes-open), that you can sneeze with them open if you work at it, and that the popping-eyeballs story is a "schoolyard legend."

So "bless you" is a Christian phrase laid over a habit at least as old as Homer. The plague story explains the English wording at best, and the soul story tells us what people guessed.

What survives is the smallest possible social contract: your body did something startling, I noticed, and I wished you well about it. Strictly FYI.
