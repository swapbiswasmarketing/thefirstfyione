**Two undressed calf or goat skins, hung at the pommel of the saddle.** That is how [a traveller](https://archive.org/details/asketchcustomsa00penngoog), writing from Mexico City in July **1824**, described the leg protection on a Mexican saddle, and the working word is *hung*. The skins were part of the horse's tack. Until it rained, nobody was wearing them.

Almost everything odd about modern chaps is a leftover of that arrangement.

## Why do cowboys wear chaps?

Chaps are leather leg coverings worn over ordinary trousers, and they do three jobs at once:

- **Brush.** The name is the job. *Chaparreras* were "leather overalls worn to protect riders' legs from the *chaparro*," the evergreen oak, according to the [Online Etymology Dictionary](https://www.etymonline.com/word/chaps).
- **Rain.** The rain version had a name of its own, *armas de agua*, [spread over the legs of the rider](https://archive.org/details/asketchcustomsa00penngoog) when the weather turned. Cowboy chaps kept the job: with an oilskin slicker, Theodore Roosevelt wrote in [Hunting Trips of a Ranchman](https://archive.org/details/huntingtripsara01roosgoog), "a pair of shaps keep out the rain almost completely."
- **Cold.** Close-fitting styles [trap body heat](https://americancowboy.com/lifestyle/types-chaps-28214/), and woolies, [in one Wyoming ranch writer's words](https://cavvysavvy.tsln.com/blog/chaps-hinder-less/), are "often busted out in cold temps through northern states."

## The first version hung off the horse

[*A Sketch of the Customs and Society of Mexico*](https://archive.org/details/asketchcustomsa00penngoog), published in London in **1828** from letters and a travel journal of 1824 to 1826, describes a Mexican saddle down to the lion's head in brass or silver sometimes set on the pommel and the case of sable, black bear-skin or stout leather that covered the seat and spread over the horse's back. Then it reaches what hangs off it.

> "At the pummel, the armas de agua, two undressed calf or goat skins, are hung; these are kept there, because the dress would not be considered complete without them, but are of use only when it rains; they are then spread over the legs of the rider, and are a good defence from a passing shower, but an hour's heavy rain would wet them through."

The very next sentence records what else rode on that pommel, [a sabre](https://archive.org/details/asketchcustomsa00penngoog), and the author's own footnote translates *armas de agua* as water arms. At that date the job of keeping a rider dry belonged to the saddle, which carried the skins until the rain came.

Later writers filled in the mechanics. Leon Coindet, a French army doctor who had served in Mexico, [described the armas](https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_35476) as enormous skins, usually calf, fixed to the pommel by one corner and tied to the rider's belt behind, so that legs and feet both stayed dry. Joaquin Garcia Icazbalceta's [Vocabulario de mexicanismos](https://archive.org/details/vocabulariodeme00icazgoog) defines them as two large, nearly square goatskins with the hair on, fastened to the saddle or tied at the rider's waist, spread on the ground as a bed when needed, and sometimes richly decorated.

Body-worn *chaparreras* replaced them. Coindet [called the new garment](https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_35476) an improvement on the armas de agua, and Garcia Icazbalceta [recorded the switch](https://archive.org/details/vocabulariodeme00icazgoog) with a caveat: chaparreras could neither cover the foot nor double as a bed. What they could do was go wherever the rider went.

## Ranch country says shaps

In **1885**, Theodore Roosevelt published [his sketches of sport on the northern cattle plains](https://archive.org/details/huntingtripsara01roosgoog) and inventoried the cowboy's dress: "a broad felt hat, a flannel shirt, with a bright silk handkerchief loosely knotted round the neck, trousers tucked into high-heeled boots, and a pair of leather 'shaps' (chaperajos) or heavy riding overalls." He spelled the spoken form first and put the Spanish in brackets, which tells you which one people said out loud. A ranchman or foreman, [he added](https://archive.org/details/huntingtripsara01roosgoog), dressed the same way in finer materials, down to "the shaps of seal-skin."

Merriam-Webster still sides with him: it [lists *shaps* ahead of *chaps*](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/chaps) as the pronunciation, though the [American Heritage Dictionary](https://ahdictionary.com/word/search.html?q=chaps) puts *chaps* first. Ranch country has not changed its mind. In **2018**, Savanna Simmons, who lives on a ranch north of Lusk, Wyoming, [was blunt about it](https://cavvysavvy.tsln.com/blog/chaps-hinder-less/): "Also, they're pronounced SHaps."

The Spanish underneath points to the brush. [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/chaps) dates English *chaps* to **1844** as a clipping of *chaparejos*, from *chaparreras*, and ties the root to *chaparro*, the oak that also gives us *chaparral*. The Real Academia Espanola agrees: its dictionary builds [chaparreras](https://dle.rae.es/chaparreras) from *chaparro* plus the suffix *-era*, and defines a [chaparro](https://dle.rae.es/chaparro) as a low clump of holm oak or oak with many branches. Coindet [read the name the same way](https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_35476), writing that the garment served above all, as its name indicates, to guard the legs in the thorny thickets of the chaparral.

## The styles track the weather, not the look

| Style | When | Cut | Built for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shotgun | peaked in the 1870s | narrow, closed the full length of the leg | holding in heat and keeping out rain and snow |
| Woolies | the late 1880s | angora fleece or hair-on hide, lined with canvas | Great Plains winters north of the Texas border |
| Batwing | popular after the open range closed | wide, buckled only around the upper leg | air circulation and freedom of movement, once more of the work was done on foot |

Shotguns were used by range-working Texas cowboys and were the most common chap in the West in the 1870s, by [American Cowboy magazine's account](https://americancowboy.com/lifestyle/types-chaps-28214/). When cowboys began competing at rodeo roundups in the late 1800s, a [loan record at the Bullock Texas State History Museum](https://www.thestoryoftexas.com/artifacts/mens-shotgun-chaps/) notes, they wore the same narrow-legged shotguns they used on the range. Chinks, the half-length version that stops two to four inches below the knee, are the fourth style, [worn by the vaqueros](https://americancowboy.com/lifestyle/types-chaps-28214/) of California and the Southwest and still popular in the Southwestern states.

## All chaps are already assless

The internet treats "assless chaps" as a separate novelty garment. It describes every pair ever made. Chaps are, by construction, [two separate legs](https://archive.org/details/vocabulariodeme00icazgoog) fastened to the waist with straps or a belt, with no seat between them. [Simmons puts it in ranch terms](https://cavvysavvy.tsln.com/blog/chaps-hinder-less/): "All chaps are 'hinder'less. If they have a hinder in them, they're just leather pants."

The older Mexican design was odder still. The chaparreras Coindet saw were [hide leggings with the hair left on](https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_35476), buttoned up the side and rising to the waist only at the front, where a belt held them; the common pairs were calfskin with copper buttons, and city dandies wore ocelot with silver or gold.

The open back fits that ancestry. A skin hanging off a pommel never needed a seat, and the two-legged replacement did not grow one, because it went over trousers the rider already had on.

The rodeo silhouette gets read backwards in the same way. Batwings look like Wild West show costume, but the wide cut that [buckles only around the upper leg](https://americancowboy.com/lifestyle/types-chaps-28214/) was for air circulation and freedom of movement, which mattered more once the open range closed and cowboys did more of their work on foot.

## By about 1875 they had travelled well past the vaqueros

The National Cowboy and Western Heritage Museum [catalogues a pair of leather chaps](https://nationalcowboymuseum.org/collections/objects/chaps/) made on the Northern Plains and attributed to Crow makers, dated **around 1875**, on display in its Native American Gallery. Some thirty years after English first borrowed the word, a pair had been made in Crow country, a long way north of the Mexican brush country where the word began.

Cowboys wear chaps for the brush, the rain and the cold, but the shape they wear was settled before any of them chose it. The garment began as a pair of saddle skins, moved onto two belted legs, and kept the open back that came with them. The pair on a bull rider tonight is a rain flap that got off the horse and never went back. Strictly FYI.
