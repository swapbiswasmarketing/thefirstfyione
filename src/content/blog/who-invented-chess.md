The wheat that the legendary inventor of chess asked for as his reward comes to **18,446,744,073,709,551,615 grains**: one grain on the first square, doubled on each square to the last, by the count [Harold Murray gives](https://archive.org/details/AHistoryOfChessHJRMurray/page/n237/mode/2up) in his 1913 A History of Chess. Nobody knows who invented chess, and the sage of that story, Sissa, is a legend.

Every other inventor the old books name is a legend too or, like the chess master as-Suli, a real man credited by mistake. The game grew out of older Indian board games, and what can be dated is the trail it left behind.

## The game grew out of India, with no name attached

Bo Utas, in his history of chess in Persia for Encyclopaedia Iranica, writes that "although its origin is wrapped in obscurity," the game's name, the organization of its pieces and the earliest Persian traditions suggest it ["developed out of various ancient Indian board games"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/chess/chess-i-the-history-of-chess-in-persia/). Chess may have been known to Patanjali in the **2nd century BC**, he adds, but the first certain mention of chaturanga comes in the **6th century AD**.

The Sanskrit name is an adjective with the literal meaning ["having four limbs"](https://archive.org/details/AHistoryOfChessHJRMurray/page/n49/mode/2up), and Murray shows it became the regular epic name for the army, which was made up of four divisions. Utas lists them as elephants, horses, chariots and foot soldiers, led by the king and his counselor.

Murray's best guess at an inventor, a view he credited to the anthropologist Edward Tylor, was a person with no name: a player who found the board of an older game, the ashtapada, convenient and [adopted it as his field of battle](https://archive.org/details/AHistoryOfChessHJRMurray/page/n49/mode/2up).

> "The game of chess was invented when some Hindu devised a game of war." - [Harold Murray, A History of Chess](https://archive.org/details/AHistoryOfChessHJRMurray/page/n49/mode/2up)

## A clear early mention hides the game in a pun

Murray found that [allusions to chess begin to appear in Sanskrit literature with the seventh century](https://archive.org/details/AHistoryOfChessHJRMurray/page/n58/mode/2up), a century later than the date Utas gives. Bana, a poet of the early 7th century, wrote the Harshacharita, an account of King Harsha, in Murray's words the [supreme ruler of Northern India from 606 to 648](https://archive.org/details/AHistoryOfChessHJRMurray/page/n59/mode/2up).

Describing the peace of Harsha's kingdom, Bana says that under this king the only feet cut off are those in metre, and ["only ashtapadas teach the positions of the chaturanga."](https://archive.org/details/AHistoryOfChessHJRMurray/page/n59/mode/2up) The joke turns on the double meaning of chaturanga, the fourfold army and the game: in a kingdom at peace, armies take up their positions on game boards and nowhere else.

Murray thought the reference particularly clear. He also cites a slightly earlier one, first pointed out by the Sanskrit scholar Frederick Thomas, in Subandhu's prose romance Vasavadatta, where ["the time of the rains played its game with frogs for chessmen,"](https://archive.org/details/AHistoryOfChessHJRMurray/page/n58/mode/2up) though that passage names neither the game nor the board.

## Persian writers credited India, and a poet made it a tragedy

Utas finds that chess was probably known in Persia by the end of the Sasanian period, but that the earliest certain evidence comes after the Islamic conquest. One Middle Persian tale, the Chatrang-namak, has a king of India named Dewasarm devise the game with **16 emerald and 16 ruby-red men**, to test the wisdom of the Persians, in the English version [Murray gives, working mainly from a German translation](https://archive.org/details/AHistoryOfChessHJRMurray/page/n170/mode/2up).

The Persian king of kings, Khosrow I, asked for 3 days, none of his wise men could work the game out, and then his minister Wajurgmitr explained the pieces as an army in battle and [won 12 games](https://archive.org/details/AHistoryOfChessHJRMurray/page/n170/mode/2up) against the Indian envoy. The minister then invented nard, or backgammon, and sent it to India as a riddle of his own, though Murray judged that nard's ["antiquity is undoubtedly very great"](https://archive.org/details/AHistoryOfChessHJRMurray/page/n172/mode/2up), with Chinese works recording its arrival from India in the 3rd century AD.

Utas calls the story legendary, a tale that ["cannot be accepted as an accurate historical account."](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/chess/chess-i-the-history-of-chess-in-persia/) He adds that a wall painting of the early 8th century at Pyandzhikent appears to show it: two men play a board game before a king, and the one dressed as an Indian makes the Persian gesture of surprise, a forefinger to his lips. Murray, writing in 1913, had already judged that [it is impossible to identify the Indian characters](https://archive.org/details/AHistoryOfChessHJRMurray/page/n173/mode/2up) with anyone who lived in Khosrow's time.

Later writers kept the Indian origin and added to it. Ferdowsi, who [died in 1020 or 1025](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ferdowsi-i/), retold the riddle story in the Shahnameh and added how the game was invented in India: two half-brothers, the princes Gav and Talkhand, fought over the throne, and Talkhand, ["trapped on the battlefield without possibility of either escape or attack"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/chess/chess-i-the-history-of-chess-in-persia/), died in his golden saddle. Gav invented chess to tell their mother how it happened.

That story, Utas argues, carries the meaning of checkmate, shah mat. The king in it is left powerless without being struck, so Utas reads mat as broken or paralyzed rather than dead.

## The wheat legend is folklore, but its sum is exact

Muslim authors attributed the invention to ["a variety of legendary wise men, usually Indian"](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/chess/chess-i-the-history-of-chess-in-persia/), and other stories name Hermes, or Mandodari, wife of the demon king Ravana in the Ramayana. Utas's list of names also includes one real man, the chess master as-Suli.

One of the legendary ones is Sissa ibn Dahir, the sage of the wheat story. The biographer Ibn Khallikan, [who died in 1282](https://archive.org/details/AHistoryOfChessHJRMurray/page/n230/mode/2up), wrote in his life of as-Suli that many people believed as-Suli had invented chess, ["but this is an erroneous opinion"](https://archive.org/details/india.history.resource.53346). The game, he went on, had been [devised by Sissah the Indian for a king named Shihram](https://archive.org/details/india.history.resource.53346), in the spelling of de Slane's English translation.

Offered any reward, Sissa asked for the doubled grain. The king's officials reported that all the wheat in the world [would be insufficient to make up the quantity](https://archive.org/details/india.history.resource.53346), and Ibn Khallikan admits he doubted the sum until an accountant at Alexandria worked it through for him, square by square. The 64th square alone, the accountant showed, would fill the corn stores of sixteen thousand three hundred and eighty-four cities.

Murray judged [the calculation Indian and possibly older than chess](https://archive.org/details/AHistoryOfChessHJRMurray/page/n237/mode/2up). As for the names, he concluded that the earliest teller of the legend [chose the Indian names that were most familiar to his generation](https://archive.org/details/AHistoryOfChessHJRMurray/page/n236/mode/2up) to make the story sound true.

## The modern queen makes her first recorded moves in a love poem

From the Muslim world the game travelled on to Europe, where, Utas notes, it was already known before the Crusades. In Islamic and early European chess, the historian [Ricardo Calvo explains](https://web.archive.org/web/20160130113355/http://history.chess.free.fr/papers/Calvo%201998.pdf), the queen, then called the alferza in Spanish, "could only reach an adjacent diagonal square," and a bishop could reach only **8 squares** in a whole game. European rules later gave her ["a privilege move allowed to the Queen for her first move in the game"](https://archive.org/details/AHistoryOfChessHJRMurray/page/n482/mode/2up), Murray notes.

The new rules of the late 15th century made the queen, ["originally far weaker than Rook or Knight"](https://archive.org/details/AHistoryOfChessHJRMurray/page/n814/mode/2up), **practically twice as strong as the rook**. Spaniards called the new game axedrez de la dama, chess of the queen, against the old axedrez del viejo, and Italians called it scacchi alla rabiosa, which Murray translates as ["mad chess"](https://archive.org/details/AHistoryOfChessHJRMurray/page/n814/mode/2up).

The earliest recorded game of modern chess is in a Catalan manuscript, Scachs d'amor, the chess of love, credited to three Valencian poets: Francesc de Castellvi plays as Mars, Narcis Vinyoles as Venus, and Bernat Fenollar referees as Mercury. "The MS bears no date," [Calvo writes](https://web.archive.org/web/20160130113355/http://history.chess.free.fr/papers/Calvo%201998.pdf), but the poets' careers point to a probable date between **about 1470 and 1490**, and in all probability before Francesch Vicent's chess book, printed at Valencia in 1495.

White's 21st move, the queen to d8, gives mate, and the stanzas add up to [64, as many as there are squares](https://web.archive.org/web/20160130113355/http://history.chess.free.fr/papers/Calvo%201998.pdf) on the board. The manuscript, [discovered in 1905](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scachs_d%27amor), was later lost, and a photograph of it is kept at the Library of Catalonia.

Every inventor chess was given before the 15th century is a legend or a mistake, and the queen's modern move has no inventor either, only a first appearance. Calvo calls Castellvi, the poet who played Mars, ["the first known winner of a game of modern chess"](https://web.archive.org/web/20160130113355/http://history.chess.free.fr/papers/Calvo%201998.pdf), and he won it in a love poem.
