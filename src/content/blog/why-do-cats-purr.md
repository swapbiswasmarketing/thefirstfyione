With no nerve signal reaching them and no muscle contracting, voice boxes taken from eight cats vibrated at [**25 to 30 cycles a second**](https://phaidra.vetmeduni.ac.at/o:3419), the pace of a purr, driven only by air blown through them.

These larynges came from pet cats put down for terminal illness at veterinary clinics in Vienna, with their owners' consent, and in the [experiment](https://phaidra.vetmeduni.ac.at/api/object/o:3419/download) each was mounted on a tube that supplied heated, humidified air, steadied by a support built from LEGO blocks. The result, published in *Current Biology* in 2023, challenged an explanation of purring that had stood for [half a century](https://www.newswise.com/articles/cats-purr-differently-than-previously-thought): a muscle twitch, timed by the brain, for every pulse.

## Why do cats purr?

Cats purr mainly to communicate. [Kittens purr while nursing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7000907/), adult cats purr when stroked or fed, and some also purr when frightened, injured or giving birth, so a purr is no proof of happiness. The sound comes from the larynx, which vibrates [25 to 30 times a second](https://phaidra.vetmeduni.ac.at/o:3419) as the cat breathes in and out.

Leslie Lyons, then at the veterinary school of the University of California, Davis, allowed in [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-do-cats-purr/) that it is tempting to say cats purr because they are happy, but judged it "more plausible that cat purring is a means of communication and a potential source of self-healing."

## A purr runs on the in-breath and the out-breath

A cat purrs with its mouth closed, "continuously during both the inspiration and expiration phases of respiration," according to a [2020 review of feline vocal communication](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7000907/) in the *Journal of Veterinary Science*. A [1991 study in the *Journal of Zoology*](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7998.1991.tb04749.x) recorded purring in pumas, cheetahs and house cats, measured the domestic cat at **26.3 cycles a second**, and found that a cat's purring frequency "does not change with age."

That rate is very low for a voice. Vocal fry, the creaky register of the human voice, is found below **70 cycles a second**, according to the [Vienna team's paper](https://phaidra.vetmeduni.ac.at/api/object/o:3419/download).

## For half a century, the beat was credited to the brain

"Purring is caused by extremely rapid twitching of the vocalis muscle," the muscle that runs inside the vocal folds, a [2002 study in the *Journal of Anatomy*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1570911/) stated. In that account, bursts of nerve-driven electrical activity in the larynx muscles, [typically 20 to 30 a second](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37794583/), make them open and close the airway.

The [1991 study](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7998.1991.tb04749.x) put the main mechanism down to "a centrally driven laryngeal modulation of respiratory flow," a beat set by the nervous system. Brain research pointed the same way: a 1936 paper in the *Journal of Comparative Neurology* was titled ["A purring center in the cat's brain"](https://doi.org/10.1002/cne.900640203).

## Eight larynges reached purring rates without a nervous system

Christian Herbst, a voice scientist at the University of Vienna, and seven colleagues tested [a plain prediction of that theory](https://phaidra.vetmeduni.ac.at/o:3419): that vibration "should be impossible without neuromuscular activity," and so impossible in a larynx removed from the body; instead, "all eight excised larynges produced self-sustained oscillations at typical cat purring rates."

In [six of the eight larynges](https://phaidra.vetmeduni.ac.at/api/object/o:3419/download), the pitch could slide from about **15 to 200 cycles a second**, joining the purr to the ranges of a cat's other calls.

Embedded in the vocal folds are pads of connective tissue [up to **4 mm** across](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37794583/), which probably make the inner edge of each fold far less stiff, so that it can vibrate slowly. Herbst said in the [university's press release](https://www.newswise.com/articles/cats-purr-differently-than-previously-thought) that the pad "may explain how such a small animal, weighing only a few kilograms, can regularly produce sounds at those incredibly low frequencies."

On this [evidence](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37794583/), a purr can be made the way a meow is made, by air flowing past vocal folds that vibrate on their own, the principle behind "most other vertebrate vocalizations" too. The team stops short of discarding the old theory: their [data](https://phaidra.vetmeduni.ac.at/o:3419) "do not fully reject" it. The paper shows the effect with air flowing outward, as in an out-breath, and proposes that in a living cat the brain-driven muscles and the passive vibration work together.

## Lions roar, house cats purr, and no cat is known to do both

In the traditional split, which goes back to the anatomist Richard Owen, the lion, tiger, jaguar and leopard have a hyoid that is not fully turned to bone, which "according to this conception, enables them to roar but not to purr," a [2010 acoustic study](https://roberteklund.info/pdf/Eklund_Peters_Duthie_2010=Purring_Cheetah_DomesticCat.pdf) co-written by Gustav Peters explains. In that scheme the other cats purr but cannot roar, yet the snow leopard, which has the roarers' kind of hyoid, purrs, the [same study](https://roberteklund.info/pdf/Eklund_Peters_Duthie_2010=Purring_Cheetah_DomesticCat.pdf) notes, and it has [never been heard to roar](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/why-margarita-can-purr-but-cant-roar). The [authors add](https://roberteklund.info/pdf/Eklund_Peters_Duthie_2010=Purring_Cheetah_DomesticCat.pdf) that there is "no well-founded and unequivocal basis" for the hyoid split.

The record on the big cats is thinner than the rule. In a review of purring, Peters tabulated that [20 of 36 species](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/why-margarita-can-purr-but-cant-roar) of cat have been said to purr, lions and leopards among them, National Geographic reported in 2012. Whether the big cats' purr-like sounds are true purrs "or are actually different noises that only vaguely sound like purrs" was then [still open](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/why-margarita-can-purr-but-cant-roar), and the article summed up Peters' view that they "might have the ability to purr but simply don't." By the definition in Peters' review, a true purr alternates between out-breath and in-breath, the [2010 study](https://roberteklund.info/pdf/Eklund_Peters_Duthie_2010=Purring_Cheetah_DomesticCat.pdf) explains, as its cheetah and house cat did.

The [2002 anatomy study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1570911/) tentatively judged the elastic hyoid to have "no direct effect on the ability to purr" and pointed to the roaring cats' vocal folds instead: "A large flexible pad like that seen in the vocal folds of the Pantherinae would tend to damp such twitchings and thus make it difficult, if not impossible, to purr." In the newer account, a pad is part of how a house cat purrs, and the [Vienna paper](https://phaidra.vetmeduni.ac.at/api/object/o:3419/download) calls the house cat's pads "functionally analogous to what has been found in roaring cats."

| Snow leopard | Never heard to | Yes |
| Cheetah | No | Yes, breathing in and out |

## A purr can come from pain as well as pleasure

Purring is most noticeable, Lyons wrote in [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-do-cats-purr/), "when an animal is nursing her kittens or when humans provide social contact via petting, stroking or feeding."

Elizabeth von Muggenthaler of the Fauna Communications Research Institute in North Carolina opened her [2001 meeting abstract](https://pubs.aip.org/jasa/article/110/5_Supplement/2666/550913/The-felid-purr-A-healing-mechanism) by setting the contentment idea against the fact that "cats purr when they are severely injured or frightened." In [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-do-cats-purr/), Lyons noted that cats "often purr while under duress, such as during a visit to the veterinarian or when recovering from injury."

The [2020 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7000907/) relays a suggestion that a purr signals no threat rather than well-being, since cats also purr "in case of stress, pain or close to death." Cats may purr while [giving birth, between contractions](https://chaire-bea.vetagro-sup.fr/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/22-07-A-purring-cat-is-a-happy-cat-TRUE-or-FALSE.pdf), according to the animal welfare chair of the French veterinary school VetAgro Sup. In a 2016 consensus of [nineteen veterinary experts](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0150040) in feline medicine, purring was judged a rare sign in cats in pain, and too unreliable to infer pain from. A purr alone cannot tell an owner which of these states a cat is in.

## The breakfast purr hides a cry

Karen McComb, a psychologist at the University of Sussex, began studying purrs because her cat, Pepo, kept waking her in the mornings with insistent purring, the [university reported](https://archive.sussex.ac.uk/broadcast/read/1208) in 2009. Her team trained owners to record their cats at home, and when the recordings were played at equal volume, people, "even those with no experience of cats," judged the purrs made while cats sought food "more urgent and less pleasant" than others, [McComb said](https://archive.sussex.ac.uk/broadcast/read/1208).

The difference, McComb said in [Cell Press's release](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/07/090713121348.htm), was "an unusual high-frequency element - reminiscent of a cry or meow - embedded within the naturally low-pitched purr." When the team removed that element and changed nothing else, the urgency ratings fell. This solicitation purr, she added in the [Sussex release](https://archive.sussex.ac.uk/broadcast/read/1208), seems to develop most often in cats that have "a one-on-one with their owners" rather than in large households.

> "The embedding of a cry within a call that we normally associate with contentment is quite a subtle means of eliciting a response." - Karen McComb, University of Sussex, 2009

## The healing purr rests on a match of numbers

Von Muggenthaler's 2001 abstract also floated the idea that purring heals, under a title that ends in a question mark: "[The felid purr: A healing mechanism?](https://pubs.aip.org/jasa/article/110/5_Supplement/2666/550913/The-felid-purr-A-healing-mechanism)" She recorded [**44 felids**](https://pubs.aip.org/jasa/article/110/5_Supplement/2666/550913/The-felid-purr-A-healing-mechanism), among them cheetahs, ocelots, pumas, servals and domestic cats, and found that "every felid in the study generated strong frequencies between 25 and 150 Hz."

Those frequencies, she went on, "correspond to vibrational/electrical frequencies used in treatment for bone growth/fractures," and also for pain, swelling, muscles, joints, breathlessness and wounds. In [four of the species](https://pubs.aip.org/jasa/article/110/5_Supplement/2666/550913/The-felid-purr-A-healing-mechanism), the purrs had strong frequencies at exactly **25 and 50 Hz**, "the two low frequencies that best promote bone growth/fracture healing."

For those two numbers she cited a [1994 study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7842923/) from Nanjing that applied mechanical vibration at five frequencies to **76 rabbits** with broken radius bones. Vibration helped the fractures heal "regardless of the frequency," the [study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7842923/) reported, and "The best frequencies are 25Hz and 50Hz, second best, 12.5 and 100Hz and then, 200Hz." The purr's two frequencies led a ranking rather than standing alone.

Lyons made the same connection in [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-do-cats-purr/), writing that "various investigators have shown that sound frequencies in this range can improve bone density and promote healing," and that purring "may provide a basis" for a veterinary legend that cats can reassemble their bones when left in a room with all the pieces. Neither text reports a measurement of healing in a purring cat, which leaves the self-healing purr a hypothesis built on a match of frequencies.

Eight voice boxes in a laboratory showed that a purr's rumble can come from air and vocal-fold tissue, with no nerve signal to time it. Why a living cat starts one, in comfort, in pain or at breakfast time, is a question no larynx on a tube of warm air can answer. Strictly FYI.
