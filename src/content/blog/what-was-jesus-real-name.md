In Rome in 1524, Gian Giorgio Trissino wrote [to Pope Clement VII](https://it.wikisource.org/wiki/De_le_lettere_nu%EA%9E%B7vamente_aggiunte_ne_la_lingua_Italiana) to defend the five new letters he had added to the Italian alphabet. Two of them were meant to end an old muddle: i and u each stood for both a vowel and a consonant, so where i was a consonant, he wrote, it would be written as ["uno j lungo"](https://it.wikisource.org/wiki/De_le_lettere_nu%EA%9E%B7vamente_aggiunte_ne_la_lingua_Italiana), a long j reaching down below the line.

A j now opens the English name Jesus. The name is far older than the letter, and in the language of the man who bore it, it most likely began with neither J nor I.

## Yeshua, a shortened Joshua, is the likeliest name

Jesus was most likely called **Yeshua**, a late, short form of the Hebrew **Yehoshua**, the name English Bibles spell **Joshua**. Merriam-Webster traces the English Jesus through Late Latin and the Greek Iesous [back to the Hebrew Yeshua](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Jesus). Greek speakers wrote it Iesous, Latin speakers Iesus, and English took Jesus from the Latin.

The vowels are a reconstruction, so Yeshua is the [probable pronunciation rather than a certain one](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeshua). The short form also [passed into Aramaic as a name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeshua), and the Aramaic of the Syriac Peshitta Bible spells Joshua and Jesus the same way.

## Yehoshua shrank to Yeshua, and the Bible keeps both

The Brown-Driver-Briggs Hebrew lexicon treats the long and the "(later)" short spelling as [one masculine proper name](https://biblehub.com/hebrew/3091.htm), and it offers two readings of what the name meant: Yahweh is salvation, or Yahweh is opulence. Either way, the lexicon adds, the name came to be associated with the Hebrew root for saving, and it points the reader to Matthew 1:21.

That verse sits in Matthew's account of [the birth of Jesus](/blog/when-was-jesus-actually-born/), where an angel tells Joseph, in the King James wording, ["thou shalt call his name Jesus: for he shall save his people from their sins."](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%201%3A21&version=KJV)

The short form was not new. Strong's concordance gives Jeshua as [the name of **ten Israelites**](https://biblehub.com/hebrew/3442.htm) in the Hebrew Bible, and Nehemiah uses it for Moses's successor: the King James Version of Nehemiah 8:17 looks back to ["the days of Jeshua the son of Nun."](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Nehemiah%208%3A17&version=KJV) That is Joshua, under the same short name Jesus would carry.

## Greek made Jesus and Joshua one word

Greek had a single spelling for both men. Thayer's Greek lexicon calls Iesous ["a very common proper name among the Israelites"](https://biblehub.com/greek/2424.htm), and under that one heading it lists Joshua, an ancestor in Luke's genealogy, Jesus Barabbas and ["Jesus, surnamed Justus,"](https://biblehub.com/greek/2424.htm) as well as Jesus himself.

The King James translators kept the Greek name where it meant Joshua. In Hebrews 4:8 the King James Bible prints Jesus, and the man it means is Joshua, the successor of Moses: the King James verse reads ["For if Jesus had given them rest,"](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%204%3A8&version=KJV%3BNIV) where the NIV has ["For if Joshua had given them rest."](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%204%3A8&version=KJV%3BNIV)

Acts 7:45 does the same: the fathers brought the tabernacle into the land ["with Jesus"](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts%207%3A45&version=KJV) in the King James Version and ["under Joshua"](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts%207%3A45&version=NIV) in the NIV. The NASB, by the concordance count, renders the Greek name as [Joshua 3 times](https://biblehub.com/greek/2424.htm).

Latin pulled the two apart, but only halfway. The Latin Bible calls the book of Joshua [Iosue](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/bible.html), and its Matthew 1:21 reads ["vocabis nomen eius Iesum"](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/bible/matthew.shtml), you shall call his name Iesus. Yet its Hebrews 4:8 still opens ["Nam si eis Iesus requiem praestitisset"](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/bible/hebrews.shtml), and its Acts 7:45 has the fathers come in ["cum Iesu"](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/bible/acts.shtml), where Iesus again means Joshua.

| Language | Form of the name | Who carries it |
|---|---|---|
| Hebrew, earlier | Yehoshua | Joshua, Moses's successor |
| Hebrew, later | Yeshua | Ten men by Strong's count, and most likely Jesus |
| Greek | Iesous | Joshua and Jesus alike |
| Latin | Iosue and Iesus | Joshua as Iosue in his own book, Iesus elsewhere, as in Acts and Hebrews |
| English, 1611 | Iesus | Jesus, and Joshua in Acts, Hebrews and the Apocrypha |
| English, now | Jesus | Jesus, with Joshua restored in versions such as the NIV |

## A common name needed a second name

With so many men called Yeshua, the name alone pointed at nobody in particular. Philip, in the Gospel of John, identifies him with a town and a father: ["Jesus of Nazareth, the son of Joseph."](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%201%3A45&version=KJV) The letter to the Colossians names a different fellow worker, ["Jesus, which is called Justus."](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Colossians%204%3A11&version=KJV)

The historian Josephus needed the same kind of tag. In one passage of his Antiquities of the Jews he describes the trial of ["the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ, whose name was James,"](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2848/pg2848.txt) and a few lines later King Agrippa makes ["Jesus, the son of Damneus,"](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2848/pg2848.txt) high priest. Shortly after that, Jesus the son of Gamaliel succeeds him.

Christ was that kind of tag, a title rather than a surname. It comes through Latin from the Greek [Christos](/blog/why-do-people-say-xmas/), which Merriam-Webster glosses as [literally "anointed"](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Christ), the same meaning it gives for the Hebrew and Aramaic words behind [messiah](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/messiah).

Burial boxes show how ordinary the name was. In 1980 builders in the Talpiot suburb of Jerusalem uncovered **10 first-century ossuaries**, the Guardian reported in 2007, when a documentary claimed the tomb for Jesus's family; one carries [an inscription read as "Jesus, son of Joseph"](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2007/feb/27/religion.israel), and another the name Mariamene. Amos Kloner, an archaeologist who was one of the first to examine the ossuaries, answered that ["We have no scientific proof that this is indeed the tomb of Jesus and his family members"](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2007/feb/27/religion.israel), and he told an Israeli newspaper that the name had been found 71 times in burial caves from around that time.

> "We know that Joseph, Jesus and Mariamene were all among the most common names of the period." - [David Mevorah, Israel Museum, quoted in the Guardian, 2007](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2007/feb/27/religion.israel)

## Iesus was the King James spelling

The split between i and j took a long time to reach English: Petrus Ramus [carried it into French and Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J), and English adopted it only in the 17th century. The New Testament of the **1611** King James Bible, ["Imprinted at London by Robert Barker,"](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_(King_James_Version,_1611)/Matthew) announces on its title page ["our Lord and Sauiour Iesvs Christ,"](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_(King_James_Version,_1611)/Matthew) and its Matthew opens with the generation of Iesus Christ.

The two New Testament verses that mean Joshua read Iesus there too, as in ["For if Iesus had giuen them rest"](https://www.kingjamesbibleonline.org/1611_Hebrews-Chapter-4/) at Hebrews 4:8. So does the Apocrypha, which opens Ecclesiasticus 46 with Moses's successor as ["Iesus the sonne of Naue"](https://www.kingjamesbibleonline.org/1611_Ecclesiasticus-Chapter-46/).

The spelling does not mean the sound was different. English scribes had begun in Middle English to write [word-initial i for the sound now spelled with j](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J), copying Old French, so an I at the start of a word could already stand for the sound of the modern j. The first English books to keep i and j clearly apart, on the same account, were a [1629 Cambridge revision of the King James Bible and an English grammar of 1633](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J).

Take off the Greek ending and the Latin spelling, and the man English calls Jesus shared his name with Moses's successor, a high priest and a fellow worker in the letter to the Colossians. The J that now begins it was still a form of i in 1524, when [a letter to a pope](https://it.wikisource.org/wiki/De_le_lettere_nu%EA%9E%B7vamente_aggiunte_ne_la_lingua_Italiana) asked for it to stand as a letter of its own.
