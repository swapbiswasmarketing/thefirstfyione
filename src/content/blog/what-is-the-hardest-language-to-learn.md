Japanese and Korean sit in the US State Department's hardest group for English speakers, but for the people who grew up speaking them, the hard language was English. In [a 2004 paper](https://docs.iza.org/dp1246.pdf), the economists Barry Chiswick and Paul Miller estimated that after five years in Canada, about **one in four** immigrant men with either first language could not hold a conversation in English or French, against **fewer than 1 in 20** of the men who grew up speaking Swedish, Norwegian or Afrikaans. The figures are predictions from a model of Canada's 1991 census, in the paper's Table 3.

To rank those languages, they used scores from the State Department's Foreign Service Institute, whose School of Language Studies [reported in 1993](https://docs.iza.org/dp1246.pdf) the speaking scores English-speaking Americans of average ability could be expected to reach in **43 languages** after 16 and 24 weeks of class. Chiswick and Miller used the scores at **24 weeks**, where Japanese and Korean came last. A scale built from those classroom scores also predicted how well immigrants spoke English.

## What is the hardest language to learn?

For native English speakers, the hardest languages are **Arabic, Cantonese, Mandarin, Japanese and Korean**, which the [US State Department](https://www.state.gov/national-foreign-affairs-training-center/foreign-language-training) labels "super-hard." Its estimate for each is **88 weeks** of full-time study, listed as **2,200 class hours**, against 24 to 30 weeks for the easiest. No language is hardest for everyone, because difficulty depends on the language you start from.

| Category | Weeks | Class hours | Languages |
|---|---|---|---|
| I, "close to English" | 24-30 | 552-690 | Danish, Dutch, Italian, Norwegian, Portuguese, Romanian, Swedish (24); French, Spanish (30) |
| II | About 36 | 828 | German, Haitian Creole, Indonesian, Malay, Swahili |
| III, "hard" | About 44 | 1,012 | 40 listed, among them Finnish, Greek, Hebrew, Hindi, Hungarian, Polish, Russian and Turkish |
| IV, "super-hard" | 88 | 2,200 | Arabic, Cantonese, Mandarin, Japanese, Korean |

A typical week, the [State Department's page](https://www.state.gov/national-foreign-affairs-training-center/foreign-language-training) says, is **23 hours** of class and **17 hours** of self-study. Category IV's 2,200 hours matches the 25-hour week implied by [the 2020 version](https://web.archive.org/web/20200826155755/https://www.state.gov/foreign-language-training/), which gave 600 class hours for 24 weeks; at 23 hours a week, 88 weeks comes to about 2,000.

## The FSI ranking measures diplomats in full-time class

The list is usually called the FSI ranking, after the State Department's Foreign Service Institute. In 2020 [the page](https://web.archive.org/web/20200826155755/https://www.state.gov/foreign-language-training/) ran it under the heading "FSI's Experience with Language Learning" and said the timelines "reflect 70 years of experience in teaching languages to U.S. diplomats."

> Even the State Department's "super-hard" label comes with a qualifier: "Languages which are exceptionally difficult for native English speakers."

Each estimate is the time usually expected for a student to reach level 3 on the Interagency Language Roundtable scale, in speaking and listening combined, and [the current page](https://www.state.gov/national-foreign-affairs-training-center/foreign-language-training) cautions that actual time can vary with several factors, including "natural ability, prior language-learning experience, and time spent in the classroom." The [ILR](https://www.govtilr.org/Skills/ILRscale2.htm) calls speaking level 3 "General Professional Proficiency" and describes such a speaker as able "to participate effectively in most formal and informal conversations in practical, social and professional topics."

The estimates also move. In 2020 [the page](https://web.archive.org/web/20200826155755/https://www.state.gov/foreign-language-training/) gave Spanish **24 weeks** and set the target as "Speaking-3/Reading-3," reading rather than listening. The [current page](https://www.state.gov/national-foreign-affairs-training-center/foreign-language-training), which credits the timelines to the department's National Foreign Affairs Training Center, gives Spanish **30**, the same as French.

In the [1993 scores](https://docs.iza.org/dp1246.pdf) the five super-hard languages were not equals: Japanese and Korean scored **1.00**, Cantonese 1.25, and Mandarin and Arabic 1.50, on a scale where lower meant harder and Norwegian and Swedish scored 3.00.

## Danish toddlers lag in one vocabulary comparison

Closely related languages do not always score alike. Danish, Norwegian and Swedish each get 24 weeks, yet the [1993 scores](https://docs.iza.org/dp1246.pdf) expected Americans to reach **3.00** in Norwegian and Swedish but **2.25** in Danish.

Danish children have a harder start too. A [2008 study](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0305000908008714) compared Danish toddlers with children in 17 other studies of early vocabulary. Their vocabulary grew along the same general trend, but from age 1 onward their [vocabulary comprehension score was the lowest across studies](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0305000908008714). The authors' hypothesis is that Danish sound structure "presents Danish children with a harder task of segmentation," the job of hearing where one word ends and the next begins. If they are right, Danish sets a harder task even for the children born into it.

## The "world's hardest language" title is unsupported

Lists that crown one language the world's hardest have no measurement behind them that applies to every learner. The State Department's list measures from English, and when Chiswick and Miller went looking for a yardstick, the prevailing view among linguists, [they wrote](https://docs.iza.org/dp1246.pdf), was that the distance between two languages "cannot be measured." They turned to learners' test scores instead.

No European language makes the State Department's Category IV. Hungarian, Finnish, Polish, Russian and Greek all sit in Category III, and in the [1993 scores](https://docs.iza.org/dp1246.pdf) Hungarian, Finnish and Polish each stood at **2.00**, while Russian scored 2.25 and Greek **1.75**.

Everyday speech has its own byword for the hardest language. In Shakespeare's [*Julius Caesar*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1522/pg1522-images.html), Casca says Cicero "spoke Greek," and adds, "for mine own part, it was Greek to me." The Real Academia Espanola's dictionary gives [griego](https://dle.rae.es/griego), Greek, a colloquial sense of "lenguaje considerado incomprensible," language considered incomprehensible, with *chino*, Chinese, and [gringo](/blog/what-does-gringo-mean/) as synonyms.

## Hard is a distance from the language you start with

Chiswick and Miller assumed what they called "linguistic symmetry": that the gap an American crosses to learn Japanese is the one a Japanese speaker crosses to learn English. In their [estimates from the 1990 US census](https://docs.iza.org/dp1246.pdf), an immigrant whose first language was Japanese was about **17 percentage points** less likely to speak English well than an otherwise similar one whose first language was Swedish, the equivalent of about **5.4 years** of schooling.

The pattern holds beyond English. A [2020 study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31733600/) in *Cognition* analyzed the speaking scores of about **50,000** adults learning Dutch, from **62** native languages. The native language accounted for [9 to 22 percent](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31733600/) of the variation in scores, and almost **80 percent** of that effect could be explained by how close it was to Dutch in sounds, word structure and vocabulary.

The super-hard five are far from English in writing, and Mandarin and Cantonese in sound as well. Mandarin and Cantonese, like the other [Chinese languages, are tonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tone_(linguistics)), so a change of pitch can change a word's meaning. Arabic has its own script, Korean its own alphabet, Mandarin and Cantonese are written in Chinese characters, and Japanese [mixes those characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_writing_system) with two syllabaries, hiragana and katakana.

Measured from Hebrew instead of English, one of the five super-hard languages becomes a head start. Among Jewish immigrants in Israel, studied in the 1972 census and in surveys from the 1970s, those whose language of origin was Arabic were the [most proficient in Hebrew](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-2435.00155), other things being equal, though by country of origin those from North Africa were the least proficient. Michael Beenstock, Chiswick and Gaston Repetto read the Arabic result as a sign of the short distance between the two Semitic languages. Immigrants from English-speaking origins were the [least proficient in Hebrew](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-2435.00155), which the authors thought more likely reflected English's role as the international language, a weaker incentive to invest in Hebrew.
