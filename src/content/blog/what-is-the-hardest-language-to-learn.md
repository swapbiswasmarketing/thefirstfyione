Japanese and Korean sit in the US State Department's hardest group for English speakers, but for the people who grew up speaking them, the hard language was English. After five years in Canada, about **one in four** immigrant men with either first language could not hold a conversation in English or French, against **fewer than 1 in 20** of the men who grew up speaking Swedish, Norwegian or Afrikaans. The figures are predictions from a model of Canada's 1991 census, in Table 3 of [a 2004 paper](https://docs.iza.org/dp1246.pdf) by the economists Barry Chiswick and Paul Miller.

To rank those languages, they used scores from the US State Department's language school, which in 1993 reported the speaking scores English-speaking Americans of average ability could be expected to reach in **43 languages** after set periods of class. Chiswick and Miller used the scores at **24 weeks**, where Japanese and Korean came last. A scale built from Americans struggling with Japanese also predicted how well immigrants spoke English.

## What is the hardest language to learn?

For native English speakers, the hardest languages are **Arabic, Cantonese, Mandarin, Japanese and Korean**, which the US State Department labels "super-hard." Its estimate for each is **88 weeks** of full-time study, listed as **2,200 class hours**, against 24 to 30 weeks for the easiest. No language is hardest for everyone, because difficulty depends on the language you start from.

| Category | Weeks | Class hours | Languages |
|---|---|---|---|
| I, "close to English" | 24-30 | 552-690 | Danish, Dutch, Italian, Norwegian, Portuguese, Romanian, Swedish (24); French, Spanish (30) |
| II | About 36 | 828 | German, Haitian Creole, Indonesian, Malay, Swahili |
| III, "hard" | About 44 | 1,012 | 40 listed, among them Finnish, Greek, Hebrew, Hindi, Hungarian, Polish, Russian and Turkish |
| IV, "super-hard" | 88 | 2,200 | Arabic, Cantonese, Mandarin, Japanese, Korean |

A typical week, the [State Department's page](https://www.state.gov/national-foreign-affairs-training-center/foreign-language-training) says, is **23 hours** of class and **17 hours** of self-study. Category IV's 2,200 hours follows the 25-hour week of [earlier versions](https://web.archive.org/web/20200826155755/https://www.state.gov/foreign-language-training/), which gave 600 class hours for 24 weeks; at 23 hours a week, 88 weeks comes to about 2,000.

## The FSI ranking measures diplomats in full-time class

The list is known as the FSI ranking because the State Department's Foreign Service Institute published it under the heading "FSI's Experience with Language Learning"; the 2020 version of the page still used that heading. That version said the timelines reflected 70 years of teaching languages to US diplomats. The current page credits them to the department's National Foreign Affairs Training Center.

> Even the State Department's "super-hard" label comes with a qualifier: "Languages which are exceptionally difficult for native English speakers."

Each estimate is the time usually expected for a student to reach level 3 on the Interagency Language Roundtable scale, in speaking and listening combined. The [ILR](https://www.govtilr.org/Skills/ILRscale2.htm) describes a level 3 speaker as able "to participate effectively in most formal and informal conversations in practical, social and professional topics." Actual time, the page cautions, can vary with factors including "natural ability, prior language-learning experience, and time spent in the classroom."

The estimates also move. In 2020 the page gave Spanish **24 weeks**; it now gives **30**, the same as French.

In the 1993 scores the five super-hard languages were not equals. Japanese and Korean scored **1.00**, Cantonese 1.25, and Mandarin and Arabic 1.50, on a scale where lower meant harder and Norwegian and Swedish scored 3.00.

## Danish toddlers lag in one vocabulary comparison

Closely related languages do not always score alike. Danish, Norwegian and Swedish, which all toast with [skal](/blog/what-does-skol-mean/), each get 24 weeks, yet the 1993 scores expected Americans to reach **3.00** in Norwegian and Swedish but **2.25** in Danish.

Danish children have a harder start too. A [2008 study](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0305000908008714) compared Danish toddlers with children in 17 other studies of early vocabulary. Their vocabulary grew along the same general trend, but from age 1 their comprehension scores were the lowest in the comparison. The authors' hypothesis is that Danish sound structure "presents Danish children with a harder task of segmentation," the job of hearing where one word ends and the next begins. If they are right, some of the difficulty sits in the language itself, not only in its distance from English.

## The "world's hardest language" title is unsupported

Lists that hand one language, often Mandarin, the title of the world's hardest have no measurement behind them that applies to every learner. The State Department's list measures from English, and when Chiswick and Miller went looking for a yardstick, the prevailing view among linguists was that even the distance between two languages "cannot be measured." They turned to learners' test scores instead.

Languages often named as Europe's hardest, such as Hungarian, Finnish and Polish, sit in Category III with Russian, Greek and 35 others. In the 1993 scores all three stood at 2.00, while Greek scored **1.75**.

Everyday speech has its own byword for the hardest language. In Shakespeare's [*Julius Caesar*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1522/pg1522-images.html), Casca says Cicero "spoke Greek," and adds, "for mine own part, it was Greek to me." The Real Academia Espanola's dictionary gives [griego](https://dle.rae.es/griego), Greek, a colloquial sense of "lenguaje considerado incomprensible," language considered incomprehensible, with *chino*, Chinese, and [gringo](/blog/what-does-gringo-mean/) as synonyms.

## Hard is a distance, and it runs both ways

Chiswick and Miller assumed what they called "linguistic symmetry": that the gap an American crosses to learn Japanese is the one a Japanese speaker crosses to learn English. Their census results are consistent with that assumption. In their estimates from the 1990 US census, an immigrant whose first language was Japanese was about **17 percentage points** less likely to speak English well than an otherwise similar one whose first language was Swedish, the equivalent of about **5.4 years** of schooling.

The pattern holds beyond English. A [2020 study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31733600/) in *Cognition* analyzed the speaking scores of about **50,000** adults learning Dutch, from **62** native languages. The native language accounted for 9 to 22 percent of the variation in scores, and almost **80 percent** of that effect could be explained by how close it was to Dutch in sounds, word structure and vocabulary.

For an English speaker, the super-hard five are far in sound and in writing. Mandarin and Cantonese are [tonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tone_(linguistics)), so a change of pitch can change a word's meaning. Arabic has its own script, Korean its own alphabet, Mandarin and Cantonese are written in Chinese characters, and Japanese mixes those characters with two syllabaries, hiragana and katakana.

Measured from Hebrew instead of English, one of the five super-hard languages becomes a head start. Among Jewish immigrants to Israel in the 1970s, those who grew up speaking Arabic were the [most proficient in Hebrew](https://docs.iza.org/dp1246.pdf), other things being equal. Chiswick and his co-authors suggested it was due to the short distance between the two Semitic languages. Strictly FYI.
