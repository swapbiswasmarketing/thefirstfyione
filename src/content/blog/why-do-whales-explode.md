Florence, Oregon, [Thursday 12 November 1970](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/evidence/newspaper-1970/rg-1970-11-12). A state highway crew has spent an hour and 45 minutes [placing twenty 50-pound cases of explosives](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/evidence/newspaper-1970/when-they-blow-up-a-whale) under something 45 feet long that washed up on the beach three days earlier and has been rotting there since. The charge goes off. Spectators run for cover while lumps of blubber come down around them, one chunk [flattening a parked car a quarter-mile away](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/evidence/newspaper-1970/rg-1970-11-17).

That sperm whale did not burst on its own, and the footage says so. In the television report, KATU's Paul Linnman sums up the Highway Division's decision in one line: ["So dynamite it was, some 20 cases or a half ton of it"](https://katu.com/news/local/the-exploding-whale-50th-anniversary-of-legendary-oregon-event).

## Why do whales explode?

Dead whales bloat because gut bacteria keep digesting after the animal dies, filling a body sealed by thick blubber and tough skin with carbon dioxide, methane, hydrogen sulfide and ammonia. Pressure rises until something gives. Most of the bursts that have been [documented](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/more-whales), though, were set off by people blowing up, cutting into or trucking the whale.

## The bacteria are already inside

A whale does not need to catch anything to start rotting. Its gut is already full of microbes, and once circulation stops they keep working and start travelling. The StatPearls reference chapter on [postmortem changes](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539741/), hosted by the US National Library of Medicine, names Clostridium perfringens as the principal agent, describes the gut's own flora migrating into the blood vessels, and lists what the process gives off: **hydrogen sulfide, ammonia, carbon dioxide and methane**. By its account even a human abdomen may burst from the pressure.

Two things make a whale a better container for that than a horse or a herring. A whale's [durable outer skin, thick blubber walls, and huge size](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/taxonomy) can leave the gas few ways out. The same fat is insulation, and the StatPearls chapter, written about human bodies, links higher body fat to faster putrefaction through ["increased fluid content and heat retention"](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539741/), and says chemical activity roughly doubles for every **10 degrees Celsius** of extra warmth. On an Oregon beach in 2024, a NOAA-authorized stranding team watched steam rise from a dead humpback ["as decomposition heated the roughly 15 to 20 tons of blubber, baleen, muscle, and internal organs."](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/feature-story/whale-story-necropsy-reveals-health-species-and-ocean)

## The gas is mostly carbon dioxide

Popular accounts reach for methane; the Daily Mail's report on a burst whale in the Faroe Islands, [reproduced in one archive](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/more-whales/20131126-faroe-islands), said "bacteria inside the carcass produce methane". When Yara Bernaldo de Quiros and colleagues sampled gas bubbles inside stranded whales and dolphins for [Frontiers in Physiology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3366475/) in 2012, the well-decomposed animals gave up mostly something else. Their bubbles were high in carbon dioxide with hydrogen alongside it, nitrogen mostly under 40 percent, and some samples came back at [**100 percent carbon dioxide**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3366475/). Hydrogen, the paper concludes, is a key putrefaction marker in stranded cetaceans, and in gut samples methane turned up only in sperm whales, the Faroese animal's family, and their small Kogia relatives.

## Oregon's whale was blown up on purpose

The Eugene Register-Guard reported on 11 November that the animal had come ashore the previous Monday, [45 feet long and eight tons](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/evidence/newspaper-1970/rg-1970-11-11), and called it a gray whale; the next day's paper [made it a sperm whale](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/evidence/newspaper-1970/rg-1970-11-12), because gray whales have no teeth and this one did.

The state Highway Division, which then had [responsibility for care of the ocean beaches](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/evidence/newspaper-1970/rg-1970-11-11), found no rendering plant that wanted it, and assistant district engineer George Thornton ruled out burial because "these things have a tendency to just get uncovered again." According to KATU reporter Paul Linnman, Thornton [had consulted the US Navy](https://katu.com/news/local/the-exploding-whale-50th-anniversary-of-legendary-oregon-event) and settled on explosives, on the theory that the pieces would be small enough for scavengers to clear.

The charge came to [1,000 pounds of explosives](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/evidence/newspaper-1970/rg-1970-11-23), half a ton. Thornton meant to throw most of the whale toward the ocean; instead the blast raised a [100-foot high column of sand and whale](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/evidence/newspaper-1970/when-they-blow-up-a-whale), and a three-foot chunk sailed over the crowd and crushed the roof of Walter Umenhofer's [1969 Oldsmobile](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/evidence/newspaper-1970/rg-1970-11-23) in a beachside parking lot. Thornton's verdict to the Register-Guard was ["It went just exactly right,"](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/evidence/newspaper-1970/when-they-blow-up-a-whale) with one reservation: "Except the blast funneled a hole in the sand under the whale."

Umenhofer, a Springfield businessman with demolitions experience from the service, had watched from farther back because he felt the shot was [improperly placed and overloaded](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/evidence/newspaper-1970/rg-1970-11-23). By 23 November the Highway Division's insurer had paid him the car's [full retail value](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/evidence/newspaper-1970/rg-1970-11-23), and his summary was "They're just lucky they didn't kill somebody." KATU's anniversary account records the opposite complaint: the [general consensus](https://katu.com/news/local/the-exploding-whale-50th-anniversary-of-legendary-oregon-event) afterwards was that not enough dynamite was used.

> The film everyone has seen is not a whale exploding. It is a whale being exploded, by a state highway department, with half a ton of dynamite and a television crew standing in the dunes.

## Three famous bursts, three different causes

| Case | What the carcass did | What set it off |
|---|---|---|
| Florence, Oregon, 12 Nov 1970 | 45-foot sperm whale broken apart on the sand | Half a ton of dynamite |
| Tainan, Taiwan, 26 Jan 2004 | 17-metre sperm whale burst in a city street | Gas pressure, while being trucked through the city |
| Faroe Islands, 26 Nov 2013 | Sperm whale carcass burst at a disused whaling station | A flensing knife |

A fisherman found the Taiwanese whale still alive on the Yunlin County shore on a Saturday morning, [Taiwan News reported](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/more-whales/20040126-taiwan), and it was dead by the time rescuers arrived that afternoon. National Cheng Kung University would not allow the necropsy on campus, so the carcass was bound for a nature reserve, and loading it took [50 workers, three cranes and 13 hours](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/more-whales/20040126-taiwan). It let go on [26 January](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/87015-largest-mammal-to-explode-natural), a Monday, in Tainan traffic, splattering shops, parked cars and passers-by.

Other accounts of the death disagree: MSNBC said the whale had died on [Jan. 17](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/more-whales/20040126-taiwan), and the Taipei Times had it ["found dead on the seashore of Yunlin County on Sunday"](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/taiwan/archives/2004/01/29/2003096602). Guinness World Records lists it as the [largest mammal to explode, natural](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/87015-largest-mammal-to-explode-natural); the archive's own write-up says the pressure was ["undoubtedly exacerbated by being under transport"](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/taxonomy).

The Faroese carcass, on **26 November 2013**, was opened deliberately. A dead sperm whale had been towed to a disused whaling station so its bones could be harvested for a museum display, and Bjarni Mikkelsen, a marine biologist at the National Museum of the Faroe Islands, was cutting it open when the body [blew out and tore the two-handed flensing knife from his hands](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/more-whales/20131126-faroe-islands). He had expected some pressure, he [told MailOnline](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/more-whales/20131126-faroe-islands), because "The animal was more than two days old when we took it".

## NOAA's options do not include explosives

NOAA Fisheries publishes what actually happens to a dead whale now. The carcass can be [left to decompose where it lies, moved somewhere remote, buried above the high tide line, towed offshore to float or sink, or taken away for incineration, composting, rendering or a landfill](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/national/marine-life-distress/how-experts-properly-dispose-dead-marine-mammals), a list that includes burial and rendering, both of which Oregon's highway engineers considered and dropped. The agency's first instruction to everyone else is ["Give space: Do not attempt to touch, handle, remove parts, or return the carcass to the water."](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/national/marine-life-distress/how-experts-properly-dispose-dead-marine-mammals) Stranded animals [can carry infectious diseases](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/national/marine-life-distress/how-experts-properly-dispose-dead-marine-mammals) that pass to people and pets, and responders may wear protective clothing, gloves, masks and eye protection.

The archive at theexplodingwhale.com [catalogues twelve incidents](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/more-whales) between 2001 and 2013, in Australia, South Africa, Taiwan, California and the Faroes. Its [taxonomy](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/taxonomy) sorts them into carcass removal, euthanasia and putrefaction, and half of the twelve are euthanasias: stranded whales still alive when the charge went off. Tainan and the Faroes are filed under putrefaction, beside a humpback at [Half Moon Bay, California, in August 2005](http://www.theexplodingwhale.com/more-whales/20050818-california) that, the archive says, "did not actually explode".

The normal outcome is undramatic. NOAA's [description of a carcass left where it lies](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/national/marine-life-distress/how-experts-properly-dispose-dead-marine-mammals) is that it will "slowly break down over many months or years, returning nutrients to the environment." When the young humpback came ashore on the northern Oregon coast in 2024, the state park's managers [decided to let it decompose naturally](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/feature-story/whale-story-necropsy-reveals-health-species-and-ocean).

A dead whale on a beach is a sealed bag of warm fat with its own bacteria working through it, and left to itself it is mostly a smell and an instruction not to touch anything. What turns one into footage is nearly always a person arriving with a knife, a truck or twenty cases of dynamite. Strictly FYI.
