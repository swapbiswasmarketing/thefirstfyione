There's a face on the front of your car. The electrical outlet on the wall has one too. There's one in the froth of your coffee, the pattern of the tiles, the burnt bit of your toast. You see them instantly, involuntarily, and often can't un-see them.

This is **face pareidolia**, which a 2017 paper in Current Biology defines as ["the compelling illusion of perceiving facial features on inanimate objects, such as the illusory face on the surface of the moon."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5584612/) The evidence for why it happens comes from brain scanners, maternity wards and a monkey lab.

## Why does your brain see faces in things?

Your brain sees faces in things because its face detector fires on a bare minimum of cues and is [tuned to over-detect rather than miss](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7481186/). A 2020 study in Nature Communications, which tracked illusory faces through the brain with two kinds of scanner, found the pattern [consistent with "a broadly-tuned face detection mechanism which privileges sensitivity over selectivity."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7481186/)

The face-like objects in a 2009 study were not drawn faces at all, just everyday things that shared ["elements representing two eyes, and mouth/nose"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2713437/) with a real face, and that was enough.

## A patch of cortex that prefers faces

In 1997 Nancy Kanwisher and colleagues used fMRI to find [an area in the fusiform gyrus in 12 of the 15 subjects tested](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6573547/) that was more active when people looked at faces than at assorted common objects. The paper called the region the **fusiform face area**, and further tests in the [same study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6573547/) found it responding more to intact faces than to scrambled ones, and more to faces than to houses or hands.

The 2009 study, by Nouchine Hadjikhani's team at the Martinos Center for Biomedical Imaging in Massachusetts, asked whether objects that only look like faces reach that region, and how fast. Using magnetoencephalography, which follows brain signals millisecond by millisecond, the team found that face-like objects [evoked activation in the ventral fusiform cortex at 165 ms, at a time and location similar to that evoked by faces, whereas common objects did not](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2713437/).

The timing is the point: at about a sixth of a second, the face arrives too early to be a considered second opinion. The study was small, with [nine volunteers, two of whom could not be analyzed](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2713437/), and its authors suggested that seeing faces in objects is "a relatively early process, and not a late re-interpretation cognitive phenomenon." You do not decide the outlet has a face. You see the face before you can think about it.

The 2020 study, by Susan Wardle and colleagues at the National Institute of Mental Health, found the second half of the story: illusory faces are [initially represented more similarly to real faces than matched objects are, but within about 250 ms the representation transforms](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7481186/) and they become equivalent to ordinary objects.

> In the first quarter of a second, the brain briefly represents an object with an illusory face as [more face-like than a matched object without one](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7481186/). Then it files the thing back among the objects, and the face stays visible anyway.

## Newborns follow faces within minutes of birth

The habit shows up before experience could have taught it. In 1975, Goren, Sarty and Wu reported in Pediatrics that [forty newborn infants, median age 9 minutes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1165958/), turned their eyes and heads further to follow a moving schematic face than two scrambled versions of it or a blank. The authors [pointed out](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1165958/) that the babies "had not seen a real face prior to testing."

The result was contested: when Johnson, of the MRC Cognitive Development Unit in London, and colleagues returned to it in 1991, they wrote that it ["has remained controversial and been largely ignored"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1786670/), then replicated it twice, the second time in a different maternity hospital, and found that the arrangement of the features mattered for tracking in the first hour of life. Their third experiment found that the preferential tracking of faces ["declines during the second month."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1786670/)

## Rhesus monkeys see faces in objects too

One obvious objection is that people learn pareidolia from cartoons and illustrations that give objects faces. Jessica Taubert and colleagues tested that in the 2017 study by showing [five rhesus monkeys 15 photographs of objects that elicited face pareidolia in human observers, with 15 matched objects that did not](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5584612/), plus photos of monkey faces. The monkeys looked longer at the face-like objects than at the matched ones, and their eye movements went to the illusory eyes and mouth in a pattern like the one they use on photographs of faces.

The authors concluded that pareidolia is driven by ["a broadly-tuned face detection mechanism that we share with other species."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5584612/) Whatever makes a kettle look worried, cartoons cannot be the whole explanation.

## The evolutionary story is a hypothesis

The usual explanation is about cost, and the 2009 paper opens with it, in an epigraph from David Navon: ["An old-time hominid would be liable to pay dearly, had s/he failed to recognize a pair of glowing dots in the bush at dark as the eyes of a predator, mistaking it for two fireflies."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2713437/) A false alarm costs a flinch. A missed face could cost everything. The 2017 monkey paper puts the same trade-off in lab terms: a broadly tuned template gives ["a highly sensitive face detection system, offset with the relatively small cost of more frequent false positives."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5584612/)

That evolution favored a detector built this way is a reasonable story, and it fits what the scanners show, but it is an inference. Nobody can go back and count which ancestors flinched at bushes and which did not. What the studies above measure is the tuning itself: fast, generous and shared with at least one other primate.

## Illusory faces come with a mood and usually a gender

The faces are not blank. In a 2022 study in PNAS, Wardle's group ran online experiments with [3,815 adults](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8812520/) in all, and in its opening experiment most illusory faces were given an expression, with only 19% rated neutral. Going by each face's most common rating, [happiness (34%), surprise (19%), anger (14%)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8812520/) were the top emotions, so the startled look of a wall outlet is the runner-up.

The result the authors called most striking was about gender: [90% of illusory face images had a male mean rating, while only 9% of images had a female mean rating](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8812520/). The bias could not be explained by gender associations with the objects or by features of the images, and the authors [concluded](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8812520/) that "the visual features that are sufficient for face detection are not generally sufficient for the perception of female."

## The same illusion on bigger canvases

The Face on Mars came with its own explanation. When NASA released the Viking 1 Orbiter picture of a mesa in July 1976, [its caption said the formation, 1.5 kilometers across, "is formed by shadows giving the illusion of eyes, nose and mouth."](https://science.nasa.gov/resource/the-face-on-mars/)

Car fronts get read the same way. A 2008 study in Human Nature asked people what traits and emotions they saw in car fronts and found that [automotive features and proportions covary with trait perception in a manner similar to that found with human faces](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26181746/).

So the next time a mailbox looks mildly judgmental at you, your brain is doing what it was tuned to do. It would rather show you a hundred faces that aren't there than let you miss the one that is. Strictly FYI.
