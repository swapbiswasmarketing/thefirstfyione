The third of three possible origins in Matt Soniak's [Mental Floss explainer](https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/31795/why-are-flea-markets-called) hands the credit to New York. Dutch traders who settled New Netherland, it says, had an outdoor market "they called the Vlaie (sometimes spelled as Vly, or Vlie) Market," named for their word for swamp. English speakers "pronounced the word with an f up front," and "the Fly/Flea Market and other places like it eventually all became flea markets."

New York's Fly Market closed in **1822**, **65 years** before the earliest known "flea market" in English print.

## Why is it called a flea market?

A flea market is named for the insect. The English name is usually traced to the French **marche aux puces**, "market of fleas," a mocking name for markets of secondhand clothes and rags that people believed carried fleas. The earliest English example found so far, from **1887**, describes a market in Copenhagen.

[Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/flea%20market) dates the English term to **1910** and quotes a 1922 book, *In Europe*: the Paris market is so called "because there are so many second-hand articles sold of all kinds that they are believed to gather fleas." [Merriam-Webster's dictionary](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/flea%20market) gives a first known use of **1891**. Both dates are later than the 1887 letter.

## The New York Fly Market story fails on dates

The Fly did come from Dutch, through the name of a marsh rather than the market itself. Records from as early as **1655** call a salt marsh along the East River "Smee's Vlie, Smidt's Vley," which English-speaking New Yorkers turned into "Smith's Fly." They were "adopting the sound of the name, without the sense," Thomas F. De Voe wrote in [*The Market Book*](https://archive.org/details/cu31924013878784), his 1862 history of public markets.

The city granted a petition for the market in **1699**, and the market house went up about seven years later at the foot of Maiden Lane. It was a food market, with butchers among its stallholders.

On **21 January 1822**, the Gazette announced that Fulton Market would open the next day and that sales at the Fly Market "will be discontinued." Several butchers stayed on while the building was torn down. The last sale, De Voe recorded, ended when a piece of the roof fell between a butcher's son weighing out meat and his customer, a ship's captain.

De Voe never calls it the Flea Market. Like the egg cup story behind [cocktails](/blog/why-are-cocktails-called-cocktails/), the New York version fails on dates.

## The earliest known English example is from Copenhagen

On **29 May 1887**, page 9 of [The Sun](https://chroniclingamerica.loc.gov/lccn/sn83030272/1887-05-29/ed-1/seq-14/), a New York paper, carried a short item headed "Last Day of the Flea Market," taken "From a Copenhagen Letter." It opens: "Yesterday was the last day of the flea market."

"The fifty-two old women who have sat haggling over their uncanny wares in the square by the Government pawn shop" had appealed to the Mayor, the Minister of the Interior and finally the King to keep their stands. They were told "there was no room for sentiment in their case."

> "Perhaps the fleas had killed it. Their mixed stock of second-hand clothes, old rags, felt shoes, and crockery certainly harbored a fair share," the letter went on.

Pascal Treguer, whose [Word Histories](https://wordhistories.net/2017/04/27/flea-market/) blog reprints the item, says he does not know whether the writer was translating the French phrase or the Danish **loppemarked**, from loppe, flea. Merriam-Webster's [article on the term](https://www.merriam-webster.com/wordplay/why-do-we-call-it-a-flea-market) quotes the same letter, crediting it to the New York Herald of the same date.

## French papers used the name as a jeer

A French paper had the phrase by **10 December 1887**, when [Le Farceur](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k9536630x/f3.item) of Boulogne-sur-Mer, reviewing paintings of the town, recommended one of a market where old clothes and scrap iron were laid out: "le marche aux puces, quoi," the flea market, you know.

In October 1890, [La Depeche de Brest](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k330744c/f2.item) called the yearly fair on the town's place de la Liberte "le marche aux puces," a once-a-year sale of the region's junk dealers' stock: worn-out uniforms, bedding with bedbugs, old books, rags and scrap iron. Only someone with "des instincts de rat," a rat's instincts, would try to sell it, the reporter wrote.

A week later [the paper admitted](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k330751k/f2.item) it had upset the town's revendeuses, the women who resold old goods. **Fourteen** of them had signed a protest, written out in elegant calligraphy, against the words "Marche aux puces, marche coquin, bric a brac, guenilles."

They also took a copy to a rival paper, which turned out to have used "Marche aux puces" as the headline of its own fair report. The Depeche declared itself forgiven for that phrase, since the rival had printed it too, and defended the other words with dictionary definitions.

In June 1891, Paris papers used the name for a ragged open-air market at the Montreuil-sous-Bois gate. "Peu de Parisiens connaissent le Marche-aux-Puces," [La Lanterne](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k75293445/f3.item) began, few Parisians know it: tattered clothes, crushed opera hats and soleless shoes sold beside meat of uncertain freshness.

## Saint-Ouen dates its own name to about 1904

The [official history](https://www.pucesdeparissaintouen.com/les-puces/) of the Saint-Ouen market traces it to the **1870s**, when ragpickers driven out of Paris settled beyond the city's fortifications, and gives **1885** as its official birth date. Their trade was threatened in **1883**, when Prefect Eugene Poubelle ordered lidded rubbish bins for the buildings of Paris.

The same history calls the name anonymous, "apparue vers 1904," appearing around 1904, and says the satirical magazine L'Assiette au beurre made the market famous in **1910** with an entire issue. The newspapers above had used the name at least 13 years earlier.

## The flee story and the bargain hunter, both unsourced

Merriam-Webster also mentions a theory that a bargain hunter declared the Saint-Ouen market of the 1880s "le marche aux puces." It names no bargain hunter and cites no source.

The other Paris story has dealers displaced by the rebuilding of the city forced to flee, so English speakers called their markets flee markets. Flee and flea only sound alike, the same trap behind the [poop deck story](/blog/why-is-it-called-a-poop-deck/). The 1887 letter already means the insect, and Merriam-Webster notes "considerably more evidence for flea than flee."

Fourteen resellers in Brest protested the name in elegant calligraphy, and it stuck anyway. Saint-Ouen, with about **1,100** dealers and galleries, now carries the old jeer in its official name, the Marche aux Puces de Paris Saint-Ouen. Strictly FYI.
