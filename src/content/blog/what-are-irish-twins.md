You may have gone to school with a brother and sister who sat in the same grade without being twins, one born in September and the other the following August. Siblings like that have an old American nickname, Irish twins, and [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Irish_twin) tags it rare, slang and offensive while noting that it fits best when the pair are born "within the same calendar year or school year."

The usual story says the nickname began in the 19th century as a jibe at the size of Irish families. A slang dictionary has examples from that century to back up the date.

## What are Irish twins?

Irish twins are two siblings born less than **12 months** apart, from separate pregnancies, so for part of every year they are the same age. The phrase is American slang that mocked the supposed fertility of Irish families. It was in print **by 1852**; Wiktionary labels it offensive, and Green's treats it as derogatory.

## The first Irish twins in the slang dictionary were a newspaper and a baby

[Green's Dictionary of Slang](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq) defines Irish twins as "two siblings born within a 12-month period," labels the phrase US and puts it down to the stereotypical fecundity, and lack of contraceptive practice, of traditional Irish families.

The quotations for Irish twins in [Green's Dictionary of Slang](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq) begin with a newspaper and a baby, in the **Louisville Daily Courier** of June 4, 1852. The writer's Irish twins were a newspaper, the Visitor, which had been "our only baby" until "baby Nettie has come," as [Green's quotes the passage](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq).

Green's does not name the writer, but the details fit Jane Grey Swisshelm, who founded a newspaper in Pittsburgh in 1847 and had a daughter, Mary Henrietta, "who was called Zo or Nettie," according to [St. Cloud State University's archive record](https://stcloudstate.libraryhost.com/index.php/swisshelm-jane-grey-cannon-1815-1884) for her. Her paper was the [Saturday Visiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Swisshelm), spelled with an e.

Green's files the passage as a figurative use, for [combining a job with childcare](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq). A figure of speech like that only works on readers who already know the literal sense.

> "We find ourselves quite unequal to the task of managing our Irish twins." - Louisville Daily Courier, June 4, 1852, as quoted in [Green's Dictionary of Slang](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq)

## By 1887 a Kansas paper was joking about Irish twins

The next citation in Green's, from the **Leavenworth Times** in Kansas on June 5, 1887, sets English twins against Irish ones. Queen Victoria sent money to every English mother "on the occasion of her first twins," the paper claimed, and it went on: "Doubtless she does not feel that it is incumbent upon her to offer a dollar premium for Irish twins," as [Green's records](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq). Green's files it under the slang sense, though the excerpt alone does not show whether the paper meant siblings a year apart or twins born to Irish mothers.

Wiktionary's own etymology says the phrase was "Originally used (in the nineteenth century) to mock the fertility of Irish families," yet the one quotation on [its page](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Irish_twin) comes from a novel of 2014. The newspapers in Green's supply the 19th-century evidence that the page lacks, and they back its date.

The company the phrase keeps points the same way. Green's prefaces its long list of slang built on the word Irish with a warning that "all combs. with Irish should be assumed to be derog." unless it marks them otherwise, in [the same entry](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq) that holds Irish twins. Spanish once singled out the Irish too: an 18th-century Spanish dictionary recorded that in Madrid [the name gringo](/blog/what-does-gringo-mean/) went particularly to them.

## A Civil War regiment's history pinned it on a Virginia family

A regimental history published in 1911 applied the label to a family it never calls Irish. In July 1861 two companies of the Brooklyn regiment known as the Fighting Fourteenth were posted as pickets at Arlington Mills, Virginia, with their headquarters in a miller's house.

The [regimental history](https://archive.org/details/cu31924095196717), published for the fiftieth anniversary of the regiment's muster in 1861, calls that house "a fair picture of the homes of the poor whites of the South," and its account of the family runs: "there were about ten children, Irish twins, i. e., about one year difference in their ages, and they were allowed to run wild, as dirty as pigs."

Nothing in the passage makes the miller's family Irish. Here the phrase is a sneer at a poor Southern household with children packed close together, whatever their ancestry.

## Most writers keep Irish twins inside a year

The writers Green's quotes mostly keep inside a year:

| Year | Where it appeared | Gap between the siblings |
|---|---|---|
| 1911 | [History of the Fighting Fourteenth](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq) | About one year |
| 1965 | [Los Angeles Times](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq) | Less than a year |
| 1976 | [Washington Post](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq) | Two days less than a year |
| 1985 | [*Happy Are the Meek*, by Andrew M. Greeley](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq) | Eleven months |
| 1989 | [A Pennsylvania newspaper, cited by Green's as the Pennsylvania Intelligencer](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq) | Anywhere from 10 to 18 months |
| 2016 | [Betrayal in Blue](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq) | About nine months |

So siblings 14 months apart fall outside the usual definition, though not outside every use of it. [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Irish_twin) stretches its own sense to siblings "born one year apart," and the Pennsylvania paper in 1989 compared children "as close as 10 to 18 months" to unequal twins, adding that "they are even called 'Irish twins'," as [Green's quotes it](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq).

Twelve months also leaves the second pregnancy very little room. Counted from fertilization, a pregnancy lasts [**about 38 weeks**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pregnancy), so a full-term baby born less than a year after an older sibling has to be conceived within **about 14 weeks** of that sibling's birth.

## The phrase reached Australia and Britain, and has a Catholic twin

The phrase has traveled. An Australian book of slang from 2021, *Betoota-isms*, gives Irish twins three senses, starting with "Male siblings born less than twelve months apart" and going on to "Local scallywags, often suspended or caught skipping school," according to [Green's](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq), which also cites a British use from 2024.

Another slang name for them is Catholic twins. [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Catholic_twins) defines it as "Siblings born within twelve months of each other," lists Irish twins as a synonym and quotes a 2008 novel by Shelly Laurenston about five children "who looked barely a year apart." Both names describe a family by the gap between births, the way [cousin terms](/blog/what-is-a-second-cousin/) describe one by counting generations.

The [Irish goodbye](/blog/what-is-an-irish-goodbye/), another American phrase that names the Irish, barely shows up in books before 2013, and its origin stories are guesses. Irish twins has a paper trail back to 1852, and the oldest pair on it were a newspaper and a baby called Nettie. Strictly FYI.
