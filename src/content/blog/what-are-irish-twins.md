You may have gone to school with a brother and sister who sat in the same grade without being twins, one born in September and the other the following August. Siblings like that have an old American nickname, Irish twins, and [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Irish_twin) tags it rare, slang and offensive while noting that it fits best when the pair are born "within the same calendar year or school year."

The usual story says the nickname began in the 19th century as a jibe at the size of Irish families. A slang dictionary has examples from that century to back up the date.

## What are Irish twins?

Irish twins are two siblings born less than **12 months** apart, from separate pregnancies, so for part of every year they are the same age. The phrase mocked the supposed fertility of Irish families, and [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Irish_twin) labels it offensive.

## The first Irish twins in the slang dictionary were a newspaper and a baby

[Green's Dictionary of Slang](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq) defines Irish twins as "two siblings born within a 12-month period," labels the phrase US and puts it down to the stereotypical fecundity, and lack of contraceptive practice, of traditional Irish families. The entry sits in a long list of slang built on the word Irish, which Green's prefaces with a warning that "all combs. with Irish should be assumed to be derog. (if seen as joc. by the coiner/speaker) unless otherwise stated." By that rule Irish twins is derogatory even when the speaker means it as a joke.

The quotations for Irish twins in [Green's Dictionary of Slang](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq) begin with a newspaper and a baby, in the **Louisville Daily Courier** of June 4, 1852. The writer's Irish twins were a newspaper, the Visitor, which had been "our only baby," and a daughter, for "baby Nettie has come," as Green's quotes the passage. Green's files it as a figurative use, for combining a job with childcare.

Green's does not name the writer, but her biographer does. Sylvia Hoffert's [*Jane Grey Swisshelm: An Unconventional Life*](https://archive.org/details/janegreyswisshel00hoff) quotes the same lines as the words of its subject, a Pittsburgh editor, and dates the birth of her daughter to the spring of 1852. The [Minnesota Historical Society](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/swisshelm-jane-grey-1815-1884) gives the year as 1851. The girl was Mary Henrietta, "who was called Zo or Nettie," according to [St. Cloud State University's archive record](https://stcloudstate.libraryhost.com/index.php/swisshelm-jane-grey-cannon-1815-1884) for her mother. Her paper was the "Pittsburg Saturday Visiter," spelled with an e, as she names it in [her 1880 autobiography, *Half a Century*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/12052/pg12052-images.html), where she also calls the girl "my only baby."

> "We find ourselves quite unequal to the task of managing our Irish twins." - Louisville Daily Courier, June 4, 1852, as quoted in [Green's Dictionary of Slang](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq)

## By 1887 a Kansas paper was joking about Irish twins

The next citation in Green's, from the **Leavenworth Times** in Kansas on June 5, 1887, sets English twins against Irish ones. Queen Victoria sent money to every English mother "on the occasion of her first twins," the paper claimed, and it went on: "Doubtless she does not feel that it is incumbent upon her to offer a dollar premium for Irish twins," as [Green's records](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq). Green's files it under the slang sense, though the excerpt alone does not show whether the paper meant siblings a year apart or twins born to Irish mothers.

Wiktionary's own etymology says the phrase was "Originally used (in the nineteenth century) to mock the fertility of Irish families," yet the one quotation on [its page](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Irish_twin) comes from a novel of 2014. The newspapers in Green's supply the 19th-century evidence that the page lacks, and they back its date.

## A Civil War regiment's history pinned it on a Virginia family

[The History of the Fighting Fourteenth](https://archive.org/details/cu31924095196717), a regimental history printed in Brooklyn in 1911, applied the label to a family it never calls Irish. On July 12, 1861, two companies of the Brooklyn regiment, H and C, went out on [picket duty](https://archive.org/details/cu31924095196717) with their headquarters at Arlington Mills, Virginia, in a miller's house.

The book, published for the fiftieth anniversary of the regiment's muster in 1861, calls that house "a fair picture of the homes of the poor whites of the South," and its account of the family runs: "there were about ten children, Irish twins, i. e., about one year difference in their ages, and they were allowed to run wild, as dirty as pigs." With no business for the mill, the family sold [meals to soldiers](https://archive.org/details/cu31924095196717) for **25 cents** and beer for 5 cents a glass.

Nothing in the passage makes the miller's family Irish. Here the phrase is a sneer at a poor Southern household with children packed close together, whatever their ancestry.

## Most writers keep Irish twins inside a year

Later writers quoted in [Green's](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq) mostly keep the gap inside a year. The Los Angeles Times in 1965 described a mother of seven whose pair were "born less than a year apart," a parent in the Washington Post in 1976 had sons "two days less than a year apart," a 1985 novel by Andrew M. Greeley had a brother and sister "born eleven months apart," and a 2016 book, *Betrayal in Blue*, had two teenage brothers "like nine months apart." A Pennsylvania newspaper in 1989 stretched it furthest, comparing children "as close as 10 to 18 months" to unequal twins and adding that "they are even called 'Irish twins'."

So siblings 14 months apart fall outside the usual definition, though not outside every use of it, and [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Irish_twin) even stretches its own sense to siblings "born one year apart."

Twelve months also leaves the second pregnancy very little room. In the 40th week of pregnancy "it has been 38 weeks since conception," as [MedlinePlus explains](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002398.htm), so a full-term baby born less than a year after an older sibling has to be conceived within **about 14 weeks** of that sibling's birth. That window opens early: "You can get pregnant 3 weeks after the birth of a baby," [the NHS warns](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/baby/support-and-services/sex-and-contraception-after-birth/), even while breastfeeding and before periods return.

## The phrase reached Australia and Britain, and has a Catholic twin

The phrase has traveled. [*Betoota-isms*](https://www.panmacmillan.com.au/9781760987749/), which its Australian publisher brought out in 2021 as a guide to "slang, nicknames and sayings" from The Betoota Advocate, gives Irish twins three senses, starting with "Male siblings born less than twelve months apart," going on to "Local scallywags, often suspended or caught skipping school" and ending with rhyming names such as Cayden and Jaiden, according to [Green's](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/oqjx6dq). Green's also quotes the phrase from *Empty Wigs*, a novel by the English writer [Jonathan Meades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Meades).

Another slang name for them is Catholic twins. [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Catholic_twins) defines it as "Siblings born within twelve months of each other," lists Irish twins as a synonym and quotes a 2008 novel by Shelly Laurenston describing five children "who looked barely a year apart."

Irish twins has a paper trail older than the Civil War, and the oldest pair on it were a newspaper and a baby called Nettie. Strictly FYI.
