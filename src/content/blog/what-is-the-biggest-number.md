Agustin Rayo wrote thirty or forty ones in a row, and Adam Elga took an eraser to them. It was **January 26, 2007**, in a room at MIT's Stata Center so packed that people stood on chairs, and the two philosophers were fighting a [Big Number Duel](https://web.mit.edu/arayo/www/bignums.html): take turns writing numbers on the board, and the biggest wins.

Elga, who taught at Princeton, erased a thin line across all of the ones but the first two, so that each of the rest became an exclamation mark, the sign for a factorial. "Eleven factorial, factorial, factorial," he declared, in [Rayo's account](http://web.mit.edu/arayo/www/brink-duel.pdf). Eleven factorial, 11 times 10 times 9 and so on down to 1, is **39,916,800**, and the factorial of that already runs to about **286 million digits**.

Infinity was [banned](http://tech.mit.edu/V126/N64/64largenumber.html). A "gentleman's agreement" also made it unsporting to win by adding one to the other side's last entry. The two men wanted, in Rayo's words, "a war of originality, not a war of patience."

## What is the biggest number?

There is **no biggest number** in the world, and no highest number either. Add 1 to any number, however huge, and you get a bigger one, so the counting numbers never end. Infinity does not win, because it is not a number you can reach by counting. Named giants run from the googol to Graham's number and beyond.

| Number | How big | Where it came from |
|---|---|---|
| Googol | 1 followed by 100 zeros, or 10^100 | Named by Edward Kasner's nine-year-old nephew; in print by 1938 |
| Googolplex | 1 followed by a googol of zeros | Named by the same boy |
| Graham's number | Takes 64 layers of arrow notation to describe | Ronald Graham's upper limit for a puzzle about cubes, made public by Martin Gardner in 1977 |
| TREE(3) | Makes Graham's number look "puny" | Harvey Friedman's game with trees, described in 2006 |
| Rayo's number | Beats any number set theory can name in fewer than a googol symbols | The winning entry in the 2007 duel |

## A nine-year-old named the googol on a walk in the woods

Edward Kasner, a [professor at Columbia University from 1910 until he retired in 1949](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Kasner/), wanted a name for 1 followed by a hundred zeros. "I was walking in the woods with my nephew one day, and I asked the boy to think up any name for the number, any amusing name that entered his head," he wrote in a January 1938 article, as quoted by Ivan Panchenko, a contributor to [Jeff Miller's history of mathematical words](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Miller/mathword/g/).

The boy said **googol**, and at the same time named a bigger number, the **googolplex**. Its first definition, Kasner and James Newman wrote in their 1940 book [*Mathematics and the Imagination*](https://archive.org/details/mathematics-and-the-imagination-edward-kasner-james-newman-1940-2), was 1 followed by zeros "until you got tired," which would not do, because "different people get tired at different times."

The googolplex became 1 followed by a googol of zeros, more than you could write "if you went to the farthest star, touring all the nebulae and putting down zeros every inch of the way." A googol alone is more than the number of atoms in the observable universe. The [1980 *Guinness Book of World Records*](https://web.archive.org/web/20131019135451/http://iteror.org/big/Source/Graham-Gardner/GrahamsNumber.html) put that count, in some models, at probably no more than **10^85**.

> "He was very certain that this number was not infinite, and therefore equally certain that it had to have a name." - Edward Kasner and James Newman, on the boy who named the googol

Google took its name from the number. "10^100 (a gigantic number) is a googol, but we liked the spelling 'Google' better," the company's [1999 website](https://web.archive.org/web/19990221202430/http://www.google.com/company.html) said.

## The usual 1938 date is when the word reached print

According to [Miller's entry](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Miller/mathword/g/), Merriam-Webster names the boy as Milton Sirotta, "b. about 1929," and the naming is often dated to the late 1930s. Kasner's relatives told *The Wall Street Journal* in 2004 that it happened closer to 1920, the year Milton turned nine, and a Social Security record shows a Milton Sirotta born on **March 8, 1911**.

Panchenko suggests that the 1938 date is the article's publication year and that the 1929 birth year came from subtracting nine. The credit is disputed too: the daughter of another nephew, Edwin Sirotta, told the *Journal* that, by her father's account, "They both came up with it." As with [rafter, for a group of turkeys](/blog/what-is-a-group-of-turkeys-called/), the first printed use is firmer evidence than the stories told about when the word was coined.

## Graham's number once held the record for the biggest number in a proof

Graham's number reached the public in November 1977, in Martin Gardner's ["Mathematical Games" column](https://web.archive.org/web/20131019135451/http://iteror.org/big/Source/Graham-Gardner/GrahamsNumber.html) in *Scientific American*. It comes from a puzzle: join every pair of corners of a cube in many dimensions with a line, color each line red or blue, and ask how many dimensions it takes before four corners lying on one flat plane must have all six of their lines in the same color.

In an unpublished proof, Gardner wrote, Ronald Graham, head of discrete mathematics at Bell Laboratories, had found an upper limit "so vast that it holds the record for the largest number ever used in a serious mathematical proof." Describing it takes **64 layers** of a special arrow notation. The [1980 *Guinness Book of World Records*](https://web.archive.org/web/20131019135451/http://iteror.org/big/Source/Graham-Gardner/GrahamsNumber.html) called it "the highest number ever used in a mathematical proof."

Nobody can write it out in ordinary digits, yet its ending is known. Graham's number is a tower of 3s, and the [On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences](https://oeis.org/A133613) lists its final digits, which close with **2464195387**.

The answer itself could be tiny by comparison, since Gardner also reported that "Ramsey-theory experts believe the actual Ramsey number for this problem is probably 6." In a [2008 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/0811.1055), Jerome Barkley raised the proven minimum to **13**. The ceiling has fallen too. In 2014, Lavrov, Lee and Mackey pulled the upper limit far below Graham's number, and a [2019 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.05617) by Eryk Lipka lowered it again.

## TREE(3) makes Graham's number look puny

In March 2006, the Ohio State mathematician Harvey Friedman described a far bigger number that comes from a game with trees, drawings of dots joined by lines with no loops. Each dot gets one of three colors. The first tree may have at most one dot, the second at most two, and so on, and no tree may contain an earlier one.

A finite form of Kruskal's tree theorem guarantees that the game cannot go on forever, so the longest possible game has a finite length, called **TREE(3)**. In a [post to the Foundations of Mathematics mailing list](https://web.archive.org/web/20240918084441/https://cs.nyu.edu/pipermail/fom/2006-March/010279.html), Friedman wrote that Graham's number "REMAINS quite important, despite its PUNY size."

## Rayo's number won the duel by describing a number

Near the end of the 2007 duel, MIT's newspaper [*The Tech*](http://tech.mit.edu/V126/N64/64largenumber.html) reported, Rayo "furiously scribbled" on the whiteboard: "The smallest number bigger than any number that can be named by an expression in the language of first order set-theory with less than a googol (10^100) symbols."

The rules barred words like "named," so Rayo rebuilt the idea in formal logic, with what he called a "second order logic trick," and it "soon won him the duel." Elga, playing a character called Dr. Evil, fell to his knees and whispered, "I've been crushed." By April 2007, The Math Factor, a University of Arkansas podcast, was calling the entry [Rayo's number](https://strauss.hosted.uark.edu/mathfactor_site/mathfactor.uark.edu/2007/04/ch-rayos-number/).

As with [the longest word in the world](/blog/what-is-the-longest-word-in-the-world/), the record depends on the rules. Rayo notes in his book, [*On the Brink of Paradox*](http://web.mit.edu/arayo/www/brink-duel.pdf), that a more powerful logical language would name a bigger number still.

## Infinity is not the biggest number, because counting never reaches it

Answering infinity mixes up two different ideas. "Above everything, we must realize that 'very big' and 'infinite' are entirely different," Kasner and Newman wrote. "Any number which may be named, or conceived of in terms of the integers is finite."

The mix-up is old. In the introduction to *The Sand Reckoner*, which Kasner and Newman quote, Archimedes told King Gelon that some people "think that the number of the sand is infinite in multitude." He then set out to show that numbers he had named were bigger than the grains in a mass of sand the size of the universe.

The computer scientist Scott Aaronson's essay [*Who Can Name the Bigger Number?*](https://www.scottaaronson.com/writings/bignumbers.html), which prompted Elga's challenge, makes the same split. Infinite collections do come in sizes: Georg Cantor showed that there are more real numbers between 0 and 1 than there are whole numbers, as Kasner and Newman explain.

"Just as there's no biggest number," Aaronson writes, "so too is there no biggest infinity."

Rayo's winning number still has a googol inside it, the word a nine-year-old made up on a walk in the woods. Like every number before it, it loses to itself plus 1. Strictly FYI.
