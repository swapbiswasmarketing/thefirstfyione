Agustin Rayo wrote [thirty or forty ones](http://web.mit.edu/arayo/www/brink-duel.pdf) in a row, and Adam Elga took an eraser to them. It was **January 26, 2007**, in a room at MIT's Stata Center so packed that [people stood on chairs](http://tech.mit.edu/V126/N64/64largenumber.html), and the two philosophers were fighting a [Big Number Duel](https://web.mit.edu/arayo/www/bignums.html): take turns writing numbers on the board, and the biggest wins.

Elga, who taught at Princeton, erased a line across all of the ones but the first two, turning the rest into exclamation marks, the sign for a factorial, and declared, "Eleven factorial, factorial, factorial," in [Rayo's account](http://web.mit.edu/arayo/www/brink-duel.pdf). Eleven factorial, 11 times 10 times 9 and so on down to 1, is **39,916,800**, and the factorial of that already runs to about **286 million digits**.

MIT's student newspaper, [*The Tech*](http://tech.mit.edu/V126/N64/64largenumber.html), saw the opening a little differently: Elga went first by writing a single 1, and his eraser later turned half of Rayo's ones into factorials.

Only finite numbers counted, and a "gentleman's agreement" made it unsporting to win by adding one to the other side's last entry, according to [Rayo's page on the duel](https://web.mit.edu/arayo/www/bignums.html). The two men wanted, in [Rayo's words](http://web.mit.edu/arayo/www/brink-duel.pdf), "a war of originality, not a war of patience".

## What is the biggest number?

There is **no biggest number**. Add 1 to any number, however huge, and you get a bigger one, so the counting numbers never end. Infinity does not win, because it is not a number you can reach by counting. Named giants run from the googol to Graham's number and beyond.

| Number | How big | Where it came from |
|---|---|---|
| Googol | 1 followed by 100 zeros, or 10^100 | Named by Edward Kasner's nine-year-old nephew; in print by 1938 |
| Googolplex | 1 followed by a googol of zeros | Named by the same boy |
| Graham's number | Takes 64 layers of arrow notation to describe | Ronald Graham's upper limit for a puzzle about cubes, made public by Martin Gardner in 1977 |
| TREE(3) | Makes Graham's number look "puny" | Harvey Friedman's sequences of trees, defined in a 2006 mailing-list post |
| Rayo's number | Beats any number set theory can name in fewer than a googol symbols | The winning entry in the 2007 duel |

## A nine-year-old named the googol on a walk in the woods

Edward Kasner, a [full professor at Columbia University from 1910 until he retired in 1949](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Kasner/), wanted a name for 1 followed by a hundred zeros. "I was walking in the woods with my nephew one day, and I asked the boy to think up any name for the number, any amusing name that entered his head," he wrote in a January 1938 article, as quoted by Ivan Panchenko, a contributor to [Jeff Miller's history of mathematical words](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Miller/mathword/g/).

The boy said **googol**, and at the same time named a bigger number, the **googolplex**. Its first definition, Kasner and James Newman wrote in their 1940 book [*Mathematics and the Imagination*](https://archive.org/details/mathematicsimagi00kasnrich), was 1 followed by zeros "until you got tired," which would not do, because "different people get tired at different times."

The googolplex became 1 followed by a googol of zeros, a number so long that, [they wrote](https://archive.org/details/mathematicsimagi00kasnrich), "there would not be enough room to write it, if you went to the farthest star, touring all the nebulae and putting down zeros every inch of the way." A googol alone is more than the number of atoms in the observable universe, which the [1980 *Guinness Book of World Records*](https://web.archive.org/web/20131019135451/http://iteror.org/big/Source/Graham-Gardner/GrahamsNumber.html) put, in some models, at probably no more than **10^85**.

> "He was very certain that this number was not infinite, and therefore equally certain that it had to have a name." - Edward Kasner and James Newman, on the boy who named the googol, as quoted by [MacTutor](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Kasner/)

Google took its name from the number. "10^100 (a gigantic number) is a googol, but we liked the spelling 'Google' better," the company's [1999 website](https://web.archive.org/web/19990221202430/http://www.google.com/company.html) said.

## The usual 1938 date may only mark when the word reached print

According to [Miller's entry](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Miller/mathword/g/), Merriam-Webster names the boy as Milton Sirotta, "b. about 1929," and the naming is often dated to the late 1930s. Kasner's relatives told *The Wall Street Journal* in 2004 that it happened closer to 1920, the year Milton turned nine, and a Social Security record shows a Milton Sirotta born on **March 8, 1911**.

[Panchenko suggests](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Miller/mathword/g/) that the 1938 date is the article's publication year and that the 1929 birth year came from subtracting nine. The credit is disputed too: the daughter of another nephew, Edwin Sirotta, told the *Journal* that, by her father's account, "They both came up with it," in a quotation [Panchenko supplied](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Miller/mathword/g/). The 1938 printing is firmer evidence than the stories told about when the word was coined.

## Graham's number once held the record for the biggest number in a proof

Graham's number reached the public in November 1977, when Martin Gardner reported in his ["Mathematical Games" column](https://web.archive.org/web/20131019135451/http://iteror.org/big/Source/Graham-Gardner/GrahamsNumber.html) in *Scientific American* that Ronald Graham, head of discrete mathematics at Bell Laboratories, had found in an unpublished proof an upper limit "so vast that it holds the record for the largest number ever used in a serious mathematical proof." It comes from a puzzle: join every pair of corners of a cube in many dimensions with a line, color each line red or blue, and ask how many dimensions it takes before four corners lying on one flat plane must have all six of their lines in the same color.

Describing it takes **64 layers** of a special arrow notation, and the [1980 *Guinness Book of World Records*](https://web.archive.org/web/20131019135451/http://iteror.org/big/Source/Graham-Gardner/GrahamsNumber.html) called it "the highest number ever used in a mathematical proof." Gardner's figure was not the bound Graham and Bruce Rothschild first gave for the problem: theirs fed 12 through a fast-growing function seven times, as Eryk Lipka recalls in a [2019 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.05617).

Nobody can write it out in ordinary digits, yet its ending is known. Graham's number is a tower of 3s, and the [On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences](https://oeis.org/A133613) lists its final digits, which end in **4195387**.

The answer itself could be tiny by comparison, since [Gardner also reported](https://web.archive.org/web/20131019135451/http://iteror.org/big/Source/Graham-Gardner/GrahamsNumber.html) that "Ramsey-theory experts believe the actual Ramsey number for this problem is probably 6." That guess has since been ruled out: the proven minimum rose to 11, and in a [2008 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/0811.1055) Jerome Barkley raised it to **13**. The ceiling has fallen too. In 2014, Lavrov, Lee and Mackey pulled the upper limit far below Graham's number, and Lipka [lowered it again in 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.05617).

## TREE(3) makes Graham's number look puny

In a [March 2006 post to the Foundations of Mathematics mailing list](https://web.archive.org/web/20240918084441/https://cs.nyu.edu/pipermail/fom/2006-March/010279.html), the Ohio State mathematician Harvey Friedman described a far bigger number that comes from a game with trees, drawings of dots joined by lines with no loops. Each dot gets one of three colors. Each tree may have at most as many dots as its place in the line, and no tree may contain an earlier one.

A finite form of Kruskal's tree theorem guarantees that the game cannot go on forever, so the longest sequence of trees it allows has a finite length: **TREE(3)**, written TREE[3] in [Friedman's notation](https://web.archive.org/web/20240918084441/https://cs.nyu.edu/pipermail/fom/2006-March/010279.html). Graham's number, he [wrote](https://web.archive.org/web/20240918084441/https://cs.nyu.edu/pipermail/fom/2006-March/010279.html), "REMAINS quite important, despite its PUNY size."

## Rayo's number won the duel by describing a number

Near the end of the 2007 duel, Rayo "furiously scribbled" on the whiteboard a description of "the smallest number bigger than any number that can be named by an expression in the language of first order set-theory with less than a googol" symbols, [*The Tech*](http://tech.mit.edu/V126/N64/64largenumber.html) reported.

Words like "named" were [against the rules](https://web.mit.edu/arayo/www/bignums.html), so the entry that won swapped that word for a formula of second-order set theory that does the same job without it. Elga, playing a character called Dr. Evil, fell to his knees and whispered, "I've been crushed," after what Rayo called his ["second order logic trick"](http://tech.mit.edu/V126/N64/64largenumber.html) carried the day. By April 2007, The Math Factor, a University of Arkansas podcast, was calling the entry [Rayo's number](https://web.archive.org/web/20090822082710/http://mathfactor.uark.edu/2007/04/ch-rayos-number/).

The record depends on the rules. Rayo notes in his book, [*On the Brink of Paradox*](http://web.mit.edu/arayo/www/brink-duel.pdf), that a more powerful logical language would name a bigger number still.

## Infinity is not the biggest number, because counting never reaches it

Answering infinity mixes up two different ideas. Kasner and Newman put it plainly: "Above everything, we must realize that 'very big' and 'infinite' are entirely different," and, in [a footnote](https://archive.org/details/mathematicsimagi00kasnrich), "Any number which can be named, or conceived of in terms of the integers is finite."

The mix-up is old. In the introduction to *The Sand Reckoner*, Archimedes told King Gelon that some people "think that the number of the sand is infinite in multitude," in [Thomas Heath's 1897 translation](https://archive.org/details/worksofarchimede00arch). He then [showed](https://archive.org/details/worksofarchimede00arch) that a sphere as big as the one Aristarchus proposed for the fixed stars would hold a number of grains "less than 10,000,000 units of the eighth order of numbers," which is 10^63 in modern notation.

The computer scientist Scott Aaronson's essay [*Who Can Name the Bigger Number?*](https://www.scottaaronson.com/writings/bignumbers.html), which gave Rayo and Elga [the idea for the duel](https://web.mit.edu/arayo/www/bignums.html), makes the same split: infinity "is fascinating and elegant, but it's not a whole number." Infinite collections do come in sizes: Georg Cantor showed that there are more real numbers between 0 and 1 than there are whole numbers, as [Kasner and Newman explain](https://archive.org/details/mathematicsimagi00kasnrich).

"Just as there's no biggest number," [Aaronson writes](https://www.scottaaronson.com/writings/bignumbers.html), "so too is there no biggest infinity."

Rayo's winning number still has a googol inside it, the word a nine-year-old made up on a walk in the woods. Like every number before it, it loses to itself plus 1. Strictly FYI.
