The animals that first taste coffee in a Latin treatise printed in Rome in **1671** are camels, unless they are goats. Antonio Fausto Naironi's herdsman is "quidam Camelorum, seu vt alij aiunt, Caprarum Custos," a certain keeper of camels or, as others say, of goats, and on page 15 Naironi sets the story "in Ayaman Regione, quae est Arabia Felix," in Yemen.

The book's full title is [*De saluberrima potione Cahue, seu Cafe nuncupata discursus*](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_XqtX_0BdDbwC/page/n16), a discourse on the most healthful drink called Cahue, or Cafe, and Naironi was a Maronite who lectured on Syriac in Rome. In his account the herdsman complains to the monks of a monastery that his animals stay awake all night, leaping about.

The prior finds the shrubs whose berries they have been eating, boils some in water, tries the drink himself and has his monks take it daily to stay alert for their night prayers. Naironi never names the herdsman. Modern tellings call him Kaldi and move him, with only his goats, to the Ethiopian plateau.

## Where does coffee come from?

Coffee is brewed from the roasted seeds, or beans, of Coffea shrubs, and the main species, arabica, grows wild in the highland forests of **southwestern Ethiopia**. The drink is first reliably recorded in **Yemen**, among Sufis of the mid-1400s. Today **Brazil** grows the most coffee, nearly **40 percent** of the world's crop, by a July 2026 USDA forecast.

That puts the plant and the drink on opposite sides of the Red Sea, a split coffee shares with [sushi, first recorded in China](/blog/where-did-sushi-originate/) and turned into nigiri in Edo, now Tokyo.

## The plant is a wild Ethiopian hybrid

A [2012 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3492365/) by botanists at the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, and Ethiopia's Environment and Coffee Forest Forum places arabica's largest and most diverse wild populations in the highlands of southwestern Ethiopia, with others in the Bale Mountains and smaller outposts on South Sudan's Boma Plateau and Kenya's Mount Marsabit. Wild arabica grows between **950 and 1,950 meters** up, almost entirely in moist evergreen Afromontane forest and transitional rain forest.

The species began as a cross. A [2024 genome study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11018527/) in *Nature Genetics* describes arabica as a natural hybrid of the ancestors of **Coffea canephora**, sold as robusta, and **Coffea eugenioides**. It finds evidence that the founding event happened **350,000 to 610,000 years ago**; earlier estimates had ranged from 10,000 to 1 million years.

The paper calls Ethiopia "the center of origin of Arabica" and credits the species with about **60 percent** of the world's coffee products (the USDA's 2026/27 forecast puts arabica at about 56 percent of the harvest). Robusta's wild range is far wider, covering much of tropical Africa from Guinea to western Tanzania, according to the 2012 study.

## The drink first turns up among Sufis in Yemen

"Coffee came into general use in the lands of Islam sometime in the mid-fifteenth century," Ralph Hattox writes in [*Coffee and Coffeehouses*](https://archive.org/details/coffeecoffeehous0000hatt), his history of the drink's origins in the medieval Near East. In Yemen, he notes, it was used by "Sufi shaykhs and others to help them stay awake during their devotional exercises."

The Arabic accounts Hattox works from credit **al-Dhabhani**, a Sufi of Aden who died in 1470 or 1471, with introducing coffee there. Hattox accepts that as possible but is less sure of the trip to Ethiopia where, the accounts say, al-Dhabhani saw it in use: "if indeed he went there."

Arabic named the bean and the drink separately. William Ukers, in his 1922 book [*All About Coffee*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/28500/pg28500.txt), describes qahwah as "the name, not of the plant, but the beverage," originally a word for wine, while the plant and berry were *bunn*. Naironi's Cahue is an early European spelling of qahwah, and the 1710 English translation of his book spells out the split: "This Fruit has Two Names, one is the Name of the Berry, the other of the Drink made of it."

Europe took the word "about 1600" from qahwah, Ukers adds, "not directly, but through its Turkish form, *kahveh*." A link to Kaffa, a town in southwestern Ethiopia, has been suggested, but, he reports, "of this there is no evidence."

## The goatherd is a legend Naironi set in Yemen

The US [National Coffee Association](https://www.aboutcoffee.org/origins/history-of-coffee/) tells the familiar version under a heading for the ninth century on the Ethiopian plateau: "According to legend, a goat herder named Kaldi was the first person who discovered coffee beans and their benefits."

Naironi's telling differs on the animals, the country and the name, and he passes it on as "the common Tradition amongst the Eastern People," in the words of the [1710 translation](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_a-discourse-on-coffee-i_naironus-antonius-faust_1710/page/n9). His inventors are Christian monks, and he reports that the Turks who served coffee prayed daily for "Sciadli and Aidrus, which they believe are the Names of the Monks."

The name Kaldi turns up in what Ukers called a "picturesque version" that "the French have preserved," in which "a young goatherd named Kaldi" watches his goats prance. Even that Kaldi ends up "the happiest herder in happy Arabia." Ukers's own summary of "the most popular legend" puts the herdsman "in upper Egypt, or Abyssinia," an older name for Ethiopia.

The French orientalist Antoine Galland had objected in **1699**, in his account of an Arabic manuscript on coffee. In Ukers's summary, Galland found the legend "unworthy of belief as facts of history" yet allowed it "some truth." Eastern Christians, he argued, had wanted the honor of inventing coffee, "for the abbot, or prior, of the convent and his companions are only the mufti Gemaleddin and Muhammid Alhadrami, and the monks are the dervishes." Ukers identifies Gemaleddin as the mufti of Aden "surnamed Aldhabani," the same al-Dhabhani.

Hattox files the herding tales among the embellishments of "European writers of the seventeenth century," such as "the tale of the shepherd who noticed the uncommon vigor of his sheep." He adds: "It is far from clear where they got such tales."

> "[T]he Arabs who wrote on coffee would have none of this sort of legend, be it of Arab or Western inspiration." - Ralph Hattox, *Coffee and Coffeehouses*

Modern tellings set the goatherd in the **ninth century**, about **800 years** before Naironi's book, which itself appeared some two centuries after the first reliable record of coffee drinking. Coffee origin claims are hard to settle even within living memory: [Sydney and Wellington both claim the flat white, and neither has proved it](/blog/what-is-a-flat-white/).

## Modern arabica varieties trace back to Yemen

Arabica farming began in Yemen too: cultivation "was initiated in fifteenth- to sixteenth-century Yemen," the genome study says, though it also mentions "ancient cultivated Ethiopian landraces." Around 1600 the "so-called seven seeds" were smuggled out of Yemen to found India's arabica lines.

A century later the Dutch began growing arabica in Southeast Asia, founding the Typica group, and **one plant** shipped to Amsterdam in **1706** started arabica farming in the Caribbean in 1723. On the island of Bourbon, now Reunion, where the French grew it, the descendants of **a single plant** that survived by 1720 became the Bourbon group.

Today's cultivars descend from those two lines, which the genome data show share Yemeni origins, "except for a few wild ecotypes with origins in natural forests in Ethiopia."

## Brazil now grows nearly 40 percent of the world's coffee

The US Department of Agriculture's [July 2026 forecast](https://apps.fas.usda.gov/psdonline/circulars/coffee.pdf) puts Brazil's 2026/27 harvest at a record **71.9 million** 60-kilogram bags and says Brazil "accounts for nearly 40 percent of world production." Ethiopia, arabica's center of origin, ranks fourth.

| Grower | 2026/27 forecast (million 60-kg bags) | Share of world total |
|---|---|---|
| Brazil | 71.9 | 38% |
| Vietnam | 32.5 | 17% |
| Colombia | 13.4 | 7% |
| Ethiopia | 12.1 | 6% |
| Indonesia | 11.4 | 6% |
| World | 189.7 | 100% |

Vietnam's crop is almost all robusta, **31.4 million** of its 32.5 million bags. Worldwide, the forecast splits into 105.9 million bags of arabica and 83.8 million of robusta.

Take the camels and goats out of Naironi's story and what remains is close to the history: men in Yemen drinking coffee to stay awake for night prayers. The Arabic accounts call them Sufis; the tradition Naironi recorded made them monks. Strictly FYI.
