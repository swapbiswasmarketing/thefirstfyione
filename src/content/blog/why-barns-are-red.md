Drive through farm country in New England, Pennsylvania or the Swedish countryside and the barns are red. The same dull, rusty red, again and again, in places that never coordinated it.

The usual explanation is that red paint was cheap, and the paper trail mostly agrees. What that leaves out is where the red came from: a waste heap at a Swedish copper mine, a pail of skimmed milk, and an element forged in exploding stars.

## Why are barns red?

**Barns are red because the paint farmers could afford was coloured with red iron oxide, the iron compound that also colours rust.** [Iron oxides have been used as yellow, red and brown pigments since prehistoric times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron).

Sweden's best-known red came from the waste of one copper mine at Falun. American farmers stirred bought iron-oxide pigments into skimmed milk and oil, then switched to ready-made barn paint when factories sold it for less than house paint. The colour came with the price.

## Sweden painted its barns with copper-mine waste

Falu red started as a by-product. [By the 16th century the tailings and slag piled around the Falun mine had begun to weather into a sludge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falu_red) rich in copper, limonite, silicic acid and zinc.

An early customer was a king. In 1573 Sweden's King Johan III told his master painter, by letter, to paint every roof of his Stockholm palace red with waste from the Falun mine, the "mine bran", [mixed with linseed oil and tar so the roofs would pass for copper](https://falurodfarg.com/en/about/the-story-of-the-red-cottage/).

Nobles copied the king, then priests and officers copied the nobles, with the brick houses of continental Europe as the model. As the paint company's [history of the red cottage](https://falurodfarg.com/en/about/the-story-of-the-red-cottage/) puts it, "Red log houses were signs of wealth and status." [The fashion reached miners' and farming estates in the 18th century, and in the 19th century farmers began painting their homes and barns on a large scale](https://falurodfarg.com/en/about/the-story-of-the-red-cottage/), followed by soldiers and crofters.

The colour is cooked, not dug. [Burned at 500 degrees, the yellow limonite in the mine waste turns into iron oxide and goes light red](https://falurodfarg.com/en/about/the-unique-pigment/); at 700 degrees it darkens, and above 950 degrees it turns black.

Paint has been made at the mine [since 1764, and the company's Swedish history dates the practice of boiling it with water and **rye flour** to the decade that followed](https://falurodfarg.com/om-falu-rodfarg/historien-om-falu-rodfarg/). [The name Falu Rodfarg may only be used on paint containing pigment from the Falu Mine](https://falurodfarg.com/en/about/).

## American farmers stirred it into skimmed milk

According to [Washington State's Department of Archaeology and Historic Preservation](https://dahp.wa.gov/barn-paint-colors), the early farmers who settled New England left most of their barns unpainted, and by the late 1700s farmers were mixing their own coatings from **skimmed milk, lime and red iron oxide**, with linseed oil added later.

The recipes survive. [The New England Farmer of September 12, 1828](https://archive.org/details/sim_new-england-farmer-and-horticultural-register_1828-09-12_7_8) printed one for "painting in milk": skimmed milk, slaked lime, linseed oil, and two iron-oxide pigments, Spanish brown and Venetian red.

The pigments in those recipes came from the paint trade, not from rust scraped off old iron. [The paint maker John Masury described Venetian red in 1868](https://archive.org/details/john-w.-masury-son-18680001) as "an artificial ochre, prepared from sulphate of iron," made mostly in England near Newcastle, and Spanish brown as "a coarse, native ochre, of dull brown color". He also admired Venetian red's staying power: "The bright red stain will remain on a building painted with it, long after the last vestige of the oil has disappeared."

He did not admire the look. "Red-browns and chocolate colors are altogether unsuited to the painting of country houses," [Masury wrote](https://archive.org/details/john-w.-masury-son-18680001), and he called "a dirty-brown earth, mixed in skimmed milk" a cheap and nasty compromise, worse than leaving the wood to weather.

Recipe books had already printed the opposite advice. [Dr. Chase's Recipes, published at Ann Arbor in 1865](https://archive.org/details/101501551.nlm.nih.gov), gives a milk paint for barns that "is too cheap to estimate," and for a red it says to "mix Venetian-red with milk, not using any lime. It looks well for fifteen years." [A mechanics' handbook of 1890](https://archive.org/details/englishamericanm00vanc_0) was still printing the same recipe, nearly word for word.

## Then factories sold it by the barrel

[In 1856 Robert Prince found an iron-rich outcrop near Lehigh Gap, Pennsylvania](https://archive.org/details/mineralpigmentso00mill), and began grinding it at his mill. [His company's 1895 pamphlet](https://archive.org/details/rustingofironste00prin) says Prince's Metallic Paint was first sold from 1858, at five cents a pound in barrel lots.

[The state's 1911 survey of mineral pigments](https://archive.org/details/mineralpigmentso00mill) also quotes an earlier geological report on a brown hematite from Monroe County that was once hauled to Weissport and used extensively in "mineral paint for barns, bridges, freight cars".

The trade was frank about why. [The same pamphlet](https://archive.org/details/rustingofironste00prin) reprints an 1892 article saying iron-oxide browns were used "by farmers in painting barns and outhouses," and conceding: "While their color is anything but beautiful, they are very durable and economical."

> In America the colour was a price tag: the paint a farmer could afford was coloured with red iron oxide.

Washington's preservation office says that by the mid to late 1800s [manufacturers sold special barn paints made from metallic oxides at a much lower price per gallon than house paint](https://dahp.wa.gov/barn-paint-colors), offered in a limited range, red being the most popular. It counts nearly half of the 600 barns on its Heritage Barn register as red, now or originally.

## The mould and white-barn stories have no paper trail

Another popular explanation is that rust kills mould. [Washington's office repeats it too](https://dahp.wa.gov/barn-paint-colors), saying rust "was known to kill many fungi, including mold and moss," but it names no source, and the period recipes quoted above sell their paint on price and durability, never on fungus. Treat it as an often-repeated story that none of these sources backs with evidence.

The same page offers a second theory, that wealthy farmers added blood from a recent slaughter to the oil. Blood paint itself existed: [a 19th-century American poultry paper](https://archive.org/details/poultrykeeper01unse) noted that "a mixture of lime and bullock's blood (fresh) makes an excellent water-proof paint." But no recipe in the period sources here puts blood in barn paint, and none of them ties blood to the colour of barns.

The idea that a white barn advertised a rich farmer is unsourced as well. [In 17th- and 18th-century Sweden the status colour was red](https://falurodfarg.com/en/about/the-story-of-the-red-cottage/), and Washington's office tells the American story the other way round, again without a source: "Red was the color of favor until [whitewash became cheaper](https://dahp.wa.gov/barn-paint-colors), at which point white barns began to spring up."

## The iron itself came out of exploding stars

Iron oxide was cheap because iron is common: [it is the fourth most abundant element in Earth's crust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron), about 5% of its mass.

Massive stars fuse light elements into heavier ones, layer by layer, up to iron, where the process stops paying. As NASA's [guide to our cosmic connection to the elements](https://imagine.gsfc.nasa.gov/educators/elements/imagine/05.html) puts it, "Unlike the elements before it, iron releases no energy when fused."

With no energy left to hold it up, the iron core collapses, and [in less than a second a neutron star, or a black hole if the star is massive enough, forms](https://chandra.harvard.edu/xray_sources/supernovas.html) while the outer layers are blown into space. Most of Earth's iron came from a smaller kind of star, though: exploding white dwarfs, [the leftover cores of sun-like stars](https://chandra.harvard.edu/xray_sources/supernovas.html), [created roughly two thirds of the iron in Earth's core and in your blood](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/little-star-supernovae-that-could-dwarf-stars/).

Much of it was not even born as iron. [The most common iron isotope, iron-56, starts out as radioactive nickel-56](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron) made in supernovae, which decays through cobalt-56 into stable iron. That scattered iron later gathered into planets, ore, ochre and, eventually, a bucket of barn paint.

Every red barn on every roadside is painted with iron that came out of exploding stars, chosen by someone who wanted the boards to last another winter for as little money as possible. Strictly FYI.
