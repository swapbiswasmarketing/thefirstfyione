A yawn, the familiar explanation goes, is the body topping up its air. Breathing grows shallow when you are tired or bored, carbon dioxide builds up in the blood, and one long gulp brings in the oxygen to set things right.

The authors of a 2014 study of yawning in Vienna wrote that ["it is commonly believed that yawns serve a respiratory function"](https://baillement.com/dossier/gallup_thermal_window.html), then added that experiments had shown yawn frequency to be "independent of brain/blood levels of O2 and CO2." The study behind that line was published in 1987, and the field has been arguing about the alternatives ever since.

## The answer is still open, with two hypotheses out in front

**Nobody has shown for certain why we yawn**, and the oxygen explanation is the one that was put to a direct test and failed it. Of the hypotheses still standing, the sharpest argument has been between two.

One says a yawn helps cool the brain, a case Andrew Gallup and Omar Eldakar set out in a 2013 review, which also notes that in humans yawning ["occurs most often before sleep onset and after waking"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3534187/). The other says its main work is social, and a 2010 review by Guggisberg and colleagues found growing support for a communicative role in [passing on drowsiness, boredom or mild psychological stress](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20382180/).

## The oxygen idea failed its test in 1987

In 1987, in the journal Behavioral and Neural Biology, Provine, Tate and Geldmacher took what they called the ["commonly cited but previously untested hypothesis"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3120687/) and tested it on college-age volunteers. The subjects breathed **100% oxygen**, gas mixtures with **3 or 5% carbon dioxide**, or compressed air as the control. If yawning answers a build-up of carbon dioxide, the CO2 mixtures should have raised the yawning rate, and if it answers a shortage of oxygen, pure oxygen should have suppressed it.

Neither happened. Breathing pure oxygen or CO2-rich air had [no significant effect on yawning](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3120687/) in Provine's 1987 study, even though both raised the breathing rate. In a second study, exercise hard enough to **double the breathing rate** left yawning unchanged.

> "The two studies suggest that yawning does not serve a primary respiratory function and that yawning and breathing are triggered by different internal states and are controlled by separate mechanisms." - Provine, Tate and Geldmacher, [Behavioral and Neural Biology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3120687/), 1987

## Cooling the head or neck cuts down contagious yawning

The brain-cooling case began with a 2007 experiment by Gallup and Gallup. Nasal breathing and forehead cooling are both known to lower brain temperature, and when shown a contagious yawning stimulus, participants doing either [showed a reduced tendency to yawn](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3534187/), the 2013 review says.

In four rats fitted with brain temperature probes, a [2010 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2965053/) found that brain temperature rose in the minute before a yawn and fell by an average of **0.11 degrees C** over the three minutes after it. Its authors cautioned that, as a correlational study, it could not rule out that the rise was part of the yawn itself rather than its trigger.

A 2019 study had volunteers hold a warm, cold or room-temperature pack against the neck, over the carotid arteries, for five minutes before watching yawns. Of the cooled group, [**48.5%** reported the urge to yawn, against **84.8%** of the warmed group](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31022409/), and the warmed group yawned about three times as often.

Size fits the idea too. A human yawn lasts [4 to 7 seconds on average](https://baillement.com/dossier/gallup_thermal_window.html), the 2014 authors wrote, and a 2021 study of [**1291 yawns from 101 species**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8102614/), 55 mammals and 46 birds, found that yawn duration rose with brain mass and neuron number. Mammals yawned considerably longer than birds of comparable brain and body mass, and the authors read the pattern as evidence that yawning is a thermoregulatory adaptation.

## Contagious yawning drops off in desert heat and near-freezing cold

If a yawn cools the brain by drawing in outside air, it should lose its point when that air is as warm as the body, and possibly in deep cold as well. Researchers tested this on pedestrians, who looked at [20 images of people yawning](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3178034/) and then reported whether they had yawned, in Tucson, Arizona, in June 2010 and February 2011. The Vienna study repeated the test in winter and summer [with 120 pedestrians](https://baillement.com/dossier/gallup_thermal_window.html).

| City and season | Air temperature | Pedestrians who reported yawning |
|---|---|---|
| Tucson, early summer | 37 C | 24% |
| Tucson, winter | 22 C | 45% |
| Vienna, summer | 19.4 C | 41.7% |
| Vienna, winter | 1.4 C | 18.3% |

More people in Tucson reported yawning in winter, and more in Vienna in summer. Vienna's summer and Tucson's winter produced about the same share of yawners, although the 2014 authors noted a large difference in daylight hours between those two samples, and they concluded that reports of yawning were [constrained to an optimal thermal zone](https://baillement.com/dossier/gallup_thermal_window.html).

## The rival camp says no physiological effect has been shown

The 2010 review found that theories giving yawning a physiological role, including the respiratory, arousal and thermoregulation hypotheses, ["lack evidence"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20382180/). Gallup replied in 2011 that yawning is likely [multifunctional across a number of species](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20883719/), with a physiological function first and any social value added later.

The reviewers answered that same year that ["the only specific effect of yawning that could be demonstrated so far is its contagiousness in humans, some non-human primates, and possibly dogs"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21168437/). Gallup and Eldakar, for their part, wrote in 2013 that ["no study has found evidence contrary to these findings"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3534187/) on brain cooling.

## Catching a yawn is a separate puzzle

Contagion is not only human. In a 2004 test of six adult female chimpanzees, [two out of the six](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1810104/) yawned significantly more at videos of chimpanzees yawning than at videos of open-mouth expressions that were not yawns. A 2022 study of [296 participants](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9332820/) found that people reported yawning after looking at photos of fish, reptiles, birds, apes, cats and dogs in mid-yawn, and that the response did not vary significantly between those groups.

Whether catching a yawn reflects empathy is disputed. A year of observations of adults found the rate of contagion greatest for the yawns of [kin, then friends, then acquaintances, and lastly strangers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3233580/).

A Duke University study of [**328 participants**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3954725/) found no influence of empathy once age was accounted for, and age itself explained only 8% of the variation. A 2017 review by Massen and Gallup found the evidence linking the two ["inconsistent and inconclusive"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28735879/).

Neither camp has settled it, and the one effect of a yawn that Guggisberg's team would grant as demonstrated, in their 2011 reply to Gallup, was [that it spreads to others](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21168437/). Strictly FYI.
