On 26 February 2008, during one of the line-ups that NASA's five THEMIS satellites made every four days, [NASA's announcement of the result](https://spaceflightnow.com/news/n0807/24themis/) says "the satellites observed an isolated substorm begin in space, while the ground-based observatories recorded the intense auroral brightening and space currents over North America." A [2010 modelling study of that substorm by Harnett and colleagues](https://earthweb.ess.washington.edu/space/Planetary/earth2/harnett_2010_earth.pdf) gives the THEMIS team's time for the auroral intensification: 0451:39 UT.

The energy behind that display came from the Sun. The electrons that lit it were already here, waiting in Earth's magnetic tail.

## What causes the northern lights?

The northern lights are caused by electrons from Earth's own magnetosphere, which are energized on the night side and [follow the magnetic field down to collide with oxygen and nitrogen in the upper atmosphere](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/phenomena/aurora). The atoms and molecules take up that energy and release it as light. Solar wind supplies the power, but the electrons that make it glow come from Earth's own magnetic tail.

That last clause fixes the timing. The store of electrons sits on the night side of the planet, so the sky performs on a schedule tied to your local midnight.

## The solar wind's job is energy transfer

NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center makes the point in its [aurora tutorial](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/content/aurora-tutorial): "It is technically not the solar wind electrons that create aurora."

The handover depends on which way the Sun's magnetic field happens to point when it arrives. The solar wind carries an embedded interplanetary magnetic field, and when that field is aligned opposite Earth's, pointing southward, more solar wind energy transfers into the magnetosphere and accelerates more of the magnetosphere's own electrons down the field toward Earth.

The tutorial is specific about where the brightest displays draw from. In [NOAA's words](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/content/aurora-tutorial), "The auroral electrons that create the best aurora in the middle of the night actually come from the tail of the magnetosphere, downstream (away from the sun)".

## Earth keeps the fuel in its own tail

The magnetosphere is not shaped like a bubble. On the night side it is drawn out into a magnetotail hundreds of Earth radii long, which [Joseph Borovsky and Juan Alejandro Valdivia, in a 2018 review in Surveys in Geophysics](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10712-018-9487-x), call "a reservoir of magnetic energy that powers several magnetospheric processes."

Inside it sit two important hot plasmas, the ion plasma sheet and the electron plasma sheet. It is the second that matters for the light:

> "Most aurora are produced by electron impact from the electron plasma sheet." - Joseph Borovsky and Juan Alejandro Valdivia, [*Surveys in Geophysics*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10712-018-9487-x), 2018

The geometry explains the standard viewing advice. NOAA's [tips for aurora watchers](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/content/tips-viewing-aurora) say **"Best aurora is usually within an hour or two of midnight (between 10 PM and 2 AM local time)."** At local midnight your patch of ground is on the side of Earth that faces down the tail.

## One team timed the trigger 20 Earth radii out

Energy loads into the tail, then lets go all at once. The [2008 Science paper by Vassilis Angelopoulos and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18653845/), which analysed that substorm, opens with the stakes: "Magnetospheric substorms explosively release solar wind energy previously stored in Earth's magnetotail."

Measuring at several distances down the tail at once, [the team reported](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18653845/) that "Reconnection was observed at 20 R(E), at least 1.5 minutes before auroral intensification", and about 3 minutes before near-Earth current disruption. [Their conclusion](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18653845/), under the title "Tail reconnection triggering substorm onset", is stated with care: "These results demonstrate that substorms are likely initiated by tail reconnection."

The abstract counts one Earth radius as 6374 kilometers, which puts [those **20 Earth radii**](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18653845/) at roughly 127,000 km.

## The order of events is still argued over

The result did not close the argument. In [a 2009 comment in Science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19520943/), A.T.Y. Lui of the Johns Hopkins University Applied Physics Laboratory challenged the timing. Writing about the same storm, [Harnett and colleagues](https://earthweb.ess.washington.edu/space/Planetary/earth2/harnett_2010_earth.pdf) summarise his case as "that auroral zone current structures become enhanced before reconnection is observed by the THEMIS satellites and therefore the reconnection is unrelated to substorm onset". Their own simulations of the storm agree that reconnection came first, but [they conclude](https://earthweb.ess.washington.edu/space/Planetary/earth2/harnett_2010_earth.pdf) that "reconnection alone does not drive substorm onset."

The dispute is alive. A [2025 Nature Communications paper by Wu and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12657864/) calls the expansion phase "one of the most debated aspects of substorm dynamics", setting the near-Earth neutral line model, with reconnection starting roughly 10 to 20 Earth radii down the tail, against a current disruption model in which near-Earth instabilities fire first and the disturbance works from the inside out.

## Green is light an oxygen atom is forbidden to emit

When an electron slams into an oxygen atom high in the atmosphere, it can leave the atom in an excited state called 1S. NOAA's tutorial gives the most common colour as "a pale green color at a wavelength of 557.7 nm", from oxygen in that 1S state, typically glowing [from 120 to 400 km](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/content/aurora-tutorial) above the ground.

Even that green often goes unseen. NOAA puts reports of white aurora down to night vision, in which the eye registers brightness before it distinguishes colour, so only a fairly bright display shows its true green.

The 1S transition is a forbidden one. The team that reported [visible-wavelength aurora on Mars](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12077521/), writing in Science Advances in 2025, refers to "the forbidden atomic oxygen transitions at 557.7 and 630 nm."

Forbidden is a statement about probability. The atom does make the jump, slowly: oxygen in the 1S state has [a lifetime of about 1 second](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/content/aurora-tutorial), while the 1D state behind deep red lasts [about two minutes](https://physics.nist.gov/cgi-bin/ASD/lines1.pl?spectra=O+I&limits_type=0&low_w=290&upp_w=640&unit=1&submit=Retrieve+Data&de=0&format=1&line_out=0&en_unit=0&output=0&bibrefs=1&page_size=15&show_obs_wl=1&show_calc_wl=1&unc_out=1&order_out=0&max_low_enrg=&show_av=2&max_upp_enrg=34000&tsb_value=0&min_str=&A_out=0&intens_out=on&max_str=&allowed_out=1&forbid_out=1&min_accur=&min_intens=&conf_out=on&term_out=on&enrg_out=on&J_out=on), and NOAA says it survives only above 300 km. Lower down, where the air is dense, another particle reaches the excited atom before it can emit and carries the energy off in a collision, which NOAA calls quenching.

## The colours are an altitude map

The [University of Alaska Fairbanks Geophysical Institute](https://www.gi.alaska.edu/monitors/aurora-forecast) puts the bottom edge of the aurora at **"about 60 miles (100 kilometers) above the surface of the Earth."** NOAA's tutorial describes molecular nitrogen's emission as prompt, with almost no delay after excitation, so nitrogen needs no quiet air. It paints the purple rim that very intense aurora often shows at the bottom, which the [Geophysical Institute](https://www.gi.alaska.edu/monitors/aurora-forecast) describes as "a mixture of blue and red emissions from nitrogen molecules."

| Colour | Emitter | Wavelength | Where it glows |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pale green | Atomic oxygen, 1S state | 557.7 nm | 120 to 400 km (NOAA) |
| Deep red | Atomic oxygen, 1D state | 630 nm | Above 300 km (NOAA) |
| Purple lower border | Molecular nitrogen | Prompt emission | Bottom edge of the display |

Red sits on top because it is slowest, green fills the middle and nitrogen fringes the bottom. A photograph of a tall curtain is, read from the top down, a profile of how long each excited atom can wait.

## The direct-hit version is a simplification

The account on many aurora pages runs like this: particles leave the Sun, cross the distance to Earth, get funnelled toward the poles and hit the air. The [Geophysical Institute's forecast page](https://www.gi.alaska.edu/monitors/aurora-forecast) uses that shorthand too, describing "the electrons and protons from the sun" colliding with gases in the atmosphere. It is a compressed teaching version, and the compression deletes the storage step where the interesting physics happens.

The honest complication is that much of the magnetosphere's plasma did start at the Sun. [Borovsky and Valdivia](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10712-018-9487-x) list "two sources for the plasmas in the magnetosphere", solar wind plasma entering across the magnetopause or into the cusps and outflow from Earth's own ionosphere, and describe the ion plasma sheet as "a hot low-density plasma, typically of solar-wind origin".

Material arrives from both. The plasma behind most aurora does not arrive and glow at once. It is captured, parked in the tail and only later accelerated back down the field lines with enough energy to excite an atom.

## Most displays are stored energy coming home

Earth's magnetic field banks solar wind energy in its tail, builds it up slowly, then spends it in a substorm, which the [2018 review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10712-018-9487-x) describes as a brief interval of about 30 minutes, and a substorm's light is the receipt.

That February display was lit by particles that had been sitting on Earth's own night side, waiting for the tail to let go. The Sun paid for the show. The timing, the storage and the green were all local. Strictly FYI.
