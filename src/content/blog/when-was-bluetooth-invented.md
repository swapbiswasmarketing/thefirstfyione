The Jewish Museum Vienna titled its exhibition on Hedy Lamarr "Lady Bluetooth," and [its page](https://jmw.at/exhibition/lady_bluetooth_hedy_lamarr_) says the Hollywood star "was the inventor of frequency hopping, a precursor to Bluetooth and WLAN." Two words in an exhibition title carry the whole popular story: a film star invented the radio trick that the earbuds in your pocket depend on.

Half of that holds up. Classic Bluetooth does hop, [usually **1,600 hops per second**](https://www.bluetooth.com/wp-content/uploads/Files/Specification/HTML/Core-60/out/en/architecture,-change-history,-and-conventions/architecture.html).

Lamarr and the composer George Antheil got [US patent 2,292,387 on August 11, 1942](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedy_Lamarr) for a torpedo guidance system that would skip between radio frequencies the way a player-piano roll skips between notes. But frequency hopping is older than her patent: [Jonathan Zenneck's 1908 book Wireless Telegraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency-hopping_spread_spectrum) described it and said Telefunken had already tried it, and [the German military made limited use of it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency-hopping_spread_spectrum) in World War I. Bluetooth itself was designed at Ericsson half a century after Lamarr's patent.

## When was Bluetooth invented?

Bluetooth was invented at Ericsson in Lund, Sweden, where the main design work [began in **1994**](https://www.invent.org/inductees/jaap-c-haartsen) and the team had [a workable solution by 1997](https://web.archive.org/web/20210703142518/http://documents.epo.org/projects/babylon/eponet.nsf/0/39F611EACF119B21C12579F30036D7E4/$File/haartsen_en.pdf). Ericsson traces the project further back, to [1989 and Nils Rydbeck, technology chief of its mobile division](https://www.ericsson.com/en/blog/6/2022/ericsson-bluetooth), and Ericsson, IBM, Intel, Nokia and Toshiba [first announced the Bluetooth Special Interest Group in **May 1998**](https://www.intel.com/pressroom/archive/releases/1998/mc102698.htm). By the SIG's own timeline, the [Bluetooth 1.0 specification was released in 1999 and the first Bluetooth headset shipped in 2000](https://web.archive.org/web/20180525083558/https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/our-history).

Phones followed. Ericsson's R520m, [the world's first GPRS phone with Bluetooth](https://www.mobilephonemuseum.com/phone-detail/r520m), became commercially available in the first quarter of 2001, and Ericsson calls its T39 phone, which reached the mass market the same year, [the first widely available Bluetooth consumer device](https://www.ericsson.com/en/blog/6/2022/ericsson-bluetooth). IBM's ThinkPad A30p, which [went on sale in Japan on October 15, 2001](https://pc.watch.impress.co.jp/docs/article/20011015/ibm.htm), came with Bluetooth built in alongside wireless networking. Every one of them carried a name its makers had [intended only as a placeholder](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/bluetooth-origin/).

## The radio began as a way to cut the headset cord

Ericsson credits the seed of the idea to Rydbeck and the Swedish physician and inventor Johan Ullman, who were looking for [marketable alternatives to a wired connection](https://www.ericsson.com/en/blog/6/2022/ericsson-bluetooth); the brief was a way to connect computers with wireless headsets. Ericsson's own milestones page, archived in 2004, starts with an initiative ["to study a low-power, low-cost radio interface between mobile phones and their accessories."](https://web.archive.org/web/20040620150507/http://www.ericsson.com/bluetooth/companyove/history-bl/) Neither that page nor a study prepared for the European Patent Office goes back as far as 1989, and the study notes that [the invention is commonly dated to 1994](https://web.archive.org/web/20210703142518/http://documents.epo.org/projects/babylon/eponet.nsf/0/39F611EACF119B21C12579F30036D7E4/$File/haartsen_en.pdf).

The engineers who turned the idea into a working radio were Jaap Haartsen, a Dutchman, and Sven Mattisson. Ericsson called them ["co-inventors of the Bluetooth wireless technology"](https://web.archive.org/web/20040620150507/http://www.ericsson.com/bluetooth/companyove/history-bl/), and Haartsen was [inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame in 2015](https://www.invent.org/inductees/jaap-c-haartsen).

Ericsson did not keep the radio to itself: it [approached several makers of portable electronic devices](https://web.archive.org/web/20040620150507/http://www.ericsson.com/bluetooth/companyove/history-bl/) to discuss developing and promoting the technology.

## A Viking king's name, found on a Toronto pub crawl

The name came from Jim Kardach, an engineer at Intel. Intel's project was called Biz-RF, Ericsson's MC-LINK and Nokia's Low Power RF. After Kardach and Mattisson got ["a lukewarm reception"](https://www.kardach.com/bluetooth/naming-bluetooth) for their separate pitches at a Home-RF SIG meeting in Toronto, Kardach decided the project needed one codename everyone could use.

That night, by Kardach's own account, the two toured Toronto's pubs and Mattisson retold Scandinavian history from [Frans G. Bengtsson's](https://www.eetimes.com/tech-history-how-bluetooth-got-its-name/) Viking novel *The Long Ships*, set while King Harald Bluetooth was fighting his son. Kardach did not read it then: Mattisson gave him the book months after the launch.

Back home, Kardach found a photograph of the Jelling runestone in Gwyn Jones's book on the Vikings. Its inscription, [as he quoted it](https://www.eetimes.com/tech-history-how-bluetooth-got-its-name/), praises "that Harald who won for himself all Denmark and Norway, and made the Danes Christian."

A king who united the Danes seemed a good mascot for a radio meant to unite computers and phones. Intel's lawyer disagreed, replying, by [Kardach's telling](https://www.kardach.com/bluetooth/naming-bluetooth), that "we require that the codename be a river, lake or city!"

## The placeholder launched because the real names failed

The codename went before the other companies at a December meeting in Lund, Sweden, on the understanding that marketing would pick a proper name before launch. Kardach has dated that meeting to ["December of 1996"](https://www.eetimes.com/tech-history-how-bluetooth-got-its-name/) in EE Times and to December of '97 [on his own site](https://www.kardach.com/bluetooth/naming-bluetooth), and the later date fits the SIG's launch in May 1998.

Ericsson's marketers floated Flirt, ["Getting close without touching"](https://www.kardach.com/bluetooth/naming-bluetooth). Intel's marketing team came up with RadioWire. At the vote, the official name ["went to PAN in a 4-1 vote"](https://www.eetimes.com/tech-history-how-bluetooth-got-its-name/), short for Personal Area Networking.

By Kardach's account, Ericsson then called an emergency meeting: PAN could not be trademarked. The SIG's own history says an internet search had turned up [tens of thousands of hits](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/bluetooth-origin/), and that a full trademark search on RadioWire could not be completed in time for the launch, which left Bluetooth as the only choice.

> "Bluetooth was only intended as a placeholder until marketing could come up with something really cool." - [Bluetooth SIG, "Origin of the name"](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/bluetooth-origin/)

## The logo is two runes, and the tooth is a guess

The logo is [a bind rune merging the Younger Futhark runes](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/bluetooth-origin/) Hagall and Bjarkan, H and B, Harald's initials. The same page states that the nickname came from his dead tooth, "a dark blue/grey color." Nobody knows that for certain.

The nickname first appears in writing in [the Chronicon Roskildense, written about 1140](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_Bluetooth), about a century and a half after Harald died. A late 12th-century chronicle glosses it as "bluish or black tooth", and a bad tooth is the traditional explanation, but [Scocozza proposed in 1997](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_Bluetooth) that it began as "blue thane" in England. The SIG page also has Harald uniting Denmark and Norway in 958, about the year he became king of Denmark; his hold on Norway [most likely lasted no more than a few years in the 970s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_Bluetooth).

## The versions arrived in steady steps

Microsoft notes that Bluetooth 2.1 radios are [backward compatible with earlier versions](https://download.microsoft.com/download/9/c/5/9c5b2167-8017-4bae-9fde-d599bac8184a/Bth_FAQ.docx), but Bluetooth Low Energy, added in 4.0, ["has no direct compatibility"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth_Low_Energy) with classic Bluetooth, so a low-energy-only gadget needs a phone that supports it:

|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | [1999](https://web.archive.org/web/20180525083558/https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/our-history) | The first specification |
| 2.0 + EDR | [2004](https://web.archive.org/web/20151003173654/https://www.bluetooth.org/en-us/specification/adopted-specifications) | Enhanced Data Rate: [3 Mbit/s raw, 2.1 Mbit/s of actual data](https://www.bluetooth.com/wp-content/uploads/Files/Specification/HTML/Core-60/out/en/architecture,-change-history,-and-conventions/architecture.html) |
| 3.0 + HS | [21 April 2009](https://web.archive.org/web/20151003173654/https://www.bluetooth.org/en-us/specification/adopted-specifications) | High-speed transfers over a Wi-Fi link |
| 4.0 | [30 June 2010](https://web.archive.org/web/20151003173654/https://www.bluetooth.org/en-us/specification/adopted-specifications) | Bluetooth Low Energy, for coin-cell devices |

Apple's Mac OS X has supported Bluetooth since [version 10.2, released in 2002](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2002/07/17Apple-Introduces-Jaguar-the-Next-Major-Release-of-Mac-OS-X/). On Windows, Service Pack 1 for XP had supported Bluetooth only through a driver for PC makers, and Service Pack 2 [put it in a regular service pack available to all customers](https://download.microsoft.com/download/9/c/5/9c5b2167-8017-4bae-9fde-d599bac8184a/Bth_FAQ.docx). The low-energy version reached phones too: the iPhone 4S, released in October 2011, was [the first handset to support Bluetooth 4.0](https://www.engadget.com/2011-10-12-iphone-4s-claims-title-of-first-bluetooth-4-0-smartphone-ready.html).

## The radio hops between 79 channels to dodge interference

Classic Bluetooth works in the unlicensed 2.4 GHz band, cuts data into packets and sends each one on [one of 79 designated Bluetooth channels](https://www.bluetooth.com/learn-about-bluetooth/tech-overview/), each 1 MHz wide, hopping between them so that interference on any one channel affects a connection only briefly. Bluetooth Low Energy [uses 40 channels instead](https://www.bluetooth.com/learn-about-bluetooth/tech-overview/), each 2 MHz wide.

Lamarr and Antheil's patent noted that a standard player-piano roll, with 88 rows of perforations, [would permit 88 different carrier frequencies](https://patents.google.com/patent/US2292387A/en). The radio in your earbuds [gets by with 79](https://www.bluetooth.com/wp-content/uploads/Files/Specification/HTML/Core-60/out/en/architecture,-change-history,-and-conventions/architecture.html). Bluetooth is named after a 10th-century Danish king, from a Viking novel retold on a Toronto pub crawl and a runestone photograph, and it launched under that name because the official pick, PAN, could not be trademarked.
