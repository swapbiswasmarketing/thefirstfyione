The Jewish Museum Vienna titled its exhibition on Hedy Lamarr "Lady Bluetooth," and [its page](https://jmw.at/exhibition/lady_bluetooth_hedy_lamarr_) says the Hollywood star "was the inventor of frequency hopping, a precursor to Bluetooth and WLAN." Two words in an exhibition title carry the whole popular story: a film star invented the radio trick that the earbuds in your pocket depend on.

Half of that holds up. Classic Bluetooth does hop, [usually **1,600 hops per second**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth).

Lamarr and the composer George Antheil got [US patent 2,292,387 on August 11, 1942](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedy_Lamarr) for a torpedo guidance system that would skip between radio frequencies the way a player-piano roll skips between notes. But frequency hopping is older than her patent: [Jonathan Zenneck's 1908 book Wireless Telegraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency-hopping_spread_spectrum) described it and said Telefunken had already tried it, and [the German military made limited use of it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency-hopping_spread_spectrum) in World War I. Bluetooth itself was designed at Ericsson half a century after Lamarr's patent.

## When was Bluetooth invented?

Bluetooth was invented at Ericsson in Lund, Sweden, where the main design work began in **1994** and the team had [a workable solution by 1997](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth). The project had been [initiated in 1989 by Nils Rydbeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth), and the Bluetooth Special Interest Group launched in **May 1998**. The first Bluetooth device, a hands-free headset, was [revealed in 1999](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth).

Phones followed. Ericsson's R520m, released in early 2001, was [the first ever commercially available Bluetooth phone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth), and IBM's ThinkPad A30 arrived that October as one of the first notebooks with Bluetooth built in. Every one of them carried a name its makers had [intended only as a placeholder](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/bluetooth-origin/).

## The radio began as a way to cut the headset cord

The original goal was a wireless headset, based on [two inventions by Johan Ullman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth). Ericsson's own milestones page, archived in 2004, describes the starting point as an initiative ["to study a low-power, low-cost radio interface between mobile phones and their accessories."](https://web.archive.org/web/20040620150507/http://www.ericsson.com/bluetooth/companyove/history-bl/)

The engineers who turned that into a working radio were Jaap Haartsen, a Dutchman, and Sven Mattisson. The same Ericsson page calls them ["co-inventors of the Bluetooth wireless technology"](https://web.archive.org/web/20040620150507/http://www.ericsson.com/bluetooth/companyove/history-bl/), and Haartsen was [inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaap_Haartsen) in 2015.

In 1997 IBM wanted to put a mobile phone inside a ThinkPad, found the power draw too high, and [agreed with Ericsson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth) to link a notebook and a phone by short-range radio instead. Neither led its market, so [they made it an open industry standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth), and IBM recruited Intel, which [brought in Toshiba and Nokia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth).

## A Viking king's name, found on a Toronto pub crawl

The name came from Jim Kardach, an engineer at Intel, the company that had already worked on [the USB standard](/blog/why-is-usb-not-reversible/). Intel's project was called Biz-RF, Ericsson's MC-LINK and Nokia's Low Power RF. After Kardach and Mattisson got ["a lukewarm reception"](https://www.kardach.com/bluetooth/naming-bluetooth) for their separate pitches at a Home-RF SIG meeting in Toronto, Kardach decided the project needed one codename everyone could use.

That night, by [Kardach's own account](https://www.kardach.com/bluetooth/naming-bluetooth), the two toured Toronto's pubs and Mattisson retold Scandinavian history from [Frans G. Bengtsson's](https://www.eetimes.com/tech-history-how-bluetooth-got-its-name/) Viking novel *The Long Ships*, set while King Harald Bluetooth was fighting his son. Kardach did not read it then: Mattisson [gave him the book months after the launch](https://www.kardach.com/bluetooth/naming-bluetooth). Mattisson also assured him, [Kardach recalled](https://www.kardach.com/bluetooth/naming-bluetooth), that Vikings never had horns on their helmets, one of several Norse legends that fall apart on inspection, like the skull cups behind [the toast skol](/blog/what-does-skol-mean/).

Back home, Kardach found a photograph of the Jelling runestone in Gwyn Jones's book on the Vikings. Its inscription, [as he quoted it](https://www.kardach.com/bluetooth/naming-bluetooth), praises "that Harald who won for himself all Denmark and Norway, and made the Danes Christian."

A king who united the Danes seemed a good mascot for a radio meant to unite computers and phones. Intel's lawyer disagreed, replying, by [Kardach's telling](https://www.kardach.com/bluetooth/naming-bluetooth), that "we require that the codename be a river, lake or city!"

## The placeholder launched because the real names failed

The codename went before the other companies at [a December meeting in Lund, Sweden](https://www.kardach.com/bluetooth/naming-bluetooth), on the understanding that marketing would pick a proper name before launch. Kardach has dated that meeting to ["December of 1996"](https://www.eetimes.com/tech-history-how-bluetooth-got-its-name/) in EE Times and to December of '97 [on his own site](https://www.kardach.com/bluetooth/naming-bluetooth), and the later date fits the SIG's launch in May 1998.

Ericsson's marketers floated Flirt, ["Getting close without touching"](https://www.kardach.com/bluetooth/naming-bluetooth). Intel's marketing team [came up with RadioWire](https://www.kardach.com/bluetooth/naming-bluetooth). At the vote, the official name ["went to PAN in a 4-1 vote"](https://www.eetimes.com/tech-history-how-bluetooth-got-its-name/), short for Personal Area Networking.

Then Ericsson [called an emergency meeting](https://www.kardach.com/bluetooth/naming-bluetooth): PAN could not be trademarked. A search had turned up [thousands of hits](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/bluetooth-origin/), and nobody had finished a trademark search on RadioWire. With the launch weeks away, Bluetooth was [the only other name](https://www.kardach.com/bluetooth/naming-bluetooth) that had been through a trademark search.

> "Bluetooth was only intended as a placeholder until marketing could come up with something really cool." - [Bluetooth SIG, "Origin of the name"](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/bluetooth-origin/)

## The logo is two runes, and the tooth is a guess

The logo is [a bind rune merging the Younger Futhark runes](https://www.bluetooth.com/about-us/bluetooth-origin/) Hagall and Bjarkan, H and B, Harald's initials. The same page states that the nickname came from his dead tooth, "a dark blue/grey color." Nobody knows that for certain.

The nickname first appears in writing in [the Chronicon Roskildense, written about 1140](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_Bluetooth), about a century and a half after Harald died. A late 12th-century chronicle glosses it as "bluish or black tooth", and a bad tooth is the traditional explanation, but [Scocozza proposed in 1997](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_Bluetooth) that it began as "blue thane" in England. The SIG page also has Harald uniting Denmark and Norway in 958, about the year [he became king of Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_Bluetooth); his hold on Norway [most likely lasted no more than a few years in the 970s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_Bluetooth).

## Bluetooth was on version 2 by 2004 and version 4 by 2010

Bluetooth was on sale in 2004, 2010 and 2012 alike, and the versions arrived in steady steps. Newer classic Bluetooth gear [still works with older devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth), but Bluetooth Low Energy, added in 4.0, ["has no direct compatibility"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth_Low_Energy) with classic Bluetooth, so a low-energy-only gadget needs a phone that supports it:

| Version | Adopted | What it added |
|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | [1999](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth) | The first specification |
| 2.0 + EDR | [2004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth) | Enhanced Data Rate: 3 Mbit/s raw, 2.1 Mbit/s of actual data |
| 3.0 + HS | [21 April 2009](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth) | High-speed transfers over a Wi-Fi link |
| 4.0 | [30 June 2010](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth) | Bluetooth Low Energy, for coin-cell devices |
| 5 | [6 December 2016](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth) | Features aimed at the Internet of Things |
| 6.0 | [27 August 2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth) | Channel Sounding; versions 6.1, 6.2 and 6.3 followed by May 2026 |

Apple's Mac OS X has supported Bluetooth since [version 10.2, released in 2002](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth), and [Windows XP Service Pack 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth) ran it without the adapter maker's drivers. By 2012 the low-energy version was in phones too: the iPhone 4S, released in October 2011, was [the first smartphone to implement the 4.0 specification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth_Low_Energy).

## The radio hops between 79 channels to dodge interference

Classic Bluetooth works in the unlicensed 2.4 GHz band, cuts data into packets and sends each one on [one of 79 designated Bluetooth channels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth), each 1 MHz wide, hopping between them so that interference on any one channel affects a connection only briefly. Bluetooth Low Energy [uses 40 channels instead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth), each 2 MHz wide.

Lamarr and Antheil's patent noted that a standard player-piano roll, with 88 rows of perforations, [would permit 88 different carrier frequencies](https://patents.google.com/patent/US2292387A/en). The radio in your earbuds [gets by with 79](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth). Bluetooth is named after a 10th-century Danish king, from a Viking novel retold on a Toronto pub crawl and a runestone photograph, and it launched under that name because the official pick, PAN, could not be trademarked.
