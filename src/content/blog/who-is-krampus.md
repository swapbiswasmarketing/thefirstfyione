**Krampus Is an Evil Man** was the title of a leaflet that Dr. Ernst Kotbauer, head of Vienna's kindergarten system, put out in 1953. He warned parents that an encounter with the creature might leave their children "scarred for life," [Time reported that December](https://content.time.com/time/subscriber/article/0,33009,890728,00.html). A Vienna daily took his side and proposed throwing Krampus out.

Nobody threw him out. Seven decades later Krampus has a Hollywood film, parades in American cities and a reputation as a survival from pagan times, and the last of those is the shakiest thing about him.

## Who is Krampus?

Krampus is the hairy, horned devil of Alpine folklore who walks with St. Nicholas on the evening of **December 5**, Krampusnacht. [Britannica describes](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Krampus) a half-goat, half-demon figure with chains, bells, a bundle of birch twigs and a basket for carting off bad children. Nicholas rewards good children with gifts. Krampus punishes the naughty ones.

The two are a team. Britannica says Krampus and Nicholas are companions, not brothers, and their visit falls on the eve of Saint Nicholas Day, December 6. Towns across Austria's Alpine regions, southern Germany and South Tyrol still hold Krampus runs, costumed street parades that peak on the evening of December 5.

## A 1912 folklorist found him under three names

The folklorist Clement A. Miles, in his 1912 book [Christmas in Ritual and Tradition, Christian and Pagan](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/19098), described a Tyrolean St. Nicholas pointing to "the hideous Klaubauf, a shaggy monster with horns, black face, fiery eyes, long red tongue, and chains that clank as he moves." Miles listed the local names:

| Region | The saint's attendant, per Miles |
|---|---|
| Tyrol | Klaubauf |
| Lower Austria | Krampus or Grampus |
| Styria | Bartel |

The word itself is uncertain. Britannica says it [may ultimately derive from the Old High German Krampf](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Krampus), meaning "cramp" or "claw," "though the etymology is disputed." [Smithsonian magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/travel/krampus-could-come-you-holiday-season-180957438/) gives a root without that caveat: the German krampen, meaning claw.

## The pagan origin is a guess that hardened into fact

A common claim about Krampus is that he is older than Christianity. Miles shows how old that idea is, and how thin, in what he wrote of the Klaubauf, Krampus and Bartel:

> "Their heathen origin is evident though it is difficult to trace their exact pedigree." - Clement A. Miles, [Christmas in Ritual and Tradition, Christian and Pagan](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/19098), 1912

A folklorist sure of the origin and unable to trace its pedigree is the pagan case in miniature.

Later sources go further. Britannica says Krampus ["is thought to have been part of pagan rituals for the winter solstice"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Krampus), and adds that according to some legends he is the son of Hel, the Norse goddess of the underworld. [Smithsonian magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/travel/krampus-could-come-you-holiday-season-180957438/) repeats the Hel line as tradition, and one of its photo captions calls an Austrian Perchten parade "a 1,500 year-old pagan ritual." Neither page says where the Hel legend was recorded.

The written trail is much shorter. [German Wikipedia](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krampus), citing a study of Perchten customs in Tyrol and Salzburg by Hans Schuhladen, says there are no sources for the custom before the end of the 16th century. The same article opens by calling the figure pre-Christian, so a single encyclopedia entry holds both views.

Al Ridenour's 2016 book The Krampus and the Old, Dark Christmas argues against a pagan survival. The darker holiday, the reviewer Eric Hoenes del Pinal writes in summing up its argument, ["is not a survival from a dimly remembered pagan past"](https://readingreligion.org/9781627310345/the-krampus-and-the-old-dark-christmas/) but developed well after Christianity arrived in the region.

Ridenour does tie Krampus to an older figure. Asked by LAist about Krampus's pagan roots, he said the tradition flows from [Frau Perchta, "a sort of ambivalent goddess figure"](https://laist.com/podcasts/off-ramp/slideshow-krampus-brings-the-dark-side-of-christmas-to-la) whom the church demonized, a story going "way back to the 1100s." Asked earlier in the same interview for the Krampus origin story, though, he gave the plainer version: ["The origin is actually kind of unknown."](https://laist.com/podcasts/off-ramp/slideshow-krampus-brings-the-dark-side-of-christmas-to-la)

## Postcards made him a joke, and sometimes a flirt

In the late 19th century, Britannica says, chromolithography led to the mass production of Krampus postcards, Krampuskarten, which were [popular from the 1880s until World War I](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Krampus). They carried greetings such as "Gruss vom Krampus," Greetings from Krampus, and put him in humorous or risque scenes, dragging off children or trying to court women.

## Austrian officials frowned on him in 1934 and again in 1953

Later the pressure on him came from politics. Smithsonian dates a political attempt to eradicate him to 1934, at the hands of [Austria's conservative Christian Social Party](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/travel/krampus-could-come-you-holiday-season-180957438/). That December The New York Times headlined a report ["Krampus Disliked in Fascist Austria"](https://www.nytimes.com/1934/12/23/archives/krampus-disliked-in-fascist-austria-genial-black-and-red-devil.html). The [often-repeated claim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krampus) that the Dollfuss regime prohibited the tradition cites that newspaper report, not a decree.

The 1953 campaign was smaller than it often sounds. [Retellings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krampus) credit the Government of Austria with the leaflets, while [Time's report](https://content.time.com/time/subscriber/article/0,33009,890728,00.html) names a single official, Dr. Kotbauer of Vienna's kindergartens, and mentions one unnamed Vienna daily that agreed with him. "There is too much fear in the world already," [the paper said, as Time quoted it](https://content.time.com/time/subscriber/article/0,33009,890728,00.html), "unemployment, high taxes, not to mention the atom bomb. Let's begin by throwing out Krampus."

## East Tyrol's Krampus runs sent dozens to hospital in 2013

The parades are not all theater. After several Krampus runs in East Tyrol in December 2013, the [Tiroler Tageszeitung reported](https://www.tt.com/artikel/7588184/70-verletzte-bei-krampuslauf) that the district hospital in Lienz admitted **eight** seriously injured people, most with broken bones, and treated more than **60** others as outpatients. At one run in Matrei, nearly 200 Krampuses and several hundred spectators fought what the paper called a real battle, and a 15-year-old suffered a skull fracture. A surgeon at the hospital told the paper it was the same every year, and that the injured included spectators as well as Krampuses.

## America found him in a postcard book

Collectors Weekly credits Monte Beauchamp, founder of BLAB! magazine, whose 2004 book of early 1900s Krampus postcards ["sparked a new fascination with the beast in America."](https://www.collectorsweekly.com/articles/krampus-is-coming-to-town/) Beauchamp does not see a villain: Krampus, he told the magazine, is ["a good-natured character; his only desire is to persuade unruly children to turn from their wicked ways."](https://www.collectorsweekly.com/articles/krampus-is-coming-to-town/)

By 2011 NPR was covering a costumed [Krampus run being prepared in Philadelphia](https://www.npr.org/2011/12/10/143485735/naughty-or-nice-krampus-horror-for-the-holidays). In December 2015 Universal-Legendary's horror-comedy Krampus was, by Variety's early estimates, headed for a [$16 million opening weekend at 2,902 U.S. theaters](https://variety.com/2015/film/news/box-office-krampus-mockingjay-good-dinosaur-creed-1201653946/). That same season Smithsonian reported a Krampusnacht festival [being held for the first time in Orlando, Florida](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/travel/krampus-could-come-you-holiday-season-180957438/).

The leaflet lost: the creature a Vienna official wanted gone went on to open in American cinemas. His family tree is another story. Clement Miles called his heathen origin evident in 1912 and [admitted it was difficult to trace](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/19098). Strictly FYI.
