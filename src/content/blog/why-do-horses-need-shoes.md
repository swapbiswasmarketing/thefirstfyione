In Swedish harness racing, taking the shoes off is the upgrade. On average, [**29% of the starts in 3- to 10-year-old**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13167801/) Standardbred trotters were made with barefoot hind hooves, and a 2020 study of Swedish Standardbreds found horses racing unshod [**0.7 s/km** faster than fully shod horses](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32363779/). Where fractions of a second decide prize money, the metal often comes off.

The speed has a price. The same study found that racing unshod raised the relative risks of galloping and disqualification [by 15%-35% in all seasons](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32363779/), and Swedish rules allow barefoot racing [only from age 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13167801/), with the trainer fined if a hoof is worn down until it bleeds. A hoof worn that far is exactly what a shoe is there to prevent.

## Why do horses need shoes?

Horses need shoes when work wears the hoof wall away faster than it grows: a New Mexico State University extension guide puts it as [**"when wear exceeds new hoof growth, horses should be shod."**](https://pubs.nmsu.edu/_b/B707/) The same guide adds that horses are also shod to enhance performance or as therapeutic treatment, and that under most circumstances regular trimming is enough to keep feet healthy.

The 2026 *Journal of Animal Science* study of young Swedish trotters says what goes missing on race day: barefoot, ["the protective properties of the shoe, which prevent the hoof from excessive wear and tear, are absent."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13167801/) The complication is that the wall never stops growing, so taking the wear away does not leave the foot where it was.

## The wall never stops growing

The 2022 study "Hoof Matters: Developing an Athletic Thoroughbred Hoof" calls the outer layer a hoof capsule "composed of insensitive cornified tissues", and records that ["the keratinized hoof capsule continues to grow in a proximodistal direction throughout life"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9686713/) - from the top down, permanently.

The rate is measurable. A 2019 paper in the *Journal of the Royal Society Interface* on hoof mechano-biology tabulates horses at [**0.2-0.3 mm a day**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6597769/), ahead of sheep and deer at 0.1-0.2. The extension guide's rule of thumb for an average hoof is [3/8 of an inch per month](https://pubs.nmsu.edu/_b/B707/), a little under a centimetre, though it notes that growth varies with climate and between horses.

Even the first hoof is temporary. Thoroughbred foals, the Hoof Matters study reports, ["completely renew the fetal hoof capsule between 120 and 165 days after birth."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9686713/) A foal walks out of its first foot inside six months.

## The ground is doing the trimming

Because growth never stops, hoof length is set by whatever removes material from the bottom. A 2019 crossover study in *Animals* put [11 Quarter Horse mares](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6940763/) through seven weeks shod and seven weeks barefoot. Barefoot, the front of the wall wore down [**8.30 mm on the left fore and 8.56 mm on the right, against 2.88 and 3.71 mm while shod**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6940763/), while growth did not differ significantly between the two.

Barefoot does not mean hands-off, though. Before the trial, the unshod mares were [maintained on a seven-week trimming interval](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6940763/) by the same farrier, and the extension guide says most horses should be [trimmed or shod every 6 to 12 weeks](https://pubs.nmsu.edu/_b/B707/).

> The wall keeps growing whether or not anything is wearing it off. Fix a plate underneath and most of the filing stops while the growing carries on, so a shod hoof has to be cut back and the shoe reset by hand.

A 2023 shoeing trial in the *Journal of Animal Science* built the reset into its protocol: ["Horses were trimmed and re-shod at week 6."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36383438/)

## The measurements cut both ways

Horan and colleagues tested [13 galloping Thoroughbred racehorses retired from racing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9454475/) in aluminium, steel, GluShu (an aluminium-rubber composite) and bare feet, on turf and an artificial track, with hoof accelerometers sampling at 5000 Hz. [Collective mean accelerations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9454475/) ran 22-98 g at impact and 17-89 g at foot-off, and the shoeing condition moved almost every measure. Their finding, in *Animals* in 2022: ["Barefoot hooves generally experienced the lowest accelerations."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9454475/)

The same trials were also filmed. Across [389 hoof-ground interactions](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11469542/), the group reported in *PLoS One* in 2024 that in the leading hindlimb, ["slip durations were also significantly longer for the aluminium shoeing condition compared to barefoot, by 3.7 ms."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11469542/) Slip is not simply a fault to remove, the authors add: ["some slip is essential to dissipate energy and lower stresses on the limb tissues during initial loading."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11469542/)

Then a trial went the other way. Panos and colleagues shod the hind hooves of [14 barefoot horses](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36383438/) for 12 weeks in three-quarter fullered or plain stamp shoes, and the shoe type made no measurable difference. The shod state did: the authors report that shoeing ["improved AAEP lameness scores"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36383438/) on the American Association of Equine Practitioners scale (P = 0.001).

Each horse was compared with its own barefoot baseline, with no unshod group alongside, and the paper discloses that its [first author co-chairs the equine research committee of the American Farrier's Association](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36383438/), which donated the publishing costs.

The Swedish data add a long-term cost. Among [12,161 Standardbreds](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13167801/), those that raced more than 30% barefoot as 3-year-olds had a 14% higher hazard of ending their career at a given time point than those racing 5% or less; the authors call the loss in days minor, about 123 days off a median male career of 1,501 for horses racing more than half their starts barefoot.

## Wild horses make a shaky benchmark

The argument that horses manage fine without shoes is older than it looks. A 1934 article in *The Classical Journal* records the skeptics' reply to the claim that mountain horses needed shoes: ["wild horses and asses in their natural state do very well among rocks and stones."](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Journals/CJ/29/9/Recent_Light_on_the_Roman_Horseshoe*.html)

Hampson, Pollitt and colleagues at the University of Queensland's Australian Brumby Research Unit took the question into the outback, where GPS collars on feral horses logged an average of [**15.9 km a day**](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21059064/), far more than managed domestic horses walk. Measuring the left forefeet of [100 Australian feral horses](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23356367/) from five populations, the team found that 37 of the 40 parameters differed significantly between populations.

The pattern followed the ground: ["Harder substrates and longer travel distances were associated with short hoof walls and minimal hoof wall flaring. Softer substrates and moderate travel distances were associated with long flared walls, similar to that of typical untrimmed feet of domestic horses."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23356367/)

The companion paper, published in 2013, is blunt about the health of those feet: [**377 gross foot abnormalities**](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23356368/) across the 100 forefeet, only three feet clean, and the conclusion that ["it may not be appropriate for the feral horse foot to be the benchmark model for equine foot health."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23356368/)

On Sable Island in Canada, where [human interference has been prohibited by federal law since 1961](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10581359/), a survey examined 356 hooves from 101 dead horses, each picked because it had at least one abnormal hoof, so the figures are not a prevalence rate. Among those horses, long heels showed up in 85% and cracking in 62%.

Breeding plays a part too. Swedish trotter data put [heritability at 0.17 to 0.28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11863800/) for the share of races a horse runs barefoot, so part of the difference between horses is inherited.

## Nailed shoes are hard to date

The Romans had the hipposandal, which the British Museum describes as ["a form of temporary shoe that could be fastened to the hoof for use on metalled roads"](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/iron-hipposandal/zQEOymgPuckeKA), probably meant for draught animals. Nailed shoes started a long-running debate, because doubters argued that the [horseshoes found at many Roman sites](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Journals/CJ/29/9/Recent_Light_on_the_Roman_Horseshoe*.html) had been cast by medieval horses passing through the ruins.

Summarizing the debate in 1934, Fraser, a University of Virginia scholar, reported Winkelmann's conclusion that the iron shoe nailed to the hoof was ["altogether unknown in Roman times"](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Journals/CJ/29/9/Recent_Light_on_the_Roman_Horseshoe*.html), then answered it with H. Hofmeister's report of nineteen horseshoes in the ruins of Mattium, a stronghold destroyed by the Romans in A.D. 15, where "The ruins had not been disturbed in the Middle Ages."

The museum's verdict is more cautious than Fraser's: ["Nailed horseshoes were known but rarely used by the Romans."](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/iron-hipposandal/zQEOymgPuckeKA) Hipposandals, it says, were much more common.

## The shoe trades wear for a calendar

Nothing here decides whether a particular animal should be shod; that is a conversation with a vet and a farrier who can see the horse. A hoof grows up to about a centimetre a month, and in the outback the Hampson team linked short walls to hard ground and long distances. Where work wears it faster than it grows, a shoe takes most of the wear instead, and from then on someone has to do by hand, every 6 to 12 weeks, the job the ground used to share. Strictly FYI.
