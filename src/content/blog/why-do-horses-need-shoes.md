In Swedish harness racing, taking the shoes off is the upgrade. Standardbred trotters aged three to ten raced with barefoot hind hooves in **29% of their starts**, and the 2026 *Journal of Animal Science* paper reporting that figure cites earlier work finding that **0.7 seconds per kilometre** can be gained by racing fully barefoot. Where fractions of a second decide prize money, the metal comes off.

Which is awkward for the assumption sitting inside the question. The reason a farrier turns up every six weeks has more to do with the shoe than with the horse.

## Why do horses need shoes?

A shoe is fitted to keep the ground from wearing the hoof wall away. That same *Journal of Animal Science* study says it outright: race a trotter barefoot and **"the protective properties of the shoe, which prevent the hoof from excessive wear and tear, are absent."** The complication is that the wall never stops growing, so taking the wear away does not leave the foot where it was.

## The wall never stops growing

The 2022 review **"Hoof Matters: Developing an Athletic Thoroughbred Hoof"** calls the outer layer a hoof capsule **"composed of insensitive cornified tissues"**, and records that **"the keratinized hoof capsule continues to grow in a proximodistal direction throughout life"** - from the top down, permanently.

The rate is measurable: a 2019 *Journal of the Royal Society Interface* review on hoof mechano-biology tabulates horses at **0.2 to 0.3 mm a day**, ahead of sheep and deer. The Thoroughbred review puts the dorsal wall of foals aged six to twelve months at **0.24 mm a day, plus or minus 0.08**, roughly 7 mm a month.

Thoroughbreds, the same review reports, **"completely renew the fetal hoof capsule between 120 and 165 days after birth."** A foal walks out of its first foot inside six months. Bodies keep these timetables whether or not they suit anyone, which is also the story behind [the third molars that keep arriving with nowhere to put them](/blog/why-we-have-wisdom-teeth/).

## The ground is doing the trimming

Because growth is constant, hoof length is set by whatever removes material from the bottom. A 2019 crossover study in *Animals* put **11 Quarter Horse mares** through seven weeks shod and seven weeks barefoot, then measured the difference. Sole length shrank more during the barefoot weeks, by **5.07 mm** in the right fore, the only side where that difference reached significance (p = 0.0010). Hoof angle rose slightly barefoot and fell when shod, a gap of 1.7 to 1.8 degrees.

Seven weeks of ground contact and seven weeks of shoe contact produce measurably different feet. Equilibrium answers stay dull until the numbers arrive, much as they do for [why a solid floats on top of its own liquid](/blog/why-does-ice-float/).

> The wall grows at 0.2 to 0.3 mm a day whether or not anything is wearing it off. Fix a plate underneath and the filing stops while the growing carries on, which is why the shoe, rather than the horse, sets the six-week calendar.

The 2023 shoeing trial in the *Journal of Animal Science* wrote that interval straight into its protocol: **"Horses were trimmed and re-shod at week 6."**

## The measurements cut both ways

Horan and colleagues galloped **13 Thoroughbred ex-racehorses** in aluminium, steel, GluShu (an aluminium-rubber composite) and bare feet, across turf and an artificial track, with accelerometers on the dorsal hoof wall sampling at **5,000 Hz**. Across at least **40,691 strides**, mean accelerations ran **22 to 98 g at impact** and 17 to 89 g at foot-off, and the shoeing condition moved almost every measure. Their finding, in *Animals* in 2022: **"Barefoot hooves generally experienced the lowest accelerations."**

Traction is the usual sales pitch, so the follow-up matters. Timing **389 hoof-ground interactions** for *PLoS One* in 2024, the same group reported that in the leading hindlimb, **"slip durations were also significantly longer for the aluminium shoeing condition compared to barefoot, by 3.7 ms."** The authors add that slip is not simply a fault to remove: **"some slip is essential to dissipate energy and lower stresses on the limb tissues during initial loading."** The intuitive answer and the measured one come apart often enough, and [the planet closest to the sun is not the hottest one](/blog/why-is-venus-hotter-than-mercury/) either.

Then a trial went the other way. Panos and colleagues shod the hind hooves of **14 barefoot horses** for 12 weeks, in either three-quarter fullered or plain stamp shoes, and repeated gait analysis and American Association of Equine Practitioners (AAEP) lameness scoring. Shoe type changed nothing. The shod state did: **"Lower AAEP lameness scores were observed in horses when shod compared with barefoot (P = 0.001)."** On that scale, lower is better.

|---|---|---|
| Hoof accelerations, galloping | Barefoot generally lowest | Horan et al., *Animals*, 2022 |
| Hoof slip, leading hindlimb | Aluminium 3.7 ms longer than barefoot | Horan et al., *PLoS One*, 2024 |

## The weak-hooves story is folklore

The story repeated most often online is that selective breeding has left domestic hooves too weak to cope, and that wild horses prove shoes are unnatural. Both halves come apart against the field surveys.

Hampson and colleagues measured the left forefeet of **100 Australian feral horses** from five separate populations, chosen for ground hardness and typical travel distance. **37 of the 40** morphometric parameters differed significantly between populations, and the pattern was environmental: **"Harder substrates and longer travel distances were associated with short hoof walls and minimal hoof wall flaring. Softer substrates and moderate travel distances were associated with long flared walls, similar to that of typical untrimmed feet of domestic horses."** Wild horses on soft ground grew the same long flared feet people blame on pedigree.

The companion paper is blunt about the health of those feet: **377 gross foot abnormalities** across the 100 forefeet, with only three feet clean, and the conclusion that **"it may not be appropriate for the feral horse foot to be the benchmark model for equine foot health."** A 2023 *Canadian Veterinary Journal* survey of Sable Island, left unmanaged with human interference prohibited by federal law since 1961, evaluated 356 hooves from 101 dead horses and found abnormal conformation in all **101 of 101**, long heels in 85% and cracking in 62%. Those hooves were collected because they were abnormal, so that is not a prevalence rate. An untouched foot is still not guaranteed to be a well-made one.

## So the six weeks belong to the shoe

Nothing here decides whether a particular animal should be shod; that is a conversation with a vet and a farrier who can see the horse. What the numbers do is invert the question. A hoof arrives self-managing at 7 mm a month, filed to length by the ground. Six weeks after a shoe goes between the two of them, someone is being paid to do the job the ground was doing for nothing.
