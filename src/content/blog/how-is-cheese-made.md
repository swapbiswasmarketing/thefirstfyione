The first genetically engineered product the US Food and Drug Administration approved for use in food was an enzyme for making cheese, [a Washington Post report carried by the Los Angeles Times](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1990-03-24-mn-681-story.html) said on March 24, 1990. The approval came [after 28 months of review](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1990-03-24-mn-681-story.html): chymosin made by bacteria carrying a cow gene, the enzyme cheesemakers had traditionally taken from calves' stomachs, could now go into dairy products, and an ounce of it "can cause 50 gallons of milk to thicken into curdles and whey," the report noted.

That enzyme is the hinge of the process for most cheeses. Milk turns into curd in two moves, one made by bacteria and one made by a clotting enzyme, usually chymosin, though some cheeses are [curdled with an acid instead](https://www.usdairy.com/news-articles/how-is-cheese-made). Most of what follows decides how much whey leaves the curd and how long the cheese ages.

## How is cheese made?

Most cheese is made by souring milk with bacteria, clotting its main protein, casein, with an enzyme called rennet, then cutting the curd so the liquid whey drains away; some are curdled using an acid in place of rennet. The curd is salted and shaped, and many cheeses are then pressed and aged for months or even years. On average, [about **10 pounds of milk**](https://clipsheet.strategiccommunication.wisc.edu/?p=184219) go into each pound of cheese, says a dairy researcher at the University of Wisconsin-Madison.

1. **Acidify.** [Starter cultures ferment the lactose](https://www.usdairy.com/news-articles/how-is-cheese-made), milk's natural sugar, into lactic acid.
2. **Coagulate.** Rennet's chymosin turns the milk into a gel, [similar to yogurt](https://www.usdairy.com/news-articles/how-is-cheese-made).
3. **Cut and cook.** In the University of Guelph's Cheddar procedure the curd is [cut into pieces of 0.5 cm and cooked to 39 C](https://books.lib.uoguelph.ca/cheesemakingtechnologyebook/chapter/8-3-firm-to-hard-cheese-low-temperature-provolone-cheddar/); that cooking, Wisconsin's Cedar Grove Cheese explains, [separates the whey from the curd](https://cedargrovecheese.com/how-we-make-our-cheese/).
4. **Drain, salt and press.** The whey runs off, [salt slows the starter bacteria](https://cedargrovecheese.com/how-we-make-our-cheese/), and Cheddar curd is pressed into a block, [overnight in Guelph's procedure, though commercial plants often press for as little as one hour](https://books.lib.uoguelph.ca/cheesemakingtechnologyebook/chapter/8-3-firm-to-hard-cheese-low-temperature-provolone-cheddar/).
5. **Ripen.** The cheese is [aged to develop flavor and texture](https://www.usdairy.com/news-articles/how-is-cheese-made), though some, like fresh mozzarella, are ready to eat immediately.

## The enzyme shaves a protein's hair

Casein sits in milk as tiny clusters called micelles, and a protein called kappa-casein coats each one in what dairy scientists describe as a "hairy layer," estimated at [4.5 to 7 nanometers thick, though 10 to 12 has also been reported](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10916671/). That layer keeps the clusters apart by "steric and electrostatic repulsive interactions," according to [a 2024 review of renneting](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10916671/), so liquid milk stays liquid.

Chymosin cuts kappa-casein at one bond, [between amino acids **105 and 106**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10916671/). The loose end floats off into the whey, and the stripped clusters stick together into the gel that becomes curd.

The bacteria matter here too. The acid made by the starter cultures, the University of Guelph's cheesemaking e-book says, ["is essential to aid expulsion of whey from the curd"](https://books.lib.uoguelph.ca/cheesemakingtechnologyebook/chapter/1-introduction-to-cheese-making/) and largely sets the finished cheese's moisture, flavor and texture.

Bacteria and handling also decide which cheese you get. Swiss cheese gets its eyes from gas-forming bacteria, [*Propionibacterium freudenreichii* subspecies *shermanii* or *freudenreichii*](https://books.lib.uoguelph.ca/cheesemakingtechnologyebook/chapter/8-4-firm-to-hard-cheese-high-temperature-romano-swiss/), which ferment lactic acid into propionic acid, acetic acid and carbon dioxide. Camembert is treated more gently than Cheddar: Guelph's method has the curd and whey [dipped into molds on a drain table](https://books.lib.uoguelph.ca/cheesemakingtechnologyebook/chapter/16-1-feta-cheese/), turned a few times and left overnight.

## Rennet came from calves' stomachs until microbes got a cow gene

Rennet is extracted from the abomasum, the [fourth stomach of calves, kids or lambs](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title21-vol3/xml/CFR-2024-title21-vol3-sec184-1685.xml), in the wording of US food rules. Calf rennet is [about 80% chymosin and 20% pepsin](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8949083/), and pepsin is the less precise cutter of casein. By 1990 rennet had become costly: the US dairy industry spent about **$100 million** on it each year, and its price had [risen from $50 to $85 a gallon in the past year](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1990-03-24-mn-681-story.html), the Post's report said.

The fix was the one the FDA approved: put the calf gene into a microbe and harvest the enzyme. US rules now list chymosin from three microbes, each [containing the prochymosin gene](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title21-vol3/xml/CFR-2024-title21-vol3-sec184-1685.xml): the bacterium *Escherichia coli* K-12, the yeast *Kluyveromyces marxianus* variety *lactis* and the mold *Aspergillus niger* variety *awamori*.

> "Since all cells read the same genetic code, the bacteria, now carrying so-called recombinant DNA, make rennet identical to the rennin calves make." - [The Washington Post, in the Los Angeles Times, March 24, 1990](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1990-03-24-mn-681-story.html)

The GMO Compass information service, a database set up with EU funding, reported estimates that **["between 80 and 90 per cent of cheese in the USA and Great Britain"](https://web.archive.org/web/20150326181805/http://www.gmo-compass.org/eng/database/enzymes/83.chymosin.html)** is made with chymosin from gene technology. A 2022 review in *Foods* put the shares at [70% in the United States and 90% in the United Kingdom](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8949083/). GMO Compass named brands made with *Aspergillus niger* and *Kluyveromyces lactis*, and said the enzyme is [cleaned of leftovers of the GM microorganisms](https://web.archive.org/web/20150326181805/http://www.gmo-compass.org/eng/database/enzymes/83.chymosin.html) and that most of it ends up in the whey, not the cheese.

Plants can curdle milk too. Homer's *Iliad* compared a god healing a wound to a farm trick: "As the juice of the fig-tree curdles milk, and thickens it in a moment though it is liquid," in [Samuel Butler's translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2199/pg2199-images.html).

| Coagulant | Where it comes from | Why cheesemakers use it |
|---|---|---|
| Calf rennet | Stomach lining of young calves | The traditional enzyme, mostly chymosin |
| Fermentation-produced chymosin | Bacteria, yeast or mold carrying the calf gene | Same enzyme as the calf's, for most cheese in the US and Britain |
| Microbial coagulants | [Molds such as *Rhizomucor miehei*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8949083/) | Cheap, and suits kosher, halal and vegetarian rules |
| Plant extracts | [Dried thistle flowers of the genus *Cynara*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8949083/) | Spanish and Portuguese cheeses such as Torta del Casar and Serpa |

## About ten pounds of milk go into a pound of cheese

Cheese is not dried milk. Each morning Cedar Grove's trucks bring in [an average of 120,000 pounds of milk](https://cedargrovecheese.com/how-we-make-our-cheese/), which by the next morning has become about 12,000 pounds of cheese. Most of the difference is whey. The Guelph e-book describes cheesemaking as ["the process of removing water, lactose, and some minerals from milk to produce a concentrate of milk fat and protein"](https://books.lib.uoguelph.ca/cheesemakingtechnologyebook/chapter/1-introduction-to-cheese-making/).

The law keeps count of what is left. The US federal standard for Cheddar sets [a maximum moisture content of 39 percent by weight](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title21-vol2/xml/CFR-2024-title21-vol2-sec133-113.xml), and milkfat must be at least half of the solids.

Draining off the whey, and its milk sugar, is an old skill. Pottery sieves from Neolithic farms in northern Europe, from the sixth millennium BC, analyzed in [a 2013 study in *Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature11698), held abundant milk fat, evidence that they were used "to separate fat-rich milk curds from the lactose-containing whey" by farmers who could not digest much lactose.

## The stomach-pouch origin story is a legend

The popular origin story credits a traveler whose milk curdled in a bag made from an animal. In Life magazine's 1958 *Picture Cook Book*, an Arab merchant put milk into ["a pouch made of the stomach of a suckling calf"](https://archive.org/details/picturecookbook0000henr) and found curds when he went to drink it.

James Trager's 1970 food history *The Food Book* told it with a hedge, "if we can believe legend": the merchant ["stopped in the desert for lunch"](https://archive.org/details/foodbook0000jame), and the jolting of his camel turned goats' milk, carried in a saddle bag "made typically of animal intestine," into a kind of cheese. No version names a person or date that can be checked, so the story belongs with folklore, though the stomach version at least gets the chemistry right.

The archaeology runs further back than any merchant. Residues in pottery from the Dalmatian coast of Croatia point to [cheese by 5200 cal BCE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6124750/), about 7,200 years ago.

## The oldest preserved cheese hung on mummies in China

Around the necks of some of the people buried in the Xiaohe cemetery, in China's Tarim Basin, archaeologists found pebble-sized hunks of a yellowish substance that National Geographic calls [the world's oldest cheese](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/tarim-basin-mummy-cheese-microbe-kefir). An earlier analysis of proteins in it found *Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens*, [a microbe used to produce kefir](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/tarim-basin-mummy-cheese-microbe-kefir), which US food standards treat as a cultured milk that may be labeled ["kefir cultured milk"](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title21-vol2/xml/CFR-2024-title21-vol2-sec131-112.xml). A DNA study [published in *Cell* in 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39326418/) called the Xiaohe hunks kefir cheese, put them at about 3,500 years old and traced a route by which kefir spread from Xinjiang to inland East Asia, after compiling [a genome for that microbe that is 92 percent complete](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/tarim-basin-mummy-cheese-microbe-kefir).

A broken jar in the Saqqara tomb of Ptahmes, a mayor of Memphis, held a cheese [about 3,200 years old](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-africa-45233347). Its analysts found [a dairy product from mixed sheep or goat and cow milk](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30044608/), along with peptide evidence of *Brucella melitensis*, a bacterium that causes brucellosis. They called it ["probably the most ancient archeological solid residue of cheese ever found to date"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30044608/), though the Xiaohe hunks are a few centuries older.

For most of cheese's history, rennet meant a piece of a young animal's stomach or the juice of a plant. Today most of it, in the US and Britain at least, comes from a vat of microbes carrying a cow gene: a calf enzyme that no calf made. Strictly FYI.
