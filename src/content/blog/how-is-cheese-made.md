The first genetically engineered product the US Food and Drug Administration approved for use in food was an enzyme for making cheese, [a Washington Post report carried by the Los Angeles Times](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1990-03-24-mn-681-story.html) said on March 24, 1990. The approval came [after 28 months of review](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1990-03-24-mn-681-story.html): bacteria carrying a cow gene could now make chymosin, the enzyme cheesemakers had traditionally taken from calves' stomachs. An ounce of it ["can cause 50 gallons of milk to thicken into curdles and whey,"](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1990-03-24-mn-681-story.html) the report noted.

That enzyme is the hinge of the process for most cheeses. Milk turns into curd in two moves, one made by bacteria and one made by a clotting enzyme, usually chymosin, though some cheeses are [curdled with an acid instead](https://www.usdairy.com/news-articles/how-is-cheese-made). Most of what follows decides how much whey leaves the curd and how long the cheese ages.

## How is cheese made?

Most cheese is made by souring milk with bacteria, clotting its main protein, casein, with an enzyme called rennet, then cutting the curd so the liquid whey drains away; some are [curdled using an acid](https://www.usdairy.com/news-articles/how-is-cheese-made) in place of rennet. The curd is salted and shaped, and many cheeses are then pressed and [aged for months or even years](https://www.usdairy.com/news-articles/how-is-cheese-made). On average, [about **10 pounds of milk**](https://clipsheet.strategiccommunication.wisc.edu/?p=184219) go into each pound of cheese, says a dairy researcher at the University of Wisconsin-Madison.

1. **Acidify.** [Starter cultures ferment the lactose](https://www.usdairy.com/news-articles/how-is-cheese-made), milk's natural sugar, into lactic acid.
2. **Coagulate.** Rennet's chymosin turns the milk into a gel, [similar to yogurt](https://www.usdairy.com/news-articles/how-is-cheese-made).
3. **Cut and cook.** For Cheddar the curd is [cut into small cubes and the temperature raised to about 39 C (102 F)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheesemaking), squeezing whey out of each piece.
4. **Drain, salt and press.** The whey runs off, [salt slows the starter bacteria](https://cedargrovecheese.com/how-we-make-our-cheese/), and for Cheddar, pressing overnight [allows the curd particles to bind together](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheesemaking).
5. **Ripen.** The cheese is [aged to develop flavor and texture](https://www.usdairy.com/news-articles/how-is-cheese-made), though some, like fresh mozzarella, are [ready to eat immediately](https://www.usdairy.com/news-articles/how-is-cheese-made).

In Naples, thin slices of fresh mozzarella often went onto pizzas decades before [the legend of a pizza made for a queen](/blog/who-invented-pizza/).

## The enzyme shaves a protein's hair

Casein sits in milk as tiny clusters called micelles, and a protein called kappa-casein coats each one in what dairy scientists describe as a "hairy layer," estimated at [4.5 to 7 nanometers thick](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10916671/). That layer keeps the clusters apart by "steric and electrostatic repulsive interactions," according to [a 2024 review of renneting](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10916671/), so liquid milk stays liquid.

Chymosin cuts kappa-casein at one bond, between amino acids **105 and 106**, [the same review explains](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10916671/). The loose end floats off into the whey, and the stripped clusters stick together into the gel that becomes curd.

The bacteria matter here too. Cedar Grove Cheese in Wisconsin describes its starter as a culture that sours the milk by fermenting lactose to lactic acid, which it calls ["necessary for the rennet to act"](https://cedargrovecheese.com/how-we-make-our-cheese/). Fermentation did a similar preserving job in [sushi's ancestor](/blog/where-did-sushi-originate/), fish packed with salt and rice.

Bacteria and handling also decide which cheese you get. Emmental's starter includes bacteria that make [the gas that results in the formation of bubbles in the cheese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheesemaking), its eye holes. Camembert is treated more gently than Cheddar: its whey is [allowed to drain from the curd by gravity, generally overnight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheesemaking).

## Rennet came from calves' stomachs until microbes got a cow gene

Calf rennet comes from the lining of the abomasum, [the fourth stomach chamber of young, nursing calves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rennet), and rennet from older calves holds less chymosin or none. By 1990 rennet had become costly: the US dairy industry spent about **$100 million** on it each year, and its price had [risen from $50 to $85 a gallon in the past year](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1990-03-24-mn-681-story.html), the Post's report said.

The fix was the one the FDA approved: put the calf gene into a microbe and harvest the enzyme. Today [the most widely used fermentation-produced chymosin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chymosin) comes from the fungus *Aspergillus niger* or the yeast *Kluyveromyces lactis*.

> "Since all cells read the same genetic code, the bacteria, now carrying so-called recombinant DNA, make rennet identical to the rennin calves make." - [The Washington Post, in the Los Angeles Times, March 24, 1990](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1990-03-24-mn-681-story.html)

[In 1999, about 60% of US hard cheese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chymosin) was made with it. The GMO Compass information service reported estimates that **["between 80 and 90 per cent of cheese in the USA and Great Britain"](https://web.archive.org/web/20150326181805/http://www.gmo-compass.org/eng/database/enzymes/83.chymosin.html)** is made with chymosin from gene technology, a share [Wikipedia's rennet article still gives for 2021](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rennet). The same GMO Compass page notes that [most of the chymosin is retained in the whey](https://web.archive.org/web/20150326181805/http://www.gmo-compass.org/eng/database/enzymes/83.chymosin.html), not the cheese, and the modified microbe [is killed after fermentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chymosin) before the enzyme is isolated.

Plants can curdle milk too. Homer's *Iliad* compared a god healing a wound to a farm trick: "As the juice of the fig-tree curdles milk, and thickens it in a moment though it is liquid," in [Samuel Butler's translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2199/pg2199-images.html).

| Coagulant | Where it comes from | Still used? |
|---|---|---|
| Calf rennet | [Stomach lining of nursing calves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rennet) | Yes, in some traditional European cheeses |
| Fermentation-produced chymosin | [Fungi or yeast carrying the calf gene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chymosin) | Yes, for most cheese in the US and Britain |
| Mold enzymes | [Molds such as *Rhizomucor miehei*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rennet) grown in fermenters | Yes, including for vegetarian cheese |
| Plant extracts | [Thistle or cardoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rennet), and fig juice in Homer | Yes, in some Mediterranean cheeses |

## About ten pounds of milk go into a pound of cheese

Cheese is not dried milk. Each morning Cedar Grove's trucks bring in [an average of 120,000 pounds of milk](https://cedargrovecheese.com/how-we-make-our-cheese/), which by the next morning has become about 12,000 pounds of cheese. Most of the difference is whey: the cheesemaker has to [remove most of the water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheesemaking) from the milk, because water and bacteria together make it spoil.

The whey also takes the milk sugar with it. Pottery sieves from Neolithic farms in northern Europe, from the sixth millennium BC, analyzed in [a 2013 study in *Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature11698), held abundant milk fat, evidence that they were used "to separate fat-rich milk curds from the lactose-containing whey" by farmers who could not digest much lactose.

## The stomach-pouch origin story is a legend

The popular origin story credits a traveler whose milk curdled in a pouch made from an animal's stomach. Wikipedia's history of cheese records it as [an Arab legend about an Arab trader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_cheese), and the same article points out that cheese was already known to the Sumerians. The legend names no person or date that can be checked, so it belongs with folklore, even if the chemistry in it is sound.

The archaeology runs further back. Residues in pottery from the Dalmatian coast of Croatia point to [cheese by 5200 cal BCE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6124750/), about 7,200 years ago.

## The oldest preserved cheese hung on mummies in China

Around the necks of some of the people buried in the Xiaohe cemetery, in China's Tarim Basin, archaeologists found [pebble-sized hunks of a yellowish substance](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/tarim-basin-mummy-cheese-microbe-kefir), [about 3,500 years old](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/tarim-basin-mummy-cheese-microbe-kefir): [the oldest preserved remnants of cheese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_cheese). An earlier [analysis of proteins](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/tarim-basin-mummy-cheese-microbe-kefir) in it found *Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens*, a microbe used to make a fermented cheese called kefir. A DNA study [reported in *Cell* in 2024](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/tarim-basin-mummy-cheese-microbe-kefir) then compiled [a genome for that microbe that is 92 percent complete](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/tarim-basin-mummy-cheese-microbe-kefir).

A broken jar in the Saqqara tomb of Ptahmes, a mayor of Memphis, held a cheese [about 3,200 years old](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-africa-45233347). Its analysts found [a dairy product from mixed sheep or goat and cow milk](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30044608/), along with peptide evidence of *Brucella melitensis*, a bacterium that causes brucellosis. Honey from Egyptian tombs [was reportedly still edible](/blog/why-honey-never-spoils/); this cheese came with a pathogen.

For most of cheese's history, rennet meant a piece of a young animal's stomach or the juice of a plant. Today most of it, in the US and Britain at least, comes from a vat of microbes carrying a cow gene: a calf enzyme that no calf made. Strictly FYI.
