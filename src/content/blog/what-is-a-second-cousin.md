Even with the outliers trimmed, the 4,172 pairs of second cousins in Blaine Bettinger's [Shared cM Project](https://thegeneticgenealogist.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Shared-cM-Project-Version-4.pdf) shared anywhere from **41 to 592 centimorgans (cM)** of DNA. A grandparent's first cousin averages 221 cM against a second cousin's 229, so DNA alone cannot always tell which one a match is. A family tree can, as long as each relative gets the right name.

## What is a second cousin?

A second cousin is a relative in your own generation whose [closest shared ancestors](https://blogs.mtu.edu/archives/2024/08/13/what-the-heck-is-that-types-of-cousins-and-cousins-removed/) with you are a set of **great-grandparents**, not grandparents. In practice that means **the child of one of your parents' first cousins**. The children of two first cousins are second cousins to each other.

| Relative | Name on a family tree | Closest shared ancestors | Average shared DNA (cM) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parent's sibling's child | First cousin | Your grandparents | 866 |
| First cousin's child | First cousin once removed | Your grandparents | 433 |
| Parent's first cousin | First cousin once removed | Your great-grandparents | 433 |
| Parent's first cousin's child | Second cousin | Your great-grandparents | 229 |
| Second cousin's child | Second cousin once removed | Your great-grandparents | 122 |
| Grandparent's first cousin | First cousin twice removed | Your great-great-grandparents | 221 |
| Parent's second cousin | Second cousin once removed | Your great-great-grandparents | 122 |
| Parent's second cousin's child | Third cousin | Your great-great-grandparents | 73 |

Averages are from version 4.0 of the [Shared cM Project](https://thegeneticgenealogist.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Shared-cM-Project-Version-4.pdf), March 2020. The third-cousin average counts only pairs who shared detectable DNA.

## Two counts name every cousin

Count the generations from each person up to your shared ancestors. The **cousin number is the smaller count minus one**, and the **removal is the difference between the two counts**, the arithmetic behind [FamilySearch's cousin chart](https://www.familysearch.org/en/blog/cousin-chart). You and a second cousin each sit three generations below your shared great-grandparents: three minus one makes you second cousins, and a difference of zero means no removal.

Your parent's first cousin sits two generations below those same great-grandparents. The smaller count, two, makes the relationship first cousin, and the gap of one makes it once removed. Removal counts generations, so a parent's first cousin can be around your age.

The chart reaches the cousin number through the ancestor's title. [FamilySearch](https://www.familysearch.org/en/blog/cousin-chart) says to count the greats in that title, as seen by whichever of you is closer to the ancestor, and add one, so shared great-grandparents make second cousins. For the removal, it subtracts the closer person's generation count from the farther person's.

## Your cousin's child and your parent's cousin are first cousins once removed

The [American Heritage Dictionary](https://www.ahdictionary.com/word/search.html?q=second+cousin) gives *second cousin* two senses: "A child of a first cousin of one's parent" and "A child of one's first cousin; a first cousin once removed". The two senses name two different people on one family tree, so genealogy uses only the parent's-cousin's-child sense and treats the other as loose usage.

The same dictionary defines [*removed*](https://www.ahdictionary.com/word/search.html?q=removed) with this very relative: "A first cousin's child is one's first cousin once removed." A family tree marks a generation gap with *removed*, never with a higher cousin number.

The [Michigan Tech archives](https://blogs.mtu.edu/archives/2024/08/13/what-the-heck-is-that-types-of-cousins-and-cousins-removed/) give the standard case: "Bob is the child of Alice's first cousin. This makes Bob and Alice first cousins once removed." The label runs both ways: a parent's first cousin is a **first cousin once removed** too, one generation up instead of down.

## A DNA test cannot always tell a second cousin from a grandparent's first cousin

A centimorgan is a unit of measure for the frequency of genetic recombination, and on average one corresponds to roughly 1 million base pairs of the human genome, according to the [genetics glossary at genome.gov](https://www.genome.gov/genetics-glossary/Centimorgan). The [Shared cM Project](https://thegeneticgenealogist.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Shared-cM-Project-Version-4.pdf) pooled nearly 60,000 known relationships, trimmed the top and bottom half-percent of submissions for each, and warns that some were "almost certainly entered incorrectly, which might be due to misunderstandings of 'removed' relationships in genealogy."

Two other relationships average almost exactly what second cousins do. In the [project's relationship chart](https://thegeneticgenealogist.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Shared-cM-Project-Version-4.pdf), first cousins twice removed, such as a grandparent's first cousin, averaged **221 cM**, and half first cousins once removed averaged **224 cM**. The project puts all three, along with half great-great-aunts and uncles, in one grouping and says its method "cannot definitely identify a specific relationship."

One rung further out, among the children of two second cousins, some pairs share nothing from the ancestors that make them cousins. [Simulations](https://gcbias.org/2013/12/02/how-many-genomic-blocks-do-you-share-with-a-cousin/) posted by Graham Coop's lab in the [Department of Evolution and Ecology at UC Davis](https://gcbias.org/about/) found that **roughly 1 in 100** pairs of third cousins share no blocks of their genome from that pair of ancestors, as do about a quarter of fourth cousins.

## The church's fourth degree moved from third cousins to first

Church law has counted cousins two ways. The Roman civil method adds the generations on both sides of the family, so first cousins stand in the fourth degree. The church's own method, which the [1908 Catholic Encyclopedia](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/04264a.htm) says Pope Alexander II (1061-73) treated as "peculiarly ecclesiastical law", counts only the longer of the two lines and puts first cousins in the second degree.

In 1215 the [Fourth Lateran Council](https://www.papalencyclicals.net/councils/ecum12-2.htm) decreed that "the prohibition against marriage shall not in future go beyond the fourth degree of consanguinity and of affinity", which under the church's count reaches **third cousins**. The [council's stated reasons](https://www.papalencyclicals.net/councils/ecum12-2.htm) were practical and physiological: the ban "cannot now generally be observed to further degrees without grave harm", and the number four fits because "there are four humours in the body, which is composed of the four elements."

Third cousins needed a dispensation to marry until the [1917 Code of Canon Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1917_Code_of_Canon_Law) came into effect on 19 May 1918. That Code kept the one-sided count in [canon 96](https://www.codex-iuris-canonici.de/cic17_lat_liber2.htm), counting the generations "in tractu longiore", but cut the bar to the **third degree** in [canon 1076](https://www.codex-iuris-canonici.de/cic17_lat_liber3.htm), which still reached **second cousins**.

The current Code of Canon Law, which [John Paul II gave the force of law](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/apost_constitutions/documents/hf_jp-ii_apc_25011983_sacrae-disciplinae-leges.html) from the first day of Advent in 1983, counts the Roman way. [Canon 108](https://www.vatican.va/archive/cod-iuris-canonici/eng/documents/cic_lib1-cann96-123_en.html) finds "as many degrees as there are persons in both the lines together, not counting the common ancestor", and [canon 1091](https://www.vatican.va/archive/cod-iuris-canonici/eng/documents/cic_lib4-cann998-1165_en.html) makes marriage invalid "up to and including the fourth degree". By that count the fourth degree now stops at **first cousins**: the phrase came back with a new count under it.

> "The fourth degree" drew the Catholic marriage line at the Fourth Lateran Council and draws it again in the 1983 Code. Counted along one side of the family it reached third cousins; counted across both sides, it stops at first cousins.

Second cousins stand in the sixth degree by the Roman count, and [clinical genetics](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3419292/) draws its line at them. A [2010 paper in PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2868287/) by Bittles and Black measures consanguineous marriage by couples "related as second cousins or closer", and estimates that those couples and their children make up about **10.4 percent** of the world's population.

## Cousin's Latin root named one kind of cousin

The word's Latin ancestor could be narrower than any of this. In [Lewis and Short's Latin dictionary](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.04.0059:entry=consobrinus), *consobrinus*, built on *soror* (sister), means in its restricted sense "the child of a mother's sister", though Romans more often used it for any first cousin. A father's brother's child had a word of its own, [*patruelis*](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.04.0059:entry=patruelis).

English took the wider sense and kept widening it. The [American Heritage Dictionary](https://www.ahdictionary.com/word/search.html?q=cousin) traces *cousin* to Middle English *cosin*, "a relative", and in [Hamlet](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1524/pg1524.txt) King Claudius addresses his nephew as "my cousin Hamlet, and my son".

So when someone at a family reunion introduces a cousin's child as a second cousin, one of the dictionary's two senses is on their side and the family tree is not. The tree counts the generations up to the shared ancestors, and it answers first cousin once removed. Strictly FYI.
