Fraser McInnes was trying to make a cappuccino at Cafe Bodega on Willis Street, Wellington, "in the summer of late 1989," when the milk would not froth. There was not enough fat in it, [Stuff](https://www.stuff.co.nz/life-style/food-wine/food-news/64886421/Kiwi-claims-flat-white-invention) reported in 2015, so he went over to the customer and, by his account, said: "sorry, it's a flat white."

Across the Tasman Sea, a Sydney cafe owner says he had put the same name on a menu years earlier. Alan Preston, then 29, took a lease on a shop at **394 Sussex Street** in Haymarket in the summer of 1984-85, and on the first menu of his Moors Espresso Bar the flat white sat second, under the cappuccino, according to [a site that makes his case](http://www.flatwhitehistory.com.au/) with his photographs.

Preston's own recipe for the drink asks for "at least 1 inch of froth."

## What is a flat white?

A flat white is a coffee made of espresso, often a double shot, and steamed milk finished with a thin layer of silky microfoam. It is smaller than a latte, with less milk, and flatter on top than a cappuccino, which carries more foam. Australia and New Zealand both claim to have invented it in the 1980s.

The wording follows New Zealand Story's [explainer](https://eyesonnewzealand.com/stories/who-invented-the-flat-white). Google's [2024 doodle](https://doodles.google/doodle/celebrating-the-flat-white/) for the drink added that it is traditionally served in a ceramic cup, and noted that the Oxford English Dictionary took in the term in **2011**.

## Less milk than a latte, less foam than a cappuccino

In Australia, "it's typically a 3 to 1 drink: 3 parts milk to 1 part espresso," Mark Prince writes in [CoffeeGeek's guide](https://coffeegeek.com/guides/howtos/flat-white-how-to/). His numbers are **40-60 ml of espresso**, a double shot, and **140-180 ml of steamed milk**, in a 180-240 ml serving with "only a few millimeters of visible foam on top."

Prince calls it "a drink that slots in between the traditional cappuccino, and the latte." In the historian Garritt Van Dyk's description, it blends the crema of the espresso with a thin layer of microfoam, "without the thicker foam of a latte or cappuccino." The proportions shift by country: in the USA, Prince notes, the ratio "sometimes gets pushed up to 4 parts milk, and sometimes as low as a 2:1 ratio."

## Sydney's claim rests on a photographed 1985 menu

Van Dyk, a history lecturer at Australia's University of Newcastle, notes claims "from England to New Zealand" and calls Preston's "the best case for coining the term." Preston "claims he was the first to use the term on a menu, and has documented this use through photographs," he writes.

Preston, originally from Far North Queensland, says the drink came south with him. The cafes there, by his account, offered five coffees: a short black, a long black, a cappuccino, a Vienna and a "flat." On his own menu, Van Dyk [writes](https://theconversation.com/who-invented-the-flat-white-italian-sugar-farmers-from-regional-queensland-likely-played-a-big-role-224747), Preston changed the last one to "flat white."

Van Dyk's own argument is that the drink "very likely" took root in regional Queensland. Preston says these Queensland cafes had the best espresso machines of the time, imported from Italy, and Van Dyk writes that the drink was supposedly popular in sugar-producing towns, where it "catered to the tastes of wealthy Italian growers and their families." By **1925**, he notes, Italians owned **44%** of the sugar farms in the Herbert River area.

The history on Preston's site, written by its researcher S.J. Will, goes one step further back: "We think the original Flat White was the North Queensland 'White Coffee - Flat'," as the drink was occasionally described in Golden Fleece roadhouses.

A photographed menu shows the name was in use by **1985**, but it is not the earliest record. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_white), citing the Sydney Morning Herald, notes a May 1983 review of the Sydney cafe Miller's Treat that mentions a "flat white coffee," and cites the Oxford English Dictionary as dating the term in print to 1971. Nor can any menu show that nobody poured the drink sooner. The same gap haunts [the Edo chef credited with nigiri](/blog/where-did-sushi-originate/). A 2015 report in Stuff called the drink's history murky, "with the name seemingly preceding the drink as we know it."

## Wellington's story begins with a failed cappuccino

McInnes, a Wellington hospitality veteran, told the story of the milk that would not froth to Stuff in January 2015, about 25 years after the night in **late 1989** it describes. His business partner, he said, then put the drink on the menu blackboard, and McInnes gave it a definition of his own: "A Wellington flat white is a failed cappuccino."

New Zealand Story files the Wellington version as "one popular New Zealand story." Its verdict: "The truth is that there is no single agreed origin story." Coffee's own discovery legend cannot even settle [whether its herdsman kept camels or goats](/blog/where-does-coffee-come-from/). McInnes himself allowed that other flat whites "may have preceded his," among them an Auckland version made with a single shot and warm milk.

Craig Miller, who was about to publish a history of Wellington's coffee houses, told Stuff the flat white was a "generic drink" with no clear original. The one he remembered making in Auckland in the mid-1980s, from an Australian recipe, was a double shot of black coffee with a jug of milk on the side.

## The two founding stories disagree about the foam

Modern guides ask for a thin layer of foam. Preston's recipe, signed with his name on the same site, starts with "30 ml of dark roasted Arabica essence in a ceramic cup" and milk "frothed as you would for a Cappuccino," and the inch of froth is there "to create the meniscus." Latte art is out: "NO ART!"

> "The idea that a Flat White has no froth is a complete nonsense." - Alan Preston

McInnes's version, by his account, began as a cappuccino gone wrong, and Stuff described the result as a double shot "topped with a silky layer of foam." Like Belgium and France over [the first fried potato](/blog/why-are-french-fries-called-french-fries/), the two countries are claiming one name for drinks that differed from city to city.

## Starbucks credited Sydney when it brought the drink to America

Starbucks put the flat white on its US menus from **January 6, 2015**, [Time reported](https://time.com/3652676/starbucks-flat-white/), made with two ristretto shots and whole milk steamed to microfoam. The Starbucks website said the drink was thought to have been created in 1980s Sydney, Stuff noted, and a company statement "made no mention of New Zealand." McInnes told Stuff he was "chuffed" to see it drunk around the world.

Google's doodle split the difference: many speculate, it said, that the drink "popped up on menus in Sydney and Auckland around the same time."

The man with the 1985 photographs insists on at least an inch of froth. The popular Wellington story begins with milk that would not froth properly, and a modern guide asks for a few millimeters. All three answer to the same name. Strictly FYI.
