The question went around a lunch table at the [**1995** National Association of Music Merchants trade show](https://priceonomics.com/the-inventor-of-auto-tune/): "What needs to be invented?" Andy Hildebrand had asked it of a few friends and their wives, and one of the women half-jokingly answered, "Why don't you make a box that will let me sing in tune?"

Nobody took it up. "I looked around the table and everyone was just kind of looking down at their lunch plates," Hildebrand told [Priceonomics](https://priceonomics.com/the-inventor-of-auto-tune/) in 2015, "so I thought, 'Geez, that must be a lousy idea', and we changed the topic."

Hildebrand, a flutist with a doctorate in electrical engineering, had spent more than a decade in the oil industry. He spent the next six months on other projects, until the suggestion came back to him. "It just kind of clicked in my head," he [said](https://priceonomics.com/the-inventor-of-auto-tune/).

## When was autotune invented?

Auto-Tune was invented in **early 1996**, when engineer **Andy Hildebrand** wrote the program over a few months, according to [Priceonomics](https://priceonomics.com/the-inventor-of-auto-tune/), and it [went on sale in **1997**](https://www.antarestech.com/about). A 2009 [Time](https://time.com/archive/6687593/auto-tune-why-pop-music-sounds-perfect/) feature gives the same few months a later end: "Auto-Tune was born in late 1996." Its robotic side [became famous in **1998**](https://www.grammy.com/news/dr-andy-hildebrand-honored-recording-academy-special-merit-award/), on Cher's "Believe."

The Recording Academy says Hildebrand's company, Antares, released the first version in [the spring of 1997](https://www.grammy.com/news/dr-andy-hildebrand-honored-recording-academy-special-merit-award/). Sound On Sound reviewed it in its August 1997 issue, when it was a plug-in "only available for Pro Tools TDM systems," as reviewer Paul White [recalled in 1998](https://www.soundonsound.com/reviews/antares-atr1).

## September 19, 1997 is the date of a later version

**September 19, 1997**, often given as Auto-Tune's release date, comes from a [news page on Antares's website](https://web.archive.org/web/20000819080205/http://www.antarestech.com/files/news.html) archived in 2000. That page's entry for 9/19/97 reads: "Auto-Tune is now stand-alone. It is shipped with AudioStream, the Antares stand-alone host program and can run on any Macintosh."

Sound On Sound had already [reviewed the Pro Tools plug-in](https://www.soundonsound.com/reviews/antares-atr1) in its August issue. Antares's own [company history](https://www.antarestech.com/about) settles for the year: Auto-Tune "was first released in 1997."

## Hildebrand learned the math mapping oil fields

Long before Auto-Tune, Simon Reynolds wrote in [Pitchfork](https://pitchfork.com/features/article/how-auto-tune-revolutionized-the-sound-of-popular-music/), Hildebrand was "helping the oil giant Exxon find drilling sites." Using "a mathematical formula called autocorrelation," [Time](https://time.com/archive/6687593/auto-tune-why-pop-music-sounds-perfect/) reported, he "would send sound waves into the ground and record their reflections, providing an accurate map of potential drill sites."

**Autocorrelation** measures how closely a signal matches [a delayed copy of itself](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocorrelation). "In the late '80s," the [Recording Academy](https://www.grammy.com/news/dr-andy-hildebrand-honored-recording-academy-special-merit-award/) says, Hildebrand "left a lucrative career pioneering 3-D seismic-mapping technologies in the oil industry to go to music school," where he studied composition at Rice University.

## The oil-drilling origin story is about the inventor, not the software

The oil career invites a tidy story in which Auto-Tune is oil-exploration technology put to work on music. Its [patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5973252A/en) never mentions oil or geophysics, and it states its aim plainly: "The purpose of the invention is to correct intonation errors of vocals or other soloists in real time in studio and performance conditions."

What crossed over was the inventor's math. Antares's [company history](https://www.antarestech.com/about) says he realized "the same techniques he'd used to map oil fields could track and correct the pitch of a human voice."

## Autocorrelation finds the repeat in a sung note

A sung note is a sound wave that repeats, and its pitch is set by how often it repeats. Hildebrand's patent, US **5,973,252**, granted in October 1999, [puts it in one line](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5973252A/en): "Determining the pitch of a sound is equivalent to determining the period of repetition of the waveform."

Slide a copy of a repeating wave along the original, and when the shift equals exactly one period, the two line up again. The method is "never fooled by the changing waveform," Hildebrand told [Priceonomics](https://priceonomics.com/the-inventor-of-auto-tune/), and the obstacle was the sheer volume of arithmetic, until he realized that "most of the arithmetic was redundant" and his simplification "changed **a million multiply adds into just four**."

With the pitch measured, the software moves the voice onto the closest note in a chosen scale, working, in [the patent's words](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5973252A/en), "by adding or dropping cycles of the musical note waveform as it is re-sampled."

## Hildebrand tells the zero-setting story two ways

A speed control sets how fast the correction happens. As Hildebrand [recalled it](https://pitchfork.com/features/article/how-auto-tune-revolutionized-the-sound-of-popular-music/) years later, "I built in a dial where you could adjust the speed from 1 (fastest) to 10 (slowest). Just for kicks, I put a 'zero' setting, which changed the pitch the exact moment it received the signal." In 2009 [Time](https://time.com/archive/6687593/auto-tune-why-pop-music-sounds-perfect/) gave the control a different scale, one that "can be set from zero to 400," on which Hildebrand said a setting of 10 brings the output "halfway to the target pitch in 10 milliseconds."

Talking to the writer [David Friedman](https://ironicsans.ghost.io/blame-this-man-for-auto-tune/) in 2014, he credited the setting to his director of marketing, who asked, "What's the harm in letting it go to instantaneous?"

> "I disagreed. I thought nobody in their right mind would ever use it that way," Hildebrand told Friedman.

The patent leans toward the second telling. In [its words](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5973252A/en), a value of zero ends up "giving instantaneous pitch changes," and instant changes are "objectionable when the human voice is being processed." At the fastest settings, [Reynolds writes](https://pitchfork.com/features/article/how-auto-tune-revolutionized-the-sound-of-popular-music/), each note is "pegged to an exact pitch," the slides a singer makes between notes vanish, and the result is the sound first heard on "Believe."

## The vocoder credit on "Believe" was a cover story

Cher's "Believe" began what the [Official Charts Company](https://www.officialcharts.com/chart-news/official-charts-flashback-1998-cher-believe__20834/) calls "a seven-week reign at Number 1" in Britain in 1998.

When Sound On Sound covered the recording in [February 1999](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/recording-cher-believe), producer Mark Taylor, who made the record with Brian Rawling, said he tried a Korg VC10, "a very rare, very groovy-looking analogue vocoder from the '70s," found "the results just weren't clear enough," and got the effect from a DigiTech Talker pedal instead.

The magazine took him at his word and put the vocal down to "vocoding and filtering," until a [footnote](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/recording-cher-believe) added later said the producers had been "apparently so keen to maintain their 'trade secret' process that they were willing to attribute the effect to the (then) recently-released Digitech Talker vocoder pedal." The vocoder story came from the people who made the record.

A vocoder needs a second sound to shape. On the Korg, Taylor told [Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/recording-cher-believe), "You just play the keyboard to provide a vocoder carrier signal, sing into the microphone to produce the modulator signal, and off you go." Auto-Tune needs no carrier: it works on the singer's own voice, [tracking and correcting its pitch](https://www.antarestech.com/about).

## Studios used it discreetly before "Believe"

The same [footnote](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/recording-cher-believe) calls "Believe" "the first commercial recording to feature the audible side-effects of Antares Auto-Tune software used as a deliberate creative effect," a sound it says became known as the "Cher effect." By Reynolds's count, Auto-Tune had been on the market for about a year when "Believe" came out in October 1998, and "its previous appearances had been discreet, as its makers, Antares Audio Technologies, intended," he [wrote](https://pitchfork.com/features/article/how-auto-tune-revolutionized-the-sound-of-popular-music/).

Until then, the [Recording Academy](https://www.grammy.com/news/dr-andy-hildebrand-honored-recording-academy-special-merit-award/) says, Auto-Tune was "a closely held studio secret, employed to discreetly polish vocal tracks."

## The robot voice came back in the mid-2000s

T-Pain, then a little-known rapper and singer, stumbled onto the Cher effect in **2003** while Auto-Tuning his own vocals, and since his **2005** debut album he had sent "a dozen slightly raunchy, mechanically cheery singles into the Top 10," [Time](https://time.com/archive/6687593/auto-tune-why-pop-music-sounds-perfect/) reported in 2009. His own account, to [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2014/11/13/363759981/t-pain-rises-above-the-haters) in 2014, is less accidental: he first heard the effect on a Jennifer Lopez song in a TV commercial, which set off "a two-year search for Auto-Tune."

By **June 2009** it was common enough for Jay-Z to premiere "D.O.A. (Death of Auto-Tune)" on New York's Hot 97, with the line "Get back to rap, you T-Pain-ing too much," [MTV News reported](https://web.archive.org/web/20090606182259/http://www.mtv.com/news/articles/1613390/20090606/jay_z.jhtml). One inspiration, he said, was hearing Auto-Tune in [a Wendy's commercial](https://web.archive.org/web/20090613021555/http://www.mtv.com/news/articles/1613694/20090610/jay_z.jhtml).

The box a lunch guest asked for was meant to correct pitch without anyone hearing it. The sound that made it famous is the artifact, from a zero setting its inventor, in one telling, said nobody in their right mind would use. Strictly FYI.
