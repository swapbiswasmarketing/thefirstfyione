Every wildlife film about the African plains eventually cuts to the same shot: a knot of hyenas shoving each other around a carcass in the dark, with that rising giggle running over the top of the shoving. It plays like a crowd enjoying itself. Almost nobody watching stops to ask which hyena in the pile is making the noise.

It is the one losing.

## Why do hyenas laugh?

Spotted hyenas giggle mostly during food fights, and researchers read the call as a sign of submission and frustration, not amusement. Observers in the wild had long heard it from animals kept off a kill by higher-ranking clanmates, and a study led by the bioacoustician Nicolas Mathevon found that its acoustic structure can carry the caller's age, individual identity and rank.

The study worked with **17 spotted hyenas** in the captive colony at the University of California, Berkeley's field station, where the team recorded [254 giggle bouts containing 1,807 notes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2859383/) for a 2010 paper in BMC Ecology. The paper credits the context to earlier field observation: giggles are often produced during food contests, by animals "prevented from securing access to the kill by the intervention of higher ranking individuals", and can also follow a simple threat away from food.

None of the Berkeley giggles was recorded at a carcass. A researcher teased each animal with a bone or a piece of meat through the enclosure fence, usually with its cage-mate shut away to prevent fights, and the team judged that the giggling came from frustration at food held out of reach rather than from harassment or chase. The authors concede that working with captive animals biased what they observed. The recordings then showed what else was leaking out.

## Pitch tracks the age, wobble tracks the rank

A giggle is a bout of very short notes, typically about seven of them in under a second, [each averaging **69 milliseconds** and pitched around **547 Hz**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2859383/). Two properties of those notes vary between animals in ways a listening clanmate could read.

The first is pitch, set by how often the sound wave repeats. Across the colony, mean fundamental frequency ran [from about 650 Hz at two years old to under 450 Hz by twenty](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2859383/), a decline of roughly **30 percent**. Those are different animals compared at different ages, not one hyena followed for life, but a giggle could still work as an approximate birth certificate, read aloud at the worst possible moment. Pitch, together with timbre, also helped tell one individual from another.

The second is steadiness. Measured across all of an animal's notes, the timbre varied **more in subordinate animals than in dominant ones**, and the gap was smaller in older animals. No single note gave rank away, so the authors see the bout, with its many notes, as what would let a listener judge rank at all.

So a more uneven giggle points to the lower-ranking hyena of a pair, at least in this sample, where rank meant whether an animal dominated or deferred to its cage-mates. Sex left no reliable acoustic trace, though most of the animals studied had been exposed to hormone-blocking drugs before birth or neutered for other research, and the authors say the prenatal treatments may have affected sex-linked features.

## The whoop is the hyena's other famous call

The hyena's other famous sound is not a giggle at all. It is the **whoop**, the long rising call that carries across open country, and the two work nothing alike.

Neither one is the whole vocabulary. Mathevon's paper, citing earlier field studies, puts the repertoire at [more than ten different vocalizations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2859383/), and notes that giggles, though made at close quarters, "are loud and propagate over great distances". The whoop is the long-range specialist: it [can be heard up to 5 km away](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9297016/) and usually comes in bouts of 2 to 34 calls.

| | The giggle | The whoop |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Staccato notes of about 69 ms | Long rising calls, 2 to 34 per bout |
| Situation | Food contests, being displaced from a kill | Long-distance contact |
| Reach | Made at close range, loud enough to carry | Audible up to **5 km** |
| Carries clues to | Age, rank, individual identity | Individual identity, but no clan accent |

That last cell comes from the field. Kenna Lehmann and co-authors analysed [514 whoops from 39 hyenas across four clans](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9297016/) in Kenya's Maasai Mara for a 2022 paper in Proceedings of the Royal Society B. On the animals recorded often enough to test, a classifier picked out the individual caller **54 percent of the time against 9 percent expected by chance**. Asked which clan the caller belonged to across the full set, the same approach managed **32 percent against a chance level of 24 percent**, which the authors judged no more accurate than chance. Their whoops carry voices, not accents.

## The Lion King version is close to backwards

The pop-culture hyena cackles because it is amused, mocking something weaker than itself. That version is folklore, assembled backwards from a noise that happens to land near human laughter, and the recordings point the other way. In the field accounts, giggling comes from the animal on the losing end of a food fight, and the [Animal Diversity Web account of the spotted hyena](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Crocuta_crocuta/) notes the call is associated with fear or excitement and is often given when an individual is being chased.

That account and the Berkeley one differ on fear versus frustration, but both put the giggle in the mouth of the hyena that is not getting what it wants. Mathevon's team then floated a hypothesis that inverts the scene entirely.

> "A dominant hyena might allow a subordinate hyena access to food to prevent it from giggling and attracting further attention from unwanted competitors."

On that reading, the giggle is closer to a car alarm than a joke. It is noisy, it advertises trouble to everyone in earshot, and the cheapest way to switch it off is to let the complainer eat.

## The famous laugh belongs to the spotted hyena

Hyaenidae is usually counted as [four living species, each in its own genus](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Hyaenidae/): the spotted hyena, the striped hyena, the brown hyena and the aardwolf, though the American Society of Mammalogists now [splits the aardwolf into two species](https://www.mammaldiversity.org/taxon/1006680/). The brown hyena [whines and squeals in submission to dominant clanmates](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Hyaena_brunnea/), and Mathevon's paper notes that it lacks the giggle altogether. The striped hyena's [vocal communication is "not highly developed"](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Hyaena_hyaena/), running mainly to soft growls, although a 1941 survey of the mammals of British India reported that its voice ["has been described as a chattering laugh"](https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.19258/2015.19258.The-Fauna-Of-British-India-Including-Ceylon-And-Burma-Mammalia-Vol-ii_djvu.txt). The [aardwolf calls only when startled, fighting or stressed](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Proteles_cristata/), between meals that by one estimate run to **300,000 termites a night**.

The doglike look misleads too. The Mammal Diversity Database places [Hyaenidae in the suborder Feliformia](https://www.mammaldiversity.org/taxon/1006680/), the cat side of the order Carnivora, while [the domestic dog sits in Caniformia](https://www.mammaldiversity.org/taxon/1005940/). The cackling dog of the popular image gets the animal and the sound wrong in the same breath.

Back at the carcass, the soundtrack stops sounding cheerful once the grammar is obvious. Every giggle in that scrum carries clues to roughly how old the caller is, which individual it is, and that it is currently losing out at dinner. The most famous laugh in the animal kingdom is a complaint with a name tag attached. Strictly FYI.
