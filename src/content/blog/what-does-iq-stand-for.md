An 8-year-old with a mental age of six has an intelligence quotient of **0.75** in the worked example of William Stern's 1912 book on testing schoolchildren, and so, in the words of its [English translation](https://archive.org/details/psychologicalmet00steruoft), does "a 12-year old child with a mental age of 9." The [German original](https://archive.org/details/diepsychologisch00ster) states the rule as a one-line formula, IQ. = IA. / LA., and a key in small type under its preface, signed at Breslau in October 1912, spells out the letters: Intelligenzquotient, Intelligenzalter for the age level a child reaches on the tests, and Lebensalter for the child's age in years.

Stern, a psychologist at the University of Breslau, built the book out of an hour-long address to German psychologists in Berlin in April 1912, as his [preface](https://archive.org/details/diepsychologisch00ster) explains. His quotient was a plain fraction of one age over another, so a child exactly at the level of his age scored 1 and a child behind it scored less.

## What does IQ stand for?

IQ stands for **intelligence quotient**. The name comes from Intelligenzquotient, the German term [William Stern proposed in a 1912 book](https://archive.org/details/diepsychologisch00ster) for a child's mental age divided by actual age. Modern tests have stopped dividing one age by another: they [compare a person's score with others of the same age](https://web.archive.org/web/20161104214157/http://www.americanscientist.org/issues/feature/rising-scores-on-intelligence-tests/99999), on a scale where **100 is the average**.

[Merriam-Webster's entry for IQ](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/IQ) gives the age-group meaning as sense 1a, "a score determined by one's performance on a standardized intelligence test relative to the average performance of others of the same age," and the ratio as sense 1b, "the ratio of the mental age (as reported on a standardized test) to the chronological age multiplied by 100." A quotient is "the number resulting from the division of one number by another," in the [same dictionary](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/quotient), from Latin quotiens, "how many times." When Stern chose IQ's two letters, the second one named an actual division.

## Binet's tests gave an age, and Stern divided it

The tests came from France. Alfred Binet and Theodore Simon published their first series in 1905, [thirty tests arranged in order of difficulty](https://archive.org/details/developmentinte00binegoog), running from following a lighted match with the eyes and pointing to a named part of the body up to reversing the hands of a clock and defining abstract terms. Their [scale of 1908](https://archive.org/details/developmentinte00binegoog) grouped the tests by age, from three years, where a child points to his nose, eyes and mouth and repeats two figures, up to thirteen. A child's result was what Binet called an intellectual level and English-speaking testers a **mental age**: a 6-year-old who passed the tests usually passed by 6-year-olds, and nothing beyond, had a mental age of six, matching his real age.

Before Stern, examiners subtracted: a mental age two years below a child's real age meant, in the [English translation of Stern's book](https://archive.org/details/psychologicalmet00steruoft), that "the child's mental development is two years behind the normal level of his age." Stern objected that "this difference does not mean the same thing at different ages," and [his book](https://archive.org/details/cu31924013425560) recommends instead "the relating to chronological age not of the difference, but of the mental age itself." He did not claim the idea alone, [writing](https://archive.org/details/cu31924013425560) that "Bobertag had already conceived a plan of this sort."

The resulting quotient, Stern [wrote](https://archive.org/details/psychologicalmet00steruoft), "shows what fractional part of the intelligence normal to his age a feeble-minded child attains," using the period's term for a child with an intellectual disability. Binet did not live to see it. He died in Paris on [October 18, 1911](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb11892077m), six months before Stern's address in Berlin.

## Stern's English translator called it the mental quotient

[Guy Montrose Whipple's 1914 translation](https://archive.org/details/cu31924013425560), made at Cornell University, renders the term as the **mental quotient**, and the psychologist Cyril Burt called it the mental ratio, as [David Wechsler later noted](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.58005). Merriam-Webster dates the English phrase intelligence quotient to [1913](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/intelligence%20quotient) and the abbreviation IQ to [1915](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/IQ).

[Lewis Terman's 1916 handbook](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/20662) for his Stanford revision of the Binet scale recommended working out IQs with a slide rule or a division table, because each score was a division. Its [footnote definition](https://archive.org/details/measurementofint00termuoft) reads, "The intelligence quotient (often designated as I Q) is the ratio of mental age to chronological age," and the handbook writes the letters with a space between them throughout.

Terman also turned the fraction into a percentage: a correct scale, [his handbook](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/20662) says, must give "a median intelligence quotient of unity, or 100 per cent," and it writes scores as whole numbers: "100 I Q means exactly average intelligence." His Stanford revision [became the most popular test in the United States for decades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence_quotient).

## Adults broke the arithmetic

For children the arithmetic was simple: "if a child of 10 attains an M.A. of 12, his I.Q. is 1.20 or, omitting the decimal point, 120," as [David Wechsler summed up the method](https://archive.org/details/measurementofadu00wech). The division worked only while mental age kept rising.

Because measured intelligence "appears to improve but little after the age of 15 or 16 years," [Terman's 1916 handbook](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/20662) counted any person over 16, however old, as just 16 years old for purposes of calculating I Q. In the [handbook's own example](https://archive.org/details/measurementofint00termuoft), a youth of 18 and a man of 60 who both test at a mental age of 12 get the same IQ, 12 divided by 16, or **.75**, a score the handbook elsewhere writes as a whole number, "75 I Q."

Wechsler, chief psychologist at Bellevue Psychiatric Hospital in New York, argued in the [1941 edition of his book The Measurement of Adult Intelligence](https://archive.org/details/measurementofadu00wech) that a fixed adult age of 14, 15 or 16 compared adults with "some favored, usually optimally functioning, age group" instead of people their own age.

> "As soon as the age factor is discarded, the I.Q. ceases to be an I.Q. in the original sense of the term." - David Wechsler, [*The Measurement of Adult Intelligence*](https://archive.org/details/measurementofadu00wech), 1941

## Wechsler kept the 100 and dropped mental age

Wechsler's own adult scale, the Wechsler-Bellevue, which his book first presented in April **1939**, gave up mental age: "we have abandoned the M.A. as a base for calculating I.Q.'s," he [wrote](https://archive.org/details/measurementofadu00wech). He kept a quotient of his own, defining an IQ in [his book](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.58005) as "the ratio between a particular score which an individual gets (on a given intelligence test) and the score which an average individual of his life age may be assumed to attain on the same test."

Each adult was compared with people of the same age: the average kept the old 100, and the bottom edge of the middle half of each age group was set at 90. An individual's IQ, [Wechsler explained](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.58005), "merely defines his relative position among the group with which he has been compared." Arthur Otis had [proposed such a deviation score in 1917](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IQ_classification), and Wechsler's scale popularized it.

The Stanford-Binet followed in its third edition of 1960, which added deviation IQs without eliminating mental age and ratio IQ, converting ratio IQs to a scale with a [standard deviation of 16](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford%E2%80%93Binet_Intelligence_Scales). On a current Wechsler test for children, the WISC-V, the Full Scale IQ is on "a standard score metric with a mean of 100 and an SD of 15," in the words of [the publisher's sample score report](https://www.pearsonassessments.com/content/dam/school/global/clinical/us/assets/wisc-v/wisc-v-interpretive-report.pdf).

| | Ratio IQ | Deviation IQ |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Stern, 1912; times 100 from Terman, 1916 | Proposed by Arthur Otis, 1917; popularized by Wechsler's adult scale, 1939 |
| How it is found | Mental age divided by actual age | Raw score placed among people the same age |
| A score of 100 means | Mental age equals actual age | Exactly average for the age group |
| Adults | Counted as 16 years old (Terman) | Compared with their own age group |
| Example | Age 10, mental age 12: IQ 120 | One standard deviation above average: IQ 115 |

## Two-thirds of people score between 85 and 115

On the modern scale, [about two-thirds of people score between 85 and 115](https://web.archive.org/web/20161104214157/http://www.americanscientist.org/issues/feature/rising-scores-on-intelligence-tests/99999), and about 2.25 percent each score above 130 and below 70, Ulric Neisser wrote in American Scientist in 1997. The WISC-V's [publisher](https://www.pearsonassessments.com/content/dam/school/global/clinical/us/assets/wisc-v/wisc-v-interpretive-report.pdf) draws a narrower average band: "scores ranging from 90 to 109 are typically considered average."

Terman's [1916 table of labels](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/20662) began "'Near' genius or genius" above 140 and called 120 to 140 "very superior intelligence," with 90 to 110 counted as "Normal, or average, intelligence." He [warned](https://archive.org/details/measurementofint00termuoft) that the boundary lines between such groups "are absolutely arbitrary, a matter of definition only." Wechsler made a related point in his book: "The numerical value of an I.Q. has no more fixed meaning than a passing mark on a scholastic examination," [he wrote](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.58005). By the 1937 revision of Terman's test, [the word genius was no longer used for any IQ score range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IQ_classification).

## The famous formula is the retired one

The formula still quoted as the meaning of IQ, mental age divided by actual age and multiplied by a hundred, survives in [Merriam-Webster's entry for the word](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/IQ) as sense 1b, listed after the age-group meaning. As an account of a modern test it is out of date, because modern scores rank a person among others the same age instead of being worked out from a second age. Older ratio-IQ tests were [still sometimes used at least as recently as 2007](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_age), for children who scored too high for a modern test to measure precisely.

Stern hoped his book would show readers the gaps in the work "so plainly as to prevent overhasty attempts at practical application," he wrote in its [preface](https://archive.org/details/psychologicalmet00steruoft). More than a century later his two letters are still the name of the score, though the score no longer divides one age by another. Strictly FYI.
