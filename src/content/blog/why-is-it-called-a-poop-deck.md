The poop deck was not where sailors went to the toilet. The crew's toilets sat at the far end of the hull, [holes cut in a plank overhanging the water in the bow](https://ussconstitutionmuseum.org/2014/01/18/head-lines/), and the sailors' word for them was the head. A man walking aft toward the poop left the crew's toilet behind him at the far end of the ship.

The name is a collision of two unrelated English words that happen to be spelled the same.

## Why is it called a poop deck?

The poop deck is named after the poop, the old word for a ship's stern, which came into English from French and goes back to the Latin *puppis*. [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/poop) traces that poop through Anglo-French *pope* to Latin *puppis*, dates it to the 15th century, and defines the modern poop as "an enclosed superstructure at the stern of a ship above the main deck." The compound came later, and [Merriam-Webster gives its first known use as 1717](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/poop%20deck). The raised deck at the back was named for the part of the hull it sits on.

## The two dates are not close

The Oxford English Dictionary's earliest example of the ship sense, quoted by [Grammarphobia](https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2017/04/pooped.html), is from **1489**, in an English translation of a treatise Christine de Pisan had written in 1410: "The pouppe whiche is the hindermost partye of the shippe." English borrowed the word from Middle French *la poupe*.

The other poop grew out of a noise. When the verb turns up in the Middle Ages, in Chaucer around 1390, the OED defines it, [Grammarphobia reports](https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2017/04/pooped.html), as "to produce a short blast of sound, as with a horn; to blow, toot." In the late 1600s that sense became nursery slang for breaking wind, and the dictionary's earliest example is Richard Hogarth's **1689** etymological dictionary *Gazophylacium Anglicanum*: "To poop, from the Belg. Poepen, to fart softly: both from the sound."

The excrement senses came last. [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/poop) dates the verb meaning "defecate" to 1882 and the noun meaning "excrement" to circa 1890. [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/poop) puts the excrement noun much earlier, at **1744**, as "a children's euphemism," so the dictionaries disagree by well over a century.

On the most generous dating available, the ship's poop beats even the politest bodily sense into English by two centuries. Even on the earliest date any dictionary here gives for poop meaning excrement, the deck already had its compound name.

## The story is folklore, assembled backwards

The belief that the deck is named after what sailors did there rests on a pun, and the pun needs a meaning the word did not carry when the stern got its name. The deck simply took the name of the part of the hull it covered.

## There really was a toilet at that end of the ship

The myth misses the word but lands at the right end of the ship for something real. A 2009 report from North Carolina's [Queen Anne's Revenge Shipwreck Project](https://www.qaronline.org/seat-ease-sanitary-facilities-shipwreck-31cr314-queen-annes-revenge-site/open) says sanitary fittings called seats of ease were in common use aboard ships by the late seventeenth century, with the crew's on each side of the bow and drainage pipes running to the sea, while "for the captain and officers, these accommodations were located at the ship's stern area, known as the quarter galleries."

The [USS Constitution Museum](https://ussconstitutionmuseum.org/2014/01/18/head-lines/) describes quarter galleries as "the lavishly decorated projections overhanging the ship's stern quarters to port and starboard." On that American frigate, [the museum records](https://ussconstitutionmuseum.org/2014/01/18/head-lines/), the crew's head consisted "of four or six holes cut in a plank overhanging the water in the bow of the ship," while the captain's "private privy was located in one of the quarter galleries."

One of those fittings has been dug up. On 19 May 2005, archaeologists recovered a flat tapered lead artifact, catalogued QAR632.000, from the stern area of North Carolina shipwreck site 31CR314, believed to be Blackbeard's flagship *Queen Anne's Revenge*, lost in 1718 at Beaufort Inlet, [the project's report says](https://www.qaronline.org/seat-ease-sanitary-facilities-shipwreck-31cr314-queen-annes-revenge-site/open). The lead tube measures **31.8 inches** long, and [the report identifies it as a seat of ease](https://www.qaronline.org/seat-ease-sanitary-facilities-shipwreck-31cr314-queen-annes-revenge-site/open), probably from the lower level of a quarter gallery. The report is careful about its own evidence and notes an outside chance the piece sat in the bow for crew use and moved to the edge of the wreckage after the sinking.

> The myth gets the word wrong and the geography nearly right. There was a toilet at the stern of a large sailing ship, in the quarter galleries, and the captain used it.

## The poop was the highest deck at the back

Admiral William Henry Smyth's [*The Sailor's Word-Book*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/26000), published in London in 1867, two years after his death, defines poop as "the aftermost and highest part of a large ship's hull," and also as a deck raised over the after-part of a spar-deck, sometimes called the round-house. The word names a place on the ship, not anything done there. Smyth's entry for the poop-lantern shows one use for that high stern: [a light "carried by admirals to denote the flag-ship by night."](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/26000)

Frigates went without the structure altogether, and [Smyth catches both meanings of the word in one line](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/26000): "A frigate has no poop, but is said to be pooped when a wave strikes the stern and washes on board."

## Pooped for exhausted is a separate claim, and it is unproven

There is a genuine nautical verb in play. [Smyth defines pooping](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/26000) as "the breaking of a heavy sea over the stern or quarter of a boat or vessel when she scuds before the wind in a gale, which is extremely dangerous, especially if deeply laden." The verb is older than his book: the satire [*A Voyage to Cacklogallinia*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/16202), published in London in 1727 under the name Captain Samuel Brunt, has its narrator scudding "altogether ignorant where we were; for a Sea which pooped us the second Day, had carried away the Binnacle with the Two Compasses." The folk version runs that verb straight into the modern sense of being worn out.

The lexicography does not cooperate. [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/poop) dates the verb "to become exhausted" to 1927 and lists its origin as unknown. [Grammarphobia](https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2017/04/pooped.html) quotes the OED's earliest example of pooped meaning exhausted, from Leonard H. Nason's 1928 novel *Sergeant Eadie* ("I haven't had any sleep in two nights, and I'm a little poobed [sic]"), and after looking for the nautical connection it concluded: "Well, it's a compelling question, but we haven't found any evidence of a link."

So the deck kept a Latin word for the back of a boat, and centuries later an old word for a toot became nursery slang that happened to sound identical. The joke is younger than the deck it is told about, and it is told about the end of a big sailing ship where the captain really did have a toilet. Strictly FYI.
