The poop deck was not where sailors went to the toilet. The crew's latrines sat at the far end of the hull, cut into planking that overhung the water at the bow, and the sailors' word for them was the **head**. A man walking aft toward the poop left the crew's toilet behind him at the far end of the ship.

The name is a collision of two unrelated English words, and one of them is about three hundred years older than the other.

## Why is it called a poop deck?

The poop deck is named from the Latin *puppis*, meaning a ship's stern, which reached English around 1400 through the Old French *poupe*. That naval sense predates the English word poop for excrement by roughly three centuries, so the raised stern deck was named after the part of the hull it sits on.

## The two dates are not close

Etymonline dates the ship sense of poop to **c. 1400**, borrowed from Old French *poupe*, "stern of a ship" (14c.), by way of Old Provencal or Italian *poppa*, from Latin *puppis*, a word of uncertain origin. The Oxford English Dictionary's earliest example of that sense, quoted by [Grammarphobia](https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2017/04/pooped.html), is from **1489**, in an English translation of a treatise Christine de Pisan had written in 1410: "The pouppe whiche is the hindermost partye of the shippe."

The other poop arrives much later and from a different direction entirely. [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/poop) records the sense "to break wind softly" from **1721** and "excrement" from **1744**, calling the latter a children's euphemism of probably imitative origin, and traces it back to a late 14th century verb *poupen* meaning to make a short blast on a horn. Grammarphobia's OED-based account pushes the flatulence sense back further, to **1689**, in Richard Hogarth's etymological dictionary *Gazophylacium Anglicanum*.

On the most generous dating available, the ship's poop still beats the other one into English by two hundred years. The compound is the youngest entry on the list at **1779**, which means English had a poop on its ships for nearly four centuries before anyone bolted the word deck onto it. Setting two dates beside each other settles the question in a way that assertion cannot, which is also how anyone pinned down [how long a moment used to be](/blog/how-long-is-a-moment/).

## The story is folklore, assembled backwards

The belief that the deck is named after what sailors did there was built out of a modern pun and then reasoned into a history. It could not have been the reason for the name, because when the deck took its name the word carried no such meaning in English. Folk etymology moves fast when a word looks obvious, which is the same instinct that makes [the shortened spelling of Christmas](/blog/why-do-people-say-xmas/) look like a modern invention.

## There really was a toilet at that end of the ship

The myth misses the word and lands one deck away from something real. From the late seventeenth century, sailing ships carried sanitary fittings called seats of ease. A 2009 report from North Carolina's [Queen Anne's Revenge Shipwreck Project](https://www.qaronline.org/seat-ease-sanitary-facilities-shipwreck-31cr314-queen-annes-revenge-site/open) describes the arrangement plainly: for the crew they sat on each side of the bow with drainage pipes running to the sea, while "for the captain and officers, these accommodations were located at the ship's stern area, known as the quarter galleries."

Quarter galleries are the balconied projections on either side of the stern, tucked directly inside the structure a poop deck roofs. The [USS Constitution Museum](https://ussconstitutionmuseum.org/2014/01/18/head-lines/) records the same split on an American frigate: the crew's heads were "four or six holes cut in a plank overhanging the water in the bow of the ship," while the captain's "private privy was located in one of the quarter galleries."

One of those fittings has been dug up. On **19 May 2005**, archaeologists recovered a flat tapered lead artifact, catalogued QAR632.000, from the stern area of North Carolina shipwreck site 31CR314, believed to be Blackbeard's flagship *Queen Anne's Revenge*, lost in 1718 at Beaufort Inlet. It measures **31.8 inches** long and was identified as a seat of ease. The report is careful about its own evidence and notes an outside chance the piece drifted aft from the bow after the wreck.

> The myth gets the word wrong and the geography nearly right. There was a toilet at the stern of a large sailing ship, in the quarter galleries under the poop, and it belonged to the captain.

## The poop was the highest deck at the back

Admiral William Henry Smyth's *The Sailor's Word-Book*, published in London in **1867**, defines poop as "the aftermost and highest part of a large ship's hull," and also "a deck raised over the after-part of a spar-deck, sometimes called the round-house." Height at the stern is all the word describes. Smyth's entry for the **poop-lantern** shows one thing that height was good for: a light "carried by admirals to denote the flag-ship by night."

Smaller warships went without the structure altogether, and Smyth catches both meanings of the word in one line: "A frigate has no poop, but is said to be pooped when a wave strikes the stern and washes on board."

Ship parts take their names from position. The same plain logic runs through most working kit, including [the leather leggings a cowboy wears over his jeans](/blog/why-do-cowboys-wear-chaps/).

## Pooped for exhausted is a separate claim, and it is unproven

There is a genuine nautical verb in play. Smyth defines pooping as "the breaking of a heavy sea over the stern or quarter of a boat or vessel when she scuds before the wind in a gale, which is extremely dangerous, especially if deeply laden." The folk version runs it straight into the modern sense of being worn out.

The lexicography does not cooperate. The OED's earliest example of pooped meaning exhausted is from Leonard Nason's **1928** novel *Sergeant Eadie*: "I haven't had any sleep in two nights, and I'm a little poobed [sic]." Etymonline dates the verb "become tired" to **1931** and lists it as of unknown origin. Grammarphobia, having gone looking for the nautical connection, wrote: "Well, it's a compelling question, but we haven't found any evidence of a link."

So the deck kept a Latin word for the back of a boat, and three hundred years later English picked up a nursery word that happened to sound identical. The joke is younger than the deck it is told about, and it happens to be told standing directly above a real toilet that almost nobody telling it knows was there. Strictly FYI.
