Hold a compass flat and turn slowly on the spot. The case turns with you, but the needle swings back to the same line every time and settles with its north end pointing roughly north.

That end is the north pole of a small magnet, named for the direction it seeks, and unlike magnetic poles attract. So the end of Earth's field that the needle's north end lines up toward has to be magnetically south, and the magnetic pole in the Arctic is exactly that.

## How does a compass work?

A compass works because its needle is a small magnet that can turn freely. Earth's magnetic field, generated by a [dynamo process in the fluid outer core](https://geomag.colorado.edu/historical-main-field-change-and-declination) of the planet, exerts a torque that swings the needle into line with the **horizontal direction of the field** where it sits. Its north end then shows **magnetic north**, which usually differs from **true north**.

The basic requirement, in NOAA's [geomagnetism FAQ](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/geomagnetism-frequently-asked-questions), is that "the compass needle must be free to rotate and align with the magnetic field." Modern compasses usually seal the needle in a capsule filled with liquid, which [damps its movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass) so it settles sooner.

## The pole in the Arctic is magnetically a south pole

Magnet poles were named for what they do. Hang a magnet freely and one end turns north, so the two ends became the north and south poles, "or more properly, north-seeking and south-seeking poles," as the OpenStax [College Physics](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/22-1-magnets) textbook puts it.

Compasses were in use before magnetism was fully understood, the University of Colorado's [geomagnetism group](https://geomag.colorado.edu/historical-main-field-change-and-declination) notes. The needle's ends already had their names, and the magnetic pole near the geographic North Pole was called north too, for where it lies. In physics terms it is a **south magnetic pole**, where magnetic field lines enter the Earth, and convention keeps the name "irregardless of the physics."

The field also slants into the ground in the north. At the north magnetic pole a dip needle stands vertical with its north end down, and at the south magnetic pole its north end points up, the [NOAA FAQ](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/geomagnetism-frequently-asked-questions) explains. Compasses built for the northern hemisphere therefore carry a small weight on the south end of the needle to hold it level, and southern-hemisphere models carry it on the north end; without the switch, the needle would not rotate freely.

## The needle follows the field where it sits

Field lines do not run straight to the poles, and the needle follows the lines. Its heading "can also differ substantially from the direction to the Magnetic North Pole," the [Colorado group](https://geomag.colorado.edu/historical-main-field-change-and-declination) writes, "since magnetic field lines are not just great circles connecting the magnetic poles."

"It is a common misconception that the needle of a magnetic compass points to a particular spot on the earth's surface," Larry Gedney wrote in a 1985 column for the University of Alaska Fairbanks [Geophysical Institute](https://www.gi.alaska.edu/alaska-science-forum/why-compasses-dont-point-north). The poles, he explained, are "not centers of attraction at all, but simply blurry localities" where the field lines meet the surface at right angles. Those pole areas also move "tens to hundreds of kilometers because of daily variations and magnetic storms," according to [NOAA](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/geomagnetism-frequently-asked-questions).

> "The compass points in the directions of the horizontal component of the magnetic field where the compass is located, and not to any single point," NOAA's geomagnetism FAQ states.

## Magnetic north and true north rarely agree

The angle between them is the **magnetic declination**, and it can shift over short distances: "A mile or two away the magnetic declination may be considerably different," [NOAA warns](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/geomagnetism-frequently-asked-questions). It also drifts over the years.

In 1985 a compass over most of Alaska pointed between **20 and 30 degrees east** of true north, [Gedney wrote](https://www.gi.alaska.edu/alaska-science-forum/why-compasses-dont-point-north). At Greenwich, compass needles had pointed west of true north since around **1660**, according to the [British Geological Survey](https://www.bgs.ac.uk/news/hold-onto-your-compass-true-north-and-magnetic-north-cross-at-greenwich-for-first-time-in-360-years/). The historical field model that NOAA's [declination calculator](https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/geomag/calculators/magcalc.shtml) uses for those years puts the westward swing at its widest, about **24 degrees**, around 1820. In **September 2019** Greenwich compasses pointed true north again, for the first time in **about 360 years**, [Royal Museums Greenwich](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/topics/true-north-magnetic-north-whats-difference) reported.

Magnetic storms, which Gedney put down to particles emitted by the sun during bursts of solar activity, and which Alaskans tie to spells of [intense auroral activity](/blog/what-causes-the-northern-lights/), can swing the needle within hours. They are worse at northern latitudes, where the field lines are steeply inclined and the horizontal component that a compass responds to is less stable. Modern measurements at Sitka had recorded erratic deflections of over **10 degrees** in the horizontal field "in a period of only hours," [Gedney reported](https://www.gi.alaska.edu/alaska-science-forum/why-compasses-dont-point-north).

## The earliest clear description already saw the needle miss due south

By Joseph Needham's reckoning in [*Science and Civilisation in China*](https://archive.org/details/sciencecivilisat0004need), the earliest clear description of a magnetic needle compass in any language is in Shen Kuo's *Dream Pool Essays*, written about **1088**, a century before the earliest European mention of the compass. Shen was a scholar-official of the Song dynasty. In Needham's translation, a needle rubbed with lodestone "is able to point to the south. But it always inclines slightly to the east, and does not point directly at the south." Shen advised hanging it from "a single cocoon fibre of new silk."

Needham called that "a clear statement of the magnetic declination," four centuries before the [traditional story](https://archive.org/details/sciencecivilisat0004need) that Columbus discovered declination on his voyage of 1492. The historian Crichton Mitchell, one of Needham's sources, examined that tradition and rejected it. Mitchell and three other scholars agreed instead that by 1450 at the latest, German makers of portable sundials with built-in compasses were putting a special mark on the dial for the needle, which shows working knowledge of the declination.

## Magnetic north sped up, then braked

The north magnetic pole wanders. James Clark Ross located it in northern Canada in **1831**, [NOAA records](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/wandering-geomagnetic-poles). By 2019 it had been in Canada for 400 years and was heading toward Russia, according to the [British Geological Survey](https://www.bgs.ac.uk/news/update-to-essential-scientific-reference-maps-reveals-new-movement-of-the-earths-magnetic-field/), which tracks its speed:

| Period | How fast magnetic north moved |
|---|---|
| 1600 to 1990 | About 10 km a year |
| The 2000s | Up to 55 km a year, a record |
| 2019 | Around 50 km a year |
| By 2025 | Slowed to about 35 km a year |

BGS called the slowdown "a deceleration that has not been seen before." Liquid iron moving in the outer core drags the field along "in an unpredictable way," [the survey explains](https://www.bgs.ac.uk/news/update-to-essential-scientific-reference-maps-reveals-new-movement-of-the-earths-magnetic-field/).

## Phones and runways use a five-year forecast of the field

A phone has no needle. It reads the field with a built-in digital compass and corrects for declination with the joint US-UK [World Magnetic Model](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/world-magnetic-model), which NOAA says "comes pre-installed in Android and iOS devices, thereby bringing its use to more than a billion devices around the world."

Because changes in the field "are difficult to predict," the model is reissued every five years, and the current version, WMM2025, was released on **December 17, 2024**, and will remain valid until late 2029, [NOAA states](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/world-magnetic-model). The 2015 edition did not last its five years: in February 2019 NOAA put out an [out-of-cycle update](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/news/world-magnetic-model-out-cycle-release) because the pole was "moving quickly away from the Canadian Arctic toward Siberia."

Under the FAA's [Aeronautical Information Manual](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap2_section_3.html), a runway's number is "the whole number nearest one-tenth the magnetic azimuth of the centerline of the runway, measured clockwise from the magnetic north." Runway 17 therefore points about 170 degrees from magnetic north. NOAA calls runways "perhaps the most visible example of a navigation aid updated to match shifts in Earth's magnetic field," in its [February 2019 announcement](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/news/world-magnetic-model-out-cycle-release).

The needle in your hand is a small north pole, lined up by a field whose Arctic end is magnetically south, and it answers to the field where you stand, not to a spot on a map. That spot is still drifting, about 35 km a year at the last count. Strictly FYI.
