Dry-cleaned clothes are washed in a liquid. About **1 gallon** of perc solvent goes into the drum for every **2 pounds** of clothing, and the load tumbles in it for **5 to 10 minutes**, according to a [1995 review](https://publications.iarc.who.int/download/mono63-5.pdf) by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC).

Cleaning with volatile liquids was once known as *French cleaning* in Scotland and *chemical cleaning* in Germany, the same review records, and such processes eventually came to be called dry cleaning because they use no water. Even that is not quite true of the perc wash: detergent and a small amount of water go into the tank with the solvent to help dissolve dirt.

## How does dry cleaning work?

Dry cleaning washes clothes in a liquid solvent instead of water, most often **perchloroethylene (perc)**. The load tumbles in solvent in a washer-like machine; the solvent is drained, spun out and evaporated with warm air, then recovered for reuse. Unlike water, the solvent does not make fibers swell, the change that can shrink or wrinkle fabric.

## Water swells the fibers that solvent leaves alone

Cotton fibers typically contain about **90 percent** cellulose by dry weight, according to a [2024 paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11555694/) in the *Biochemical Journal*. The long cellulose chains are held side by side by hydrogen bonds.

In a wash, water gets into the loosely packed regions of the fiber and breaks those bonds, so the chains can shift. New bonds form in the swollen fiber and lock in as it dries, which leaves cotton wrinkled and shrunk, as a [2019 study](https://fashionandtextiles.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s40691-018-0160-2) of shrink-resistant cotton in *Fashion and Textiles* describes.

Wool is a protein fiber whose polar and charged groups form hydrogen bonds with water, and it can hold up to about **a third of its weight** in water, according to a [2025 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12523852/) in *Animals*. Its surface is covered in overlapping scales.

When wet wool is tumbled with heat and pressure, the scale edge of one fiber locks into the gap between scales on another, like a ratchet, and the fibers cannot slide back, a [2019 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6369147/) of shrink-proofing treatments explains. That felting is useful in blankets and felted rugs and a serious problem in clothing.

> The [IARC review](https://publications.iarc.who.int/download/mono63-5.pdf) explains the difference: "Aqueous solvents swell the hydrophilic textile fibres, so that the dimensions of the fibre change, which can cause wrinkles or shrinkage of the fabric. Nonaqueous dry cleaning solvents do not swell the fibres and can remove oily stains at low temperatures."

## A drained load still holds gallons of solvent

Stained clothes are first "spotted" with chemicals. [The IARC](https://publications.iarc.who.int/download/mono63-5.pdf) takes its figures for the rest of the perc process from the Kirk-Othmer Encyclopedia of Chemical Technology.

| Step | What happens | Perc in the load |
|---|---|---|
| Wash, 5 to 10 min | Clothes tumble in solvent at 75 to 80 F as it cycles through filters | About 50 gallons per 100 pounds (1 gallon per 2 pounds) |
| Drain | Solvent runs out through a strainer that traps buttons, coins and pins | 11 to 12 gallons left per 100 pounds |
| Spin, 3 to 5 min | The drum turns at 350 to 450 revolutions per minute | 2 to 3 gallons left per 100 pounds |
| Dry, 10 to 25 min | Air at 120 to 150 F evaporates the rest | Condensed back to liquid |

The filters, often coated with diatomaceous earth and activated carbon, remove loose dyes and insoluble dirt, and most dry cleaners also distill the solvent for reuse. Afterward the clothes are pressed with steam.

## Early dry cleaners washed clothes in gasoline and turpentine

US dry cleaning dates to the 1800s, according to a [2021 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7973082/) in *Frontiers in Public Health*, when people "washed fabrics in open tubs with solvents such as gasoline, kerosene, benzene, turpentine, and petroleum and then hung to dry."

Those petroleum liquids caused many fires and explosions, and the [same review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7973082/) lists carbon tetrachloride among the nonflammable solvents the trade turned to. It was imported from Germany in 1898 and sold as a dry-cleaning and spot-removing agent called Carbona, and US dry cleaners used an estimated **45 million pounds** of it in 1940, according to a [chronology](https://web.archive.org/web/20121021093948/https://drycleancoalition.org/download/drycleaning-historical_developments.pdf) compiled by a coalition of state programs that clean up dry-cleaner sites.

Petroleum solvents got safer too. In the 1920s W.J. Stoddard, an Atlanta dry cleaner and president of the National Institute of Drycleaning, helped develop specifications for one with a higher flash point, which became known as Stoddard solvent, the [coalition's chronology](https://web.archive.org/web/20121021093948/https://drycleancoalition.org/download/drycleaning-historical_developments.pdf) records.

Perc overtook carbon tetrachloride as the leading chlorinated solvent in 1948, and carbon tetrachloride was dropped in the 1950s over its toxicity and the corrosion it caused in equipment; petroleum solvents still led overall until perc became the US solvent of choice in **1962**, by the [same chronology](https://web.archive.org/web/20121021093948/https://drycleancoalition.org/download/drycleaning-historical_developments.pdf).

## The spilled-lamp origin story is unverified

The [IARC](https://publications.iarc.who.int/download/mono63-5.pdf) opens its history with a hedge, saying dry cleaning "is believed to have originated in France in 1825," and relays an account from the French Federation of Dyeing and Cleaning in which a servant in the Paris household of Jean-Baptiste Jolly "spilled the contents of a lamp on a soiled tablecloth." When the cloth dried, the story goes, the spots were gone.

The monograph guesses the lamp held camphene, but that name was coined in an [1838 US patent](https://aoghs.org/products/camphene-to-kerosene-lamps/) for a lamp burning distilled spirits of turpentine, 13 years after the supposed spill. One trade retelling swaps in kerosene: a 2025 column by Howard Bradley in [Laundry and Cleaning News](https://www.laundryandcleaningnews.com/analysis/drycleaning-the-french-way/) describes "an accidental spillage of kerosene from an oil lamp," then asks what the lamp held, since "Kerosene was not invented until 1846."

Even that date is the inventor's own claim. Abraham Gesner wrote in his [1861 treatise](https://archive.org/details/apracticaltreat02gesngoog/page/n16/mode/2up) that he had made oil from coal and burned it in lamps at public lectures on Prince Edward Island in August 1846. His lamp fuel, which he named kerosene, was [patented in 1854](https://aoghs.org/products/camphene-to-kerosene-lamps/), and a factory on Long Island, New York, soon began making it.

None of these versions cites a record made at the time, so the spill remains an unverified story. A firm bearing his name is easier to place: the [coalition's chronology](https://web.archive.org/web/20121021093948/https://drycleancoalition.org/download/drycleaning-historical_developments.pdf) dates a commercial dry-cleaning business in Paris run by the firm of Jolly-Belin, using spirits of turpentine, to the 1840s.

A document with a firm date points to New York. A [list of patents](https://archive.org/details/us-secretary-of-state-patent-list-1821/page/n5/mode/1up) that Secretary of State John Quincy Adams sent to the Speaker of the House, dated January 1, 1822, records one issued on **March 3, 1821**, to Thomas L. Jennings of New York for "Dry scouring clothes." The list gives only "the dates, and general objects" of patents, so it does not say how his method worked.

Jennings was born to a free Black family in New York City and trained as a tailor, according to the [Lemelson-MIT Program](https://lemelson.mit.edu/resources/thomas-jennings), which notes that his patent file was among about **10,000** early patents lost to a fire in 1836.

## Perc's share has fallen since 1991

In 1991, perc was used in an estimated **28,121** US dry-cleaning plants, and **82 percent** of commercial dry cleaners used it, by EPA figures the [IARC](https://publications.iarc.who.int/download/mono63-5.pdf) cites. The [2021 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7973082/) put the share using it as their main solvent at **60 to 65 percent**, with most of the rest using a high-flashpoint hydrocarbon, a petroleum solvent built from hydrogen and carbon. The EPA's December 2024 rule [estimated](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-12-18/html/2024-30117.htm) that **6,000** dry cleaners still use perc.

Other options include siloxane, liquid carbon dioxide and professional wet cleaning, in which "fabrics are cleaned with water and detergent in a computer-controlled washing machine," and a 2012 ranking by the Toxics Use Reduction Institute placed wet cleaning alone in its most-preferred group, [the review reports](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7973082/).

[California's air board](https://ww2.arb.ca.gov/our-work/programs/phase-out-perchloroethylene-dry-cleaning-process/about) voted in January 2007 to phase out perc dry-cleaning machines, with a deadline of January 1, 2023. In December 2024 the EPA [issued a final rule](https://www.epa.gov/assessing-and-managing-chemicals-under-tsca/risk-management-perchloroethylene-pce) that set a **10-year** phase-out of perc in dry cleaning and barred its use in newly acquired machines after 6 months. The agency based the rule on [neurotoxicity](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-12-18/html/2024-30117.htm), harm to the nervous system, from chronic exposure. In July 2025 it [opened a public comment period](https://www.epa.gov/assessing-and-managing-chemicals-under-tsca/risk-management-perchloroethylene-pce) as the first step to reconsider and, if appropriate, revise the rule.

The coat that comes back from the dry cleaner spent its wash soaked in solvent that will be filtered, distilled and used again on the next customer's clothes. Strictly FYI.
