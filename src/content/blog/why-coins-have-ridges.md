Run a thumbnail around the edge of a quarter and you will feel them: tiny, even grooves, all the way around. The [US Mint's coin specifications](https://web.archive.org/web/20240531154731/https://www.usmint.gov/learn/coin-and-medal-programs/coin-specifications) put the count at **119** on a quarter and **118** on a dime. Now check a penny and a nickel: smooth. The difference is the fossil of a crime wave.

## Why do coins have ridges?

Coins have ridges because they were once made of silver and gold, and people [shaved small amounts of metal off them](https://www.perthmint.com/news/collector/coin-collecting/how-isaac-newton-helped-shape-our-coins/) for profit, then spent the lightened coin as if it were whole. A machine-made patterned edge, called **reeding**, or ridged or grooved [in American usage and milled in British usage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reeding), made that theft hard to hide.

The [Royal Mint Museum](https://www.royalmintmuseum.org.uk/journal/british-monarchs/charles-ii/) says milling and edge lettering made coins harder to counterfeit and "prevented the coinage from being clipped; a dishonest process whereby a small amount of gold or silver was shaved from the edges."

## A hammered coin was an open invitation

Until the 1660s, the [Royal Mint Museum explains](https://www.royalmintmuseum.org.uk/journal/history/making-money/), English coins were struck between a pair of hand-held dies, the upper one taking blows from a hammer.

Coins made that way came out irregular. In the historian [Thomas Babington Macaulay's account](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2613), "few pieces were exactly round; and the rims were not marked," and from the reign of Elizabeth I a clipper faced the penalties of high treason. Shave a whisper of silver from the rim and pass the coin on. Do it to a thousand coins and you have a pile of silver that came out of everyone else's pockets.

## The milled edge arrived in 1662, under Charles II

The fix was machinery. According to the [Royal Mint Museum's history of the reign](https://www.royalmintmuseum.org.uk/journal/british-monarchs/charles-ii/), the mint in the Tower of London made room for horse-drawn rolling mills, edge-marking machinery and large screw presses, and production began in **February 1662** on the gold coinage, with machine-struck half crowns and shillings following in 1663.

The equipment was the responsibility of a Frenchman, **Pierre Blondeau**, who operated the machines that applied edge lettering and milling himself. He made the few Mint staff who knew the process swear an oath of secrecy, a practice the [museum says](https://www.royalmintmuseum.org.uk/journal/british-monarchs/charles-ii/) continued until 1811.

Some coins carried words instead of grooves. The five guinea piece, the crown and the half crown bore the edge inscription **DECUS ET TUTAMEN**, which the [museum translates](https://www.royalmintmuseum.org.uk/journal/british-monarchs/charles-ii/) as "an ornament and a safeguard" and describes as "an added security measure."

## Good coins vanished, and clipped coins stayed

The new money had a flaw that no machine could fix: the hammered coins and the milled coins, in [Macaulay's words](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2613/pg2613-images.html#link2HCH0005), "were current together," and "the perfect coin will not drive the light coin out of circulation, but will itself be driven out." A good milled crown was worth more in the crucible or across the Channel, so of the new coins, [Macaulay writes](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2613), "Great masses were melted down; great masses exported; great masses hoarded."

Meanwhile the shears kept working. [Macaulay writes](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2613) that for more than thirty years "this evil had gone on increasing", and that on one morning "seven men were hanged and a woman burned for clipping."

By the autumn of 1695 the damage could be measured with scales. Three London goldsmiths each sent a hundred pounds in current silver to be weighed, and [Macaulay reports](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2613/pg2613-images.html#link2HCH0005) the result: "Three hundred pounds ought to have weighed about twelve hundred ounces. The actual weight proved to be six hundred and twenty-four ounces." That London sample held barely half the silver it claimed.

> "It was a mere chance whether what was called a shilling was really tenpence, sixpence or a groat." - [Macaulay](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2613), on English money in 1695

## Isaac Newton came to clean up, not to invent

**Isaac Newton** was appointed Warden of the Royal Mint in the spring of **1696**, and the [Royal Mint Museum's biography](https://www.royalmintmuseum.org.uk/journal/people/isaac-newton/) says he arrived at the Tower that April to find the Mint "grappling with the recoinage of old silver coins dating back to the reign of Elizabeth I and beyond."

The recoinage was finished within three years, after which, in the [museum's words](https://www.royalmintmuseum.org.uk/journal/people/isaac-newton/), Newton had more time for "his main duty of investigating and bringing to justice those who clipped and counterfeited the coin of the realm," and he conducted interviews with criminals and informers himself.

[Among his targets](https://www.newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/view/texts/normalized/MINT00847) was **William Chaloner**, a coiner with advice for Parliament. In a [1697 pamphlet addressed to the House of Commons](https://www.newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/view/texts/normalized/MINT01536), Chaloner proposed that coins "should be Mill'd with a Hollow, or Groove," so that nobody could counterfeit them by casting. When he accused Newton of conniving at abuses in the Mint, the [pamphlet life printed after his death](https://www.newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/view/texts/normalized/MINT01534) says, a parliamentary committee cleared the Warden, and Chaloner was later "Executed at Tyburn" on 22 March 1699.

## The 1698 story gets Newton backwards

[The Perth Mint](https://www.perthmint.com/news/collector/coin-collecting/how-isaac-newton-helped-shape-our-coins/) notes that "Milled edges on coins are often associated with Isaac Newton," and Wikipedia's article on reeding goes further, saying clipping was ["made more difficult through the implementation of reeding by Isaac Newton in 1698"](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Reeding&oldid=1373908745). The dates do not fit. The [Royal Mint Museum dates the end of hammered coins](https://www.royalmintmuseum.org.uk/journal/british-monarchs/james-ii/) to the reign of James II, deposed in 1688, "the first in which exclusively milled coins were struck." The milled coin edge had been in production for more than three decades when Isaac Newton was [appointed Warden of the Royal Mint in 1696](https://www.royalmintmuseum.org.uk/journal/people/isaac-newton/).

The [Perth Mint article](https://www.perthmint.com/news/collector/coin-collecting/how-isaac-newton-helped-shape-our-coins/) itself tells a different story: the new Warden found at his disposal "a new invention of rounding the money and making the edges of them with letters or grainings", and the recoinage he oversaw was "Complete by the middle of 1698". So 1698 is when the recoinage ended, not when the reeding began.

The Royal Mint Museum's verdict on Newton, who took the more lucrative, though technically less senior, post of Master on Christmas Day 1699 and held it until his death in 1727, is blunter: "he was not an innovator", it writes, and he left the Mint "very much as he found it 30 years before," though he did want coins of the [correct weight and fineness](https://www.royalmintmuseum.org.uk/journal/people/isaac-newton/). Newton's mark was on the weight of the coins and on the men who clipped them. The edge itself was older than his job.

## American dimes, quarters and half dollars got the grooves, and the nickel and cent did not

The dime [made its debut in 1796](https://web.archive.org/web/20110810142143/http://www.ngccoin.com/CoinDetail.aspx?ContentID=95) as a coin of 89.24% silver, the standard the [Coinage Act of 1792](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/United_States_Statutes_at_Large/Volume_1/2nd_Congress/1st_Session/Chapter_16) set for American silver coins. In 1828 Chief Engraver William Kneass began minting coins within "a close, reeded collar," which, [NGC explains](https://web.archive.org/web/20100726105422/http://www.ngccoin.com/CoinDetail.aspx?ContentID=98), ended the hand work of reeding each edge and "discouraged counterfeiting." Modern presses work the same way: the [Royal Mint Museum describes](https://www.royalmintmuseum.org.uk/journal/history/making-money/) a struck blank being forced outward into a collar that is "plain or milled depending on the denomination."

Until 1965 the reeded coins in American change were silver, and [Lyndon Johnson said as much](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/remarks-the-signing-the-coinage-act) when he signed the Coinage Act that year: "dimes, and quarters, and half dollars, and dollars have contained 90 percent silver," while "There is no change in the penny and the nickel."

Yet the Mint's [five-cent coin began](https://web.archive.org/web/20241105140311/https://www.usmint.gov/learn/coins-and-medals/circulating-coins/nickel) as a small silver half disme, and the copper-nickel coin people called a nickel only arrived in 1866. In the Second World War nickel was needed to harden steel for tank armor and anti-aircraft guns, and the [National Park Service records](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/currency-on-the-world-war-ii-home-front.htm) that the Mint produced wartime nickels "made from 56% copper, 35% silver, and 9% manganese from 1942 to 1945." The alloy reached the presses in October 1942, [NGC says](https://www.ngccoin.com/news/article/999/), and because the coins were to be withdrawn after the war, they carried an enlarged mint mark above Monticello's dome, and even Philadelphia's normally unmarked coins bore a large P.

Reeding was even worth faking. The Liberty Head nickel of 1883 lacked the word "CENTS", so crooks [gold-plated it and passed it off as a five-dollar gold piece](https://www.pcgs.com/coinfacts/category/nickels/liberty-nickel/type-1-no-cents-1883/674), some with edge reeds added by hand to mimic the real thing. [Later that year](https://www.pcgs.com/coinfacts/category/nickels/liberty-nickel/type-1-no-cents-1883/674) the Mint added CENTS to the design.

The [US Mint's specification table](https://web.archive.org/web/20240531154731/https://www.usmint.gov/learn/coin-and-medal-programs/coin-specifications) lines up today's edges like this:

| Coin | Edge | Reeds | Diameter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cent | Plain | None | 19.05 mm |
| Nickel | Plain | None | 21.21 mm |
| Dime | Reeded | 118 | 17.91 mm |
| Quarter | Reeded | 119 | 24.26 mm |
| Half dollar | Reeded | 150 | 30.61 mm |

## The ridges outlived the silver

The 1965 act took the silver out of the dime and the quarter and, as the [Federal Reserve Bank of New York summed it up](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/monthly_review/1967_pdf/08_4_67.pdf) in 1967, "reduced the silver content of the half-dollar to 40 per cent." The new dimes and quarters "will contain no silver," [Johnson told his audience](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/remarks-the-signing-the-coinage-act), adding, "They will show a copper edge," the copper stripe visible in the grooves today. He also promised that "Our present silver coins won't disappear and they won't even become rarities."

The promise [rested on the Treasury holding down the price of silver](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/remarks-the-signing-the-coinage-act). On July 14, 1967, the [New York Fed reported](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/monthly_review/1967_pdf/08_4_67.pdf), the Treasury stopped selling silver at the fixed $1.29 an ounce, and by early August "the silver content of subsidiary coins exceeds their face value." A [1970 law](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-84/pdf/STATUTE-84-Pg1769.pdf) let the silver half dollars be minted only "until January 1, 1971."

The reeding found new work. [The Perth Mint says](https://www.perthmint.com/news/collector/coin-collecting/how-isaac-newton-helped-shape-our-coins/) that although clipping base-metal coins "is largely pointless", milled edges remain "a useful deterrent to would-be coin counterfeiters" and help "blind people distinguish different coin denominations." The European Commission's [guide to euro coins](https://economy-finance.ec.europa.eu/euro/euro-coins-and-notes/euro-coins/types-euro-coins_en) makes the second point plainly: "Milled edges make it easier for the visually impaired to recognise different coin values." In an American pocket, the dime and the cent are only about a millimeter apart in diameter, [17.91 mm against 19.05 mm](https://web.archive.org/web/20240531154731/https://www.usmint.gov/learn/coin-and-medal-programs/coin-specifications), and the dime is the one with the grooves.

So the ridges are a message, if an accidental one: this object was once treasure, worth stealing a sliver at a time, and the mint won the arms race so thoroughly that we forgot there was ever a war. Count them yourself: all 119 of them. Strictly FYI.
