One page of Ole Worm's 17th-century book on runes shows where the story of Vikings drinking from skulls began. In the 1651 edition, stanza 25 of [Krakumal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kr%C3%A1kum%C3%A1l), the legendary Ragnar Lodbrok's death song, is printed in runes under lines of Latin, and [on page 203](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_Lf96CFoAOhQC/page/n232/mode/1up) the two disagree about what the dying king will drink his beer from in Odin's hall.

The [Latin line](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_Lf96CFoAOhQC/page/n232/mode/1up) reads *Ex concavis crateribus craniorum*, **from the hollow bowls of skulls**. The runes beneath it say *or bjugvidum hausa*, which E.V. Gordon translates as ["from curved branches of skulls"](https://archive.org/details/AnIntroductionToOldNorse/page/n37/mode/1up), a Norse poet's name for **drinking horns**. The skull cups start in the top line.

## What does skol mean?

Skol is the Scandinavian word for **cheers**, and it literally means **bowl**. It is the English spelling of *skal*, the Swedish, Danish and Norwegian toast, from Old Norse *skal*, ["bowl, drinking vessel"](https://www.etymonline.com/word/skoal). The story that it comes from *skull* is a myth, and the Viking skull cups it leans on began as a 17th-century mistranslation of a Norse phrase for drinking horns.

The Minnesota Vikings, [named partly to reflect Minnesota's Scandinavian American culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minnesota_Vikings), have used [Skol, Vikings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skol,_Vikings) as their fight song since around their first season in 1961. The Skol Chant [they added in 2016](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/what-does-skol-actually-mean/) adapts the "Viking war chant" that [Iceland's soccer fans used at Euro 2016](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minnesota_Vikings).

## The toast is named after a shared bowl

[Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/skoal) traces skoal, "also skol", to Danish *skaal*, "a toast," literally "bowl, cup", and, citing the OED, says it "might have been popularized or introduced by the visit of James VI of Scotland to Denmark in 1589." The [Dictionary of the Older Scottish Tongue](https://dsl.ac.uk/entry/dost/scoll_n) quotes a Scots poem of **about 1590**, "we drink such scoles", and marks the word as Scottish only until the 19th century.

"It means bowl," Gregg White, who taught Swedish at the American Swedish Institute, told [CBS Minnesota](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/what-does-skol-actually-mean/) in 2018, describing the custom behind it: the first person picked up the bowl, took a sip, said the word and passed it to the next.

Cleasby and Vigfusson's *Icelandic-English Dictionary* of 1874 [defines skal as "a bowl"](https://archive.org/details/icelandicenglish00cleauoft/page/n660/mode/1up), with a bowl full of mead among its examples. The [Swedish Academy's dictionary](https://www.saob.se/artikel/?unik=S_05637-0031.x384) first cites *skal* as an exclamation urging someone to drink in **1614**, centuries after the Viking Age. The [same entry](https://www.saob.se/artikel/?unik=S_05637-0031.x384) records a skal drunk in someone's honour as early as 1566, when a man swore he had never willingly drunk the King of Denmark's skal, and describes the older custom White means, a drinking vessel passed from one guest to the next.

## The skull cups are a 17th-century mistranslation

The popular version says skol comes from Vikings drinking beer out of their enemies' skulls. It is a folk etymology. White's verdict to [CBS](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/what-does-skol-actually-mean/): "That's a really wonderful myth, but it's not fact."

Worm first published *Runir seu Danica literatura antiquissima* [in Copenhagen in **1636**](https://archive.org/details/AnIntroductionToOldNorse/page/n36/mode/2up), with Krakumal among its texts and a Latin translation by **Magnus Olafsson**. In [An Introduction to Old Norse](https://archive.org/details/AnIntroductionToOldNorse/page/n36/mode/2up), E.V. Gordon says the book "supplied a conception of the viking" who was "eager to enter Valhalla and drink beer from the skulls of his enemies", and calls Olafsson "the originator of the absurdity".

Gordon calls *or bjugvidum hausa* [a kenning for drinking-horns](https://archive.org/details/AnIntroductionToOldNorse/page/n37/mode/1up), a poet's stand-in name. Cleasby and Vigfusson's dictionary agrees, glossing it as ["the crooked branches of the head, i.e. the horns"](https://archive.org/details/icelandicenglish00cleauoft/page/n891/mode/1up).

The note printed under the stanza, which Gordon credits to Olafsson, goes further than the Latin line, glossing *hausa* as *crania*, skulls, and adding: ["Sperabant Heroes se in aula Othini bibituros ex craniis eorum qvos occiderant."](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_Lf96CFoAOhQC/page/n232/mode/1up) The heroes hoped to drink in Odin's hall from the skulls of those they had killed.

> Read as a Norse poet meant it, nobody in the stanza drinks from a skull: the curved branches are horns, and the skulls are the heads they grew on.

## Mallet and Percy put the enemies into Ragnar's mouth

The skull cups reached English long before Percy, and Gordon names [a book Aylett Sammes published in 1676](https://archive.org/details/AnIntroductionToOldNorse/page/n36/mode/2up) first among those that repeated them. Sammes printed the opening of stanza 25 in runes with an English verse version ending ["Of Nappy-Ale in full-brim'd Skull"](https://archive.org/details/britannia-antiqua-illustrata-sammes/page/n458/mode/1up), and wrote that Woden's worshippers expected to drink ale in his hall "in the Skulls of their Enemies". In 1690 Sir William Temple pictured Odin's guests ["Carowsing every Man in Bowls made of the Sculls of their Enemies they had slain"](https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_miscellanea-_temple-sir-william_1690_0/page/n237/mode/1up) and cited the 25th and 29th stanzas of Ragnar's song.

For a long time, Gordon writes, ["few writers could mention a viking without telling the strange fashion of his drinking."](https://archive.org/details/AnIntroductionToOldNorse/page/n36/mode/2up)

Thomas Percy's *Five Pieces of Runic Poetry*, [printed in London in **1763**](https://archive.org/details/fivepiecesrunic00norwgoog/page/n6/mode/1up), gave the stanza the English prose version that stuck. [On page 40](https://archive.org/details/fivepiecesrunic00norwgoog/page/n61/mode/1up) the king says: "Soon, in the splendid hall of Odin, we shall drink Beer out of the sculls of our enemies." *Sculls* is an older spelling of skulls.

| Version | Date | The drinking line |
|---|---|---|
| Krakumal, in Old Norse | 12th century | *or bjugvidum hausa*: from curved branches of skulls, meaning drinking horns |
| Olafsson's Latin, in Worm's *Runir* | 1636, quoted from the 1651 edition | *ex concavis crateribus craniorum*: from the hollow bowls of skulls |
| Note on the stanza, same page | 1651 edition | *ex craniis eorum qvos occiderant*: from the skulls of those they had killed |
| Sammes, *Britannia Antiqua Illustrata* | 1676 | "in the Skulls of their Enemies" (prose); "full-brim'd Skull" (verse) |
| Mallet, *Monumens de la mythologie et de la poesie des Celtes* | 1756 | *dans les cranes de nos ennemis*: in the skulls of our enemies |
| Percy, *Five Pieces of Runic Poetry* | 1763 | "out of the sculls of our enemies" |

The Norse and Latin lines name no enemies, and Sammes and Temple mention them only in their own narration. In [Paul-Henri Mallet's French version, printed in Copenhagen in 1756](https://archive.org/details/gri_33125011171986/page/n190/mode/1up), Ragnar himself promises to drink beer *dans les cranes de nos ennemis*, in the skulls of our enemies. Percy said he translated the ode from the Icelandic original Worm published, but [his introduction to it](https://archive.org/details/fivepiecesrunic00norwgoog/page/n45/mode/1up) quotes Mallet's Edda, and his page 40 follows Mallet's French sentence by sentence. Gordon says Percy ["knew very little Norse; he translated the translations of Worm, Bartholin, and others, reproducing their errors."](https://archive.org/details/AnIntroductionToOldNorse/page/n37/mode/1up)

## Olafsson's Latin also missed a negative

Elsewhere in Krakumal, Ragnar says the pleasure of one battle was not like kissing a young widow, and the Norse carries that "not" in a suffix. Olafsson, Gordon writes, ["did not know that -at was a negative"](https://archive.org/details/AnIntroductionToOldNorse/page/n37/mode/1up), and Percy followed him. [Percy's page 34](https://archive.org/details/fivepiecesrunic00norwgoog/page/n55/mode/1up) reads: "The pleasure of that day was like kissing a young widow at the highest seat of the table."

## Skal means bowl, even when the bowl is a skull

Skal and skull may still be distant cousins. [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/skoal) says skal, originally a cup made from a shell, comes from a root meaning "to cut", and its [entry for skull](https://www.etymonline.com/word/skull) says the word probably comes from Old Norse *skalli*, "a bald head, skull", itself probably related to the Old English word for a husk and so to *shell*, from the same root. The same entry adds that early English use in the southwest points rather to a Dutch or Low German relative.

Cleasby and Vigfusson list [skal](https://archive.org/details/icelandicenglish00cleauoft/page/n660/mode/1up) as a bowl, a hollow and a pair of scales, never as a skull on its own. Their entry for the compound [*hjarn-skal*](https://archive.org/details/icelandicenglish00cleauoft/page/n385/mode/1up), from *hjarni*, brain, reads "the brain pan, the skull".

Skulls also become bowls in two Eddic poems. In [Volundarkvida](https://heimskringla.no/wiki/V%C3%B6lundarkvi%C3%B0a) the smith Volund beheads King Nidud's sons, and the poem calls their skulls *thaer skalar, er und skorum varu*, the bowls that lay under the hair, which he covers in silver and sends to the king, as [Bellows's translation](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Poetic_Edda_%28tr._Bellows%29/V%C3%B6lundarkvitha) also tells it. In [Atlamal](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Poetic_Edda_%28tr._Bellows%29/Atlamol_en_Gr%C3%B6nlenzku), Gudrun tells Atli, "The skulls of thy boys thou as beer-cups didst have", and Bellows, who suggests the line may have been borrowed from a poem of the Volund tradition, adds that "the idea doubtless came from some such source". Both are revenge stories about legendary kings, and neither describes a drinking custom.

Say skol over a drink and you name the bowl White describes going from hand to hand. The skol story's skull cups came from a translator, and even the Latin word he used for skull, *cranium*, comes from a Greek word for skull that [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/cranium) ties to a root meaning "horn; head".
