A blue cloud beside a file's name in Windows File Explorer marks a file whose contents are not stored on the computer. "A blue cloud icon next to a OneDrive file or folder indicates that the file is only available online," [Microsoft's OneDrive help page](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/onedrive/save-disk-space-with-onedrive-files-on-demand-for-windows) explains, and the file "doesn't download to your device until you open it."

The file still sits on a drive, inside a building. OneDrive's data "is mirrored into at least two different Azure regions, which are at least several hundred miles away from each other," [Microsoft says](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/onedrive/how-onedrive-safeguards-your-data-in-the-cloud), and only "a limited number of essential personnel" can get into its datacenters, which have "on-premises security officers." The icon stands in for those buildings, and the name for them began as a symbol on engineers' diagrams.

## What is the cloud?

The cloud is **computing done on someone else's computers, reached over the internet**. Storage, processing power and apps kept "in the cloud" are [hosted in remote data centers](https://www.technologyreview.com/2011/10/31/257406/who-coined-cloud-computing/) instead of on the user's own device. The name comes from the cloud that [engineers drew](https://www.technologyreview.com/2011/10/31/257406/who-coined-cloud-computing/) to stand for a network.

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology set down a formal definition in 2011. Cloud computing, [it says](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/145/final), is "a model for enabling ubiquitous, convenient, on-demand network access to a shared pool of configurable computing resources" that "can be rapidly provisioned and released with minimal management effort or service provider interaction." Under what the definition calls resource pooling, the customer "generally has no control or knowledge over the exact location of the provided resources," [NIST's glossary](https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/resource_pooling) adds, though a customer may be able to pick the country, the state or the datacenter.

NIST sorts cloud services into three models, [software](https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/software_as_a_service), [platform](https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/platform_as_a_service) and [infrastructure](https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/infrastructure_as_a_service) as a service, which differ in how much of the system the customer runs:

| Service model | What the customer uses | What the customer controls |
|---|---|---|
| Software as a service | The provider's applications, through a web browser or a program, as with web-based email | At most a few user settings |
| Platform as a service | The provider's platform, to run programs the customer made or bought, written with languages and tools the provider supports | Those programs, and possibly settings for the environment that hosts them |
| Infrastructure as a service | Processing, storage and networks, to run any software | Operating systems, storage and programs |

The same definition names [four deployment models](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/145/final) (private, community, public and hybrid), sorted by who the cloud is provisioned for. A [public cloud](https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/public_cloud) is "provisioned for open use by the general public," while a [private cloud](https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/private_cloud) is "provisioned for exclusive use by a single organization."

## Engineers drew the network as a cloud

Before anyone sold cloud computing, a cloud in an engineering drawing represented the network, and by late 1996 "telecom networks were already referred to as the cloud," [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2011/10/31/257406/who-coined-cloud-computing/) found when it traced the phrase in 2011. The machines behind a cloud service need not be "addressed or managed individually by users"; instead, "the entire provider-managed suite of hardware and software can be thought of as an amorphous cloud," [Wikipedia's article on cloud computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cloud_computing) explains.

General Magic, a start-up co-founded by Andy Hertzfeld and Bill Atkinson of the original Macintosh team, used the word for the network its software would roam, [Wired reported in April 1994](https://www.wired.com/1994/04/general-magic/). Jim White, the telecom programmer behind its Telescript system, had envisioned a network where every message was "a computer program that executed itself as it went out on the Net," and Hertzfeld told [the magazine](https://www.wired.com/1994/04/general-magic/): "The beauty of Telescript... is that now, instead of just having a device to program, we now have the entire Cloud out there, where a single program can go and travel to many different sources of information and create sort of a virtual service."

## Cloud computing began as a marketing term at Compaq in 1996

The earliest documented uses of the phrase that [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2011/10/31/257406/who-coined-cloud-computing/) could find are two documents from late 1996. On **October 29, 1996**, Sean O'Sullivan, founder of a start-up called NetCentric, wrote "Cloud Computing: The Cloud has no Borders" in his daily planner after a meeting with George Favaloro, a marketing executive at Compaq Computer. Two weeks later came a **50-page** internal Compaq analysis, dated November 14, 1996, titled "Internet Solutions Division Strategy for Cloud Computing."

Both men agree that cloud computing "was born as a marketing term," [the magazine reported](https://www.technologyreview.com/2011/10/31/257406/who-coined-cloud-computing/): they were hunting for "a slogan to link the fast-developing Internet opportunity to businesses Compaq knew about." Favaloro believes he coined it, and O'Sullivan thinks it could have been his idea.

> "Computing was bedrock for Compaq, but now this messy cloud was happening... And we needed a handle to bring those things together." - George Favaloro, in [*MIT Technology Review*](https://www.technologyreview.com/2011/10/31/257406/who-coined-cloud-computing/), 2011

NetCentric's business plan put prices on the idea: an imaginary bill for one George Favaloro charged **$4.95** for 253 [megabytes](/blog/what-is-a-byte/) of internet storage and $18.50 for 37 minutes of video conferencing, [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2011/10/31/257406/who-coined-cloud-computing/) reported. The same bill charged $3.95 to view a Mike Tyson fight.

The name did not catch on: a draft of a January 1997 Compaq press release called its investment in NetCentric part of "a strategic initiative to provide 'Cloud Computing' to businesses," until Compaq's public relations team objected and changed the phrase to "Internet computing" in the final version, [the magazine found](https://www.technologyreview.com/2011/10/31/257406/who-coined-cloud-computing/). NetCentric's May 1997 application to trademark "cloud computing" was never approved, and O'Sullivan stopped using the term as he struggled to sell an internet [fax](/blog/when-was-the-fax-machine-invented/) service. The company eventually closed.

## Google and Amazon picked the name up in 2006

Many believe the term's modern use began on **August 9, 2006**, when Google's chief executive, Eric Schmidt, used it at an industry conference, [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2011/10/31/257406/who-coined-cloud-computing/) notes. "It starts with the premise that the data services and architecture should be on servers," Schmidt said, according to [the magazine](https://www.technologyreview.com/2011/10/31/257406/who-coined-cloud-computing/). "We call it cloud computing - they should be in a 'cloud' somewhere."

Later that month, on August 24, Amazon announced the beta of a service with the word in its name, the Elastic Compute Cloud. It was "a web service that provides resizable compute capacity in the cloud," [the announcement](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2006/08/24/announcing-amazon-elastic-compute-cloud-amazon-ec2---beta/) said, and customers would pay "only for capacity that you actually use." The phrase spread the next year, [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2011/10/31/257406/who-coined-cloud-computing/) found, as Amazon, Microsoft and IBM promoted cloud efforts of their own, and on November 15, 2007, a New York Times headline read "I.B.M. to Push 'Cloud Computing,' Using Data From Afar."

## Storms reach the cloud through its buildings

In a 2012 survey conducted by Wakefield Research for the software company Citrix, **51 percent** of respondents thought inclement weather would interfere with cloud computing, [NBC News reported](https://www.nbcnews.com/technolog/survey-51-percent-think-stormy-weather-interferes-online-cloud-968907). Of the 54 percent who believed they never used the cloud, 95 percent reported doing things like banking online, storing photos on web services or using file-sharing sites.

During "a large scale electrical storm which swept through the Northern Virginia area" on the night of June 29, 2012, two months before that report, a voltage spike hit the electrical switching equipment in two of Amazon's datacenters there, and both began switching to backup generators. In one of them the generators "failed to provide stable voltage," so its servers ran on batteries; after utility power failed a second time, the batteries ran down and servers began losing power at 8:04 p.m. Pacific time, [Amazon's account of the outage](https://aws.amazon.com/message/67457/) says.

Data centers, the cloud's and everyone else's, consumed about **4.4 percent** of all US electricity in 2023 and are expected to use 6.7 to 12 percent by 2028, [the Department of Energy reported](https://www.energy.gov/articles/doe-releases-new-report-evaluating-increase-electricity-demand-data-centers). The department lists the rise of artificial intelligence applications among the forces behind that growing demand.

Engineers drew a cloud where a diagram needed the network but not the equipment inside it. That equipment fills buildings with security officers and backup generators, and a OneDrive file marked with a blue cloud is mirrored in at least two Azure regions several hundred miles apart. Strictly FYI.
