On **27 May 1644** a Ming general named Wu Sangui [rode out of Shanhai Pass, at the traditional eastern end of the Great Wall, and let the Manchu army through](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_8nXLwSG2O8AC), surrendering to its commander Dorgon at the gates. The same day the combined force [routed the peasant army of Li Zicheng, which had taken Beijing on 25 April, by the Sha River several kilometres west of the pass, and on 5 June Dorgon entered Beijing](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_8nXLwSG2O8AC), and the Manchus kept it.

The most famous failure of the Great Wall was not a breach. Nobody scaled it or knocked a hole in it. The man holding the keys turned them.

Whether that counts as the wall failing depends on what it was for, and on which wall is meant. There were several, raised centuries apart, and they do not add up to the barrier the name suggests.

## Did the Great Wall of China work?

Partly, and not in the way the question implies. The walls slowed and channelled raids rather than sealing the frontier, and doubled as a customs and signalling system that forced traffic through guarded passes. In 1550 a Mongol army that could not break the doubled walls at Xuanfu rode around them to a lightly defended pass, and in 1644 a Ming general opened the gates of Shanhai Pass himself.

The size of the disagreement shows up in the length. [National Geographic Education](https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/great-wall-china/) says that depending on how the wall is measured, it stretches "somewhere between 4,000 and 5,500 kilometers (2,500 and 3,400 miles)". A survey by China's State Administration of Cultural Heritage, [reported in 2012](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-china-18337039), put it at **21,196.18 km** and counted 43,721 heritage sites along the way.

The smaller range takes in the length that, [the BBC reported in 2009](https://news.bbc.co.uk/2/hi/asia-pacific/8008108.stm), was "commonly put at about 5,000km" before the surveys, when estimates were "mainly based on historical records". The larger figure [adds up every surviving wall and trench](http://www.ncha.gov.cn/art/2016/11/30/art_722_135294.html) built from the Spring and Autumn period to the Ming.

## Three separate walls, built over two thousand years

Wall-building starts long before there is one empire to defend. [UNESCO's World Heritage listing](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/438/) dates Chinese defence works on the northern frontier to the Chunqiu, or Spring and Autumn, period. The same heritage agency, in [its report on the protection of the wall](http://www.ncha.gov.cn/art/2016/11/30/art_722_135294.html) (in Chinese), describes the surviving walls of that period and the Warring States era that followed as built mostly of rammed earth or earth and stone.

After Qin unified China in 221 BC, [the walls that had divided one warring state from another came down](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Great_Wall_of_China), because they threatened the unity of the new empire, while the frontier walls were being linked. UNESCO dates that linking to around 220 BC, when [sections of earlier fortifications were joined together](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/438/) into a single defence system against invasions from the north.

Those Qin walls were mostly [rammed earth, raised by forced labour, and by 212 BC they ran from Gansu to the coast of southern Manchuria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Great_Wall_of_China), and later dynasties rarely followed Qin's routes.

The wall in the photographs is more than fifteen centuries younger, the work of the [Ming dynasty (1368-1644)](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/438/). [The same heritage report](http://www.ncha.gov.cn/art/2016/11/30/art_722_135294.html) describes the eastern Ming wall as mostly brick-faced stone, brick-faced loess or plain stone, and the western stretches as mostly rammed earth, and [up to **25,000 watchtowers**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Wall_of_China) are estimated to have gone up on the wall.

| What "the Great Wall" can mean | When | Built of |
|---|---|---|
| Walls of the rival states | Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods | rammed earth, earth and stone |
| Qin's connected frontier line | from about 220 BC | mostly rammed earth, forced labour |
| The Ming wall tourists visit | 1368 to 1644 | brick and stone in the east, rammed earth in the west |

## A quarter of the Ming wall is hills and rivers

A two-year government mapping study of the Ming wall, [reported in 2009](https://news.bbc.co.uk/2/hi/asia-pacific/8008108.stm), traced it from Hu Mountain in Liaoning to Jiayu Pass in Gansu and found **8,850 km** (5,500 miles): 6,259 km of wall sections, 359 km of trenches, and **2,232 km of natural defensive barriers such as hills and rivers**.

So about a quarter of the official length is not built wall at all. Those 2,232 km are terrain, counted as defence because a ridge or a river was already doing the job.

## The wall's steady job was border control

Defence was intermittent. Besides relaying warnings by smoke or fire, the wall [was a border control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Wall_of_China), used to control immigration and emigration, levy duties on goods and regulate trade.

A fortified line with gates does not stop movement so much as decide where movement happens: at a pass, under the eyes of a garrison.

## Raiders got through, and a trade deal ended the raids

On **1 September 1449** a Ming field army, often estimated at half a million men though [the real number may have been far smaller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tumu_Crisis), was broken at the Tumu post station in Huailai County. [The Great Wall had not yet been built there](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tumu_Crisis), and the border was guarded only by patrols between fortified cities. The historian Frederic Wakeman records that the emperor himself [was captured by Essen's Oirat Mongols, and that the systematic refortification of the Great Wall began in 1472](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_8nXLwSG2O8AC).

It did not settle the frontier. Wakeman writes that in **1550** Altan Khan ["himself rode beneath the walls of Beijing"](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_8nXLwSG2O8AC). The walls worked where they were strongest: Altan [could not take Xuanfu, behind Weng Wanda's double fortified line, so he circled around it to the lightly defended Gubeikou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Great_Wall_of_China), passed through the defences there and raided the suburbs of Beijing. The Ming rebuilt that sector in stone and mortar, and in 1554 [raiders expecting a repeat of 1550 were surprised by the higher wall and stiff Chinese resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Great_Wall_of_China).

What Altan wanted was a market. He had been [trying to trade horses for Chinese agricultural products and textiles, his envoys were sometimes killed, and his raids of 1529, 1530 and 1542 brought livestock and produce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altan_Khan) but never the finer goods he actually wanted. In **April 1571** the two sides [reached a peace agreement, the Longqing Fenggong](https://ijkh.khistory.org/journal/view.php?number=13), and the Ming invested him as Shunyi prince and granted the trading rights he had spent decades asking for.

> Altan Khan wanted to trade horses for Chinese cloth and grain. For four decades the Ming answered mostly with masonry, and got raids, including the one that reached the suburbs of Beijing in 1550. In 1571 they answered with a market, and he took the title and the trading rights instead.

## The myth is the continuity

Arthur Waldron's The Great Wall of China: From History to Myth came from Cambridge University Press in 1990, and a [Publishers Weekly review](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780521365185) that year named its target: "China's modern rulers have nurtured the popular myth that the Great Wall of China is a single, continuous barrier built in the third century B.C. and surviving to the present." The [same review](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780521365185) says Ming rulers, fearful of invasion by Mongols and other nomads, "chose wall-building over trade or diplomatic relations".

The continuous wall since Qin is a backwards-assembled idea: separate campaigns, dynasties apart, gathered under one name and one founding date long after the last brick.

## Not visible from the moon, and the claim is older than spaceflight

The wall's other durable claim predates any way of testing it. [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/is-chinas-great-wall-visible-from-space/) quotes a **1932** Ripley's Believe It or Not cartoon calling the wall "the mightiest work of man, the only one that would be visible to the human eye from the moon", nearly three decades before anyone reached orbit.

[NASA's caption on an International Space Station photograph of the wall](https://www.nasa.gov/vision/space/workinginspace/great_wall.html) says that "despite myths to the contrary, the wall isn't visible from the moon, and is difficult or impossible to see from Earth orbit without the high-powered lenses used for this photo". Yet [UNESCO's listing](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/438/) still calls the Ming wall "the only work built by human hands on this planet that can be seen from the moon".

Low orbit is a closer call. [National Geographic Education](https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/great-wall-china/) says the wall is hard to discern even there, because it looks a lot like the stone and soil that surround it. Jeffrey Hoffman, a former NASA astronaut, [told Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/is-chinas-great-wall-visible-from-space/) that he never saw it, though the same article says Eugene Cernan and Ed Lu have said they've seen the wall from low orbit. When Yang Liwei, China's first man in space, returned from the Shenzhou 5 mission in 2003 and admitted he had not seen it, [the Ministry of Education moved to revise its elementary school textbooks](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/is-chinas-great-wall-visible-from-space/).

So the honest verdict is a frontier management system that worked best at management: it slowed cavalry, controlled and taxed whoever came through a named gate, and relayed warnings by smoke. Where it was doubled and manned, as at Xuanfu in 1550, it held.

On 27 May 1644 the masonry did exactly what it had been built to do, and it made no difference, because the man holding the gate had decided to open it. Strictly FYI.
