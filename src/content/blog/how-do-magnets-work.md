By the schoolroom rule, a magnet sticks to iron, yet type 304, [the most common grade of stainless steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stainless_steel), is mostly iron and [is essentially non-magnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAE_304_stainless_steel). Its recipe adds [**at least 18 percent chromium and 8 percent nickel**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAE_304_stainless_steel) to the iron, and that is enough to stop a magnet from holding on.

The difference lies in how the atoms are packed. Austenitic grades like 304 [have a face-centered cubic crystal structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stainless_steel), while ferritic stainless steel [owes its magnetism to a body-centered cubic one](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stainless_steel). Iron atoms alone do not make a magnet; their electrons have to line up.

## How do magnets work?

A magnet works because its electrons are tiny magnets, through a quantum property called **spin**, and in iron, cobalt and nickel the unpaired ones line up instead of cancelling. The **exchange interaction** holds neighboring spins parallel in microscopic regions called **domains**, and when most domains point the same way, their fields add up to a north and a south pole.

The poles follow two rules: like poles repel and unlike poles attract, and north and south poles "always occur in pairs," as the OpenStax [College Physics](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/22-1-magnets) textbook puts it. Break a magnet in half and each piece has a north and a south pole of its own. The pole names come from the way a hanging magnet settles, the principle behind [the compass](/blog/how-does-a-compass-work/).

## Classical physics predicts no magnetic materials

Niels Bohr showed in his doctoral dissertation of **1911** that classical physics cannot account for magnetism in materials. The result, [the Bohr-van Leeuwen theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohr%E2%80%93Van_Leeuwen_theorem), was rediscovered by Hendrika Johanna van Leeuwen in her thesis of 1919: apply classical mechanics and statistical mechanics consistently, and "the thermal average of the magnetization is always zero." That makes [magnetism in solids a purely quantum mechanical effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohr%E2%80%93Van_Leeuwen_theorem).

That leaves no everyday picture to fall back on. In 1983, filming the BBC series *Fun to Imagine*, an interviewer asked Richard Feynman about a pair of magnets: "what's going on between these two bits of metal?" Feynman said that comparing magnets to things joined by rubber bands would be cheating, because rubber bands pull back together through "electrical forces," the very thing he would be using them to explain, as [ScienceAlert transcribed the clip](https://www.sciencealert.com/watch-richard-feynman-on-why-he-can-t-tell-you-how-magnets-work).

> "I can't explain that attraction in terms of anything else that's familiar to you." - Richard Feynman, BBC, 1983, as quoted by [ScienceAlert](https://www.sciencealert.com/watch-richard-feynman-on-why-he-can-t-tell-you-how-magnets-work)

## Every electron is a magnet, and most of them cancel

An electron has a magnetic dipole moment: [it "behaves like a tiny magnet, producing a magnetic field"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism), and electron spin is the main source of the effect. Most electrons in an atom sit in pairs with opposite spins, and in each pair [every electron's magnetic moment is cancelled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism) by its partner's.

Only atoms with unpaired spins can have a net magnetic moment, and even then the moments of neighboring atoms usually point in random directions. Ferromagnetism, the strong magnetism that holds a magnet to iron, ["occurs in only a few substances,"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism) and the OpenStax textbook gives [iron, cobalt, nickel, and gadolinium](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/22-2-ferromagnets-and-electromagnets) as examples of materials that show strong magnetic effects.

## The force that lines up the spins is electrical

The spins in iron do not line up because their magnetic fields pull on one another. The exchange interaction does the work, and it [has no classical analogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange_interaction). It rests on [the rule that two electrons with the same spin cannot also be in the same orbital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism): under certain conditions, when the orbitals of unpaired electrons on neighboring atoms overlap, that rule keeps parallel-spin electrons farther apart and lowers the electrostatic energy between them.

In iron, [the exchange force is **about 1,000 times stronger** than the dipole interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism) between the electrons' magnetic moments. Werner Heisenberg and Paul Dirac [discovered exchange interaction effects independently in 1926](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange_interaction).

## A magnet pulls on iron by reorganizing it

An ordinary iron nail is not a magnet, even though the spins inside it line up. Pierre Weiss [suggested in 1906](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_domain) that a ferromagnet is divided into domains, each magnetized in one direction. In an unmagnetized piece, the direction [varies from domain to domain in a more or less random manner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_domain), so their fields cancel.

Bring a magnet near and the walls between domains shift, with [the domains aligned with the field growing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_domain) at the expense of the others. The magnet "causes local magnetization of the material with unlike poles closest," the [OpenStax textbook](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/22-2-ferromagnets-and-electromagnets) explains, so the nail and the magnet attract. When the field is removed, [the domain walls remain pinned in their new orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_domain) and the aligned domains produce a field of their own, so a nail stroked with a magnet becomes a weak magnet itself.

A permanent magnet is a material whose domains stay lined up. An electromagnet gets the same result from a current: Hans Christian Oersted found that [a compass needle was deflected by a current-carrying wire](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/22-2-ferromagnets-and-electromagnets), and a current in a coil of wire makes what the textbook calls [temporarily induced magnets](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/22-2-ferromagnets-and-electromagnets). Wound around iron, the coil does better: combining a ferromagnet with an electromagnet "can produce particularly strong magnetic effects," in the words of the [same chapter](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/22-2-ferromagnets-and-electromagnets).

The link runs the other way too: [magnetic fields exert forces on moving charges](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/22-4-magnetic-field-strength-force-on-a-moving-charge-in-a-magnetic-field), at right angles to their motion. That sideways push steers the electrons behind [the northern lights](/blog/what-causes-the-northern-lights/) into the upper atmosphere.

## Heat scrambles a magnet, and gadolinium gives up at room temperature

Heat shakes the spins out of line. Above a material's **Curie temperature**, [the system can no longer maintain a spontaneous magnetization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism), and for iron, the [OpenStax textbook](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/22-2-ferromagnets-and-electromagnets) gives that point as 1043 K, or **770 C**.

| Material | Curie temperature |
|---|---|
| Cobalt | [1388 K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism) |
| Iron | [1043 K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism) |
| Nickel | [627 K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism) |
| Neodymium magnet alloy | [593 K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism) |
| Gadolinium | [292 K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism) |

Gadolinium sits at the bottom of the list: below its Curie point of [**about 20 C (68 F)**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gadolinium) it is ferromagnetic, and above it, the metal is strongly paramagnetic, attracted to a magnet without holding a field of its own. It also [warms up when it enters a magnetic field and cools down when it leaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gadolinium), which is known as the magnetocaloric effect.

Neodymium magnets, developed independently by General Motors and Sumitomo Special Metals in 1984, are [the strongest type of permanent magnet available commercially](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neodymium_magnet), but the standard grade is rated for use only [up to 80 C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neodymium_magnet). Heating a magnet, or hammering it, [tends to pull the domain walls free from their pinned states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_domain), and the domains drift back toward random directions. Even at room temperature, a British magnet supplier notes, the domains [can slowly become disordered](https://e-magnetsuk.com/how-does-magnetism-work-do-magnets-lose-their-strength/), so a permanent magnet weakens over the years.

A fridge magnet works because of a rule about electrons that classical physics has no room for, and the same rule decides whether a magnet sticks to a stainless steel door. [Most of the stainless steel made](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stainless_steel) is austenitic, iron packed so that its spins do not fall into step. Strictly FYI.
