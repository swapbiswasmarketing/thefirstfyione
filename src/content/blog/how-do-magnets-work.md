By the schoolroom rule, a magnet sticks to iron, yet type 304, [the most common grade of stainless steel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAE_304_stainless_steel), is mostly iron and [essentially non-magnetic](https://bssa.org.uk/bssa_articles/magnetic-properties-of-stainless-steel/). Its classic recipe adds [**18 percent chromium and 8 percent nickel**](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_37.html) to the iron, and that is enough to stop a magnet from holding on.

The difference lies in how the atoms are packed. Austenitic grades like 304 [have a face-centered cubic crystal structure](https://bssa.org.uk/bssa_articles/austenitic-stainless-steels/), while ferritic stainless steels, which are magnetic, [have a body-centered cubic one, the same as pure iron at room temperature](https://bssa.org.uk/bssa_articles/ferritic-stainless-steels/). Iron atoms alone do not make a magnet; their electrons have to line up.

## How do magnets work?

A magnet works because its electrons are tiny magnets, through a quantum property called **spin**, and in iron, cobalt and nickel the unpaired ones line up instead of cancelling. The **exchange interaction** holds neighboring spins parallel in microscopic regions called **domains**, and when most domains point the same way, their fields add up to a north and a south pole.

The poles follow two rules: like poles repel and unlike poles attract, and north and south poles "always occur in pairs," as the OpenStax [College Physics](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/22-1-magnets) textbook puts it. Break a magnet in half and each piece has a north and a south pole of its own.

## Classical physics predicts no magnetic materials

Niels Bohr showed in his [doctoral dissertation of 1911](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1922/bohr/biographical/) that [classical physics cannot account for magnetism in materials](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/vleck-lecture.pdf). Apply classical dynamics and statistical mechanics consistently, the physicist John Van Vleck said in his [1977 Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/vleck-lecture.pdf), and the diamagnetic and paramagnetic effects cancel exactly: "Thus there should be no magnetism at all." [Van Vleck](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/vleck-lecture.pdf) called Bohr's thesis "perhaps the most deflationary publication of all time in physics," and noted that the same result was proved independently in a Leiden dissertation of 1919.

The result is now called [the Bohr-van Leeuwen theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohr%E2%80%93Van_Leeuwen_theorem), after Bohr and the author of that dissertation, Hendrika Johanna van Leeuwen. Richard Feynman's [lectures on physics](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_34.html) state the consequence plainly: "it is not possible to understand the magnetic effects of materials in any honest way from the point of view of classical physics," because such effects "are a completely quantum-mechanical phenomenon."

That leaves no everyday picture to fall back on. In 1983, filming the BBC series *Fun to Imagine*, an interviewer [asked Feynman about a pair of magnets](https://www.sciencealert.com/watch-richard-feynman-on-why-he-can-t-tell-you-how-magnets-work): "what's going on between these two bits of metal?" Feynman said that comparing magnets to things joined by rubber bands would be cheating, because rubber bands pull back together through ["electrical forces,"](https://www.sciencealert.com/watch-richard-feynman-on-why-he-can-t-tell-you-how-magnets-work) the very thing he would be using them to explain.

> "I can't explain that attraction in terms of anything else that's familiar to you." - Richard Feynman, BBC, 1983, as quoted by [ScienceAlert](https://www.sciencealert.com/watch-richard-feynman-on-why-he-can-t-tell-you-how-magnets-work)

## Every electron is a magnet, and most of them cancel

An electron has an intrinsic spin, and with it, [Feynman's lectures explain](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_34.html), "both an angular momentum and a magnetic moment," in a ratio twice the one that orbital motion gives, "for reasons that are purely quantum-mechanical." Yet [in most materials the magnetic moments of the electrons cancel](http://hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/hbase/Solids/magpr.html). In paramagnetic ones, where the cancellation is incomplete, the effect stays weak because [thermal motion keeps knocking the atoms' moments out of order](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_34.html).

In a ferromagnet, a long-range order ["causes the unpaired electron spins to line up parallel with each other,"](http://hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/hbase/Solids/ferro.html) and among the elements, iron, nickel, cobalt and some of the rare earths, such as gadolinium and dysprosium, behave this way.

## The spins line up for a reason that is not magnetic

The spins in iron do not line up because their magnetic fields pull on one another. Treat them as tiny bar magnets and, [Feynman's lectures show](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_36.html), the field at each atom would have to be boosted "by some large factor," something "like one thousand or more," which no magnetic interaction can supply; the lectures call that magnetic theory of ferromagnetism "a dismal failure."

The real cause is the exclusion principle, under which two electrons [cannot occupy exactly the same state](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_37.html): electrons with parallel spins must stay apart, so a nearby pair with parallel spins has much more energy than a pair with opposite spins. The result acts on the spins like a force, the **exchange interaction**, though [Feynman insisted](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_37.html) it is "not a magnetic force or any other kind of actual force, but only an apparent force."

Usually it makes neighboring spins point opposite ways, which [Feynman called](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_37.html) "the explanation of the lack of magnetism in almost all substances," while in iron it does "just the reverse of what we should expect." In a paper of 1928, Werner Heisenberg showed that the mysterious internal field Pierre Weiss had proposed two decades earlier to explain ferromagnetism "arose from exchange effects connecting the different magnetic atoms," [Van Vleck recalled](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/vleck-lecture.pdf).

## A magnet pulls on iron by reorganizing it

An ordinary iron nail is not a magnet, even though the spins inside it line up. Weiss [suggested in 1906](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_domain) that a ferromagnet is divided into domains, each magnetized in one direction. In an unmagnetized piece, ["the many domains will themselves be randomly oriented with respect to one another,"](http://hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/hbase/Solids/ferro.html) so their fields cancel.

Bring a magnet near and the domains already pointing along its field ["grow at the expense of their neighbors."](http://hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/hbase/Solids/ferro.html) The magnet ["causes local magnetization of the material with unlike poles closest,"](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/22-2-ferromagnets-and-electromagnets) so the nail and the magnet attract. When the field is removed, a ferromagnet ["will not relax back to zero magnetization"](http://hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/hbase/Solids/hyst.html), because it takes energy to turn reoriented domains back, so a nail stroked with a magnet becomes a weak magnet itself.

A permanent magnet is a material whose domains stay lined up. An electromagnet gets the same result from a current: Hans Christian Oersted [saw a compass needle swing beside a wire carrying a current](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/22-2-ferromagnets-and-electromagnets), and a current in a coil of wire makes a temporary magnet. Wound around iron, the coil does better: combining a ferromagnet with an electromagnet ["can produce particularly strong magnetic effects."](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/22-2-ferromagnets-and-electromagnets)

## Heat scrambles a magnet, and gadolinium gives up at room temperature

Heat shakes the spins out of line. Above a material's **Curie temperature** it can no longer be magnetized, and for iron, the [OpenStax textbook](https://openstax.org/books/college-physics-2e/pages/22-2-ferromagnets-and-electromagnets) gives that point as 1043 K, or **770 C**.

| Material | [Curie temperature](http://hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/hbase/Tables/curie.html) |
|---|---|
| Cobalt | 1388 K |
| Iron | 1043 K |
| Nickel | 627 K |
| Gadolinium | 293 K |

Gadolinium sits at the bottom of the list: its Curie point of [**293 K**](http://hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/hbase/Tables/curie.html) is about 20 C (68 F), so a lump of it can be ferromagnetic on a cool day and lose that on a warm one. It also warms up when it enters a magnetic field and cools down when it leaves, the magnetocaloric effect, and [a 2025 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12418802/) says gadolinium "is widely considered a benchmark for magnetic refrigeration near room temperature."

Neodymium magnets, announced independently by Sumitomo and General Motors [at the same conference in Pittsburgh in 1983](https://spectrum.ieee.org/the-men-who-made-the-magnet-that-made-the-modern-world), are [the strongest type of permanent magnet available commercially](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neodymium_magnet). Unlike most magnets, they [will not lose their magnetization if dropped](https://www.kjmagnetics.com/neodymium-magnet-info.asp), but they are sensitive to heat: standard N grades [begin to lose strength above 80 C](https://www.kjmagnetics.com/neodymium-magnet-info.asp) and lose their magnetization completely above a Curie temperature of 310 C, according to the supplier K&J Magnetics.

In other magnets, a hard knock can [leave the domains misaligned](https://e-magnetsuk.com/how-does-magnetism-work-do-magnets-lose-their-strength/), and even at room temperature the domains [can slowly become disordered](https://e-magnetsuk.com/how-does-magnetism-work-do-magnets-lose-their-strength/), so a permanent magnet weakens over the years.

A fridge magnet works because of a rule about electrons that classical physics has no room for, and the same rule decides whether a magnet sticks to a stainless steel door. [Most of the stainless steel made](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stainless_steel) is austenitic, iron packed so that its spins do not fall into step. Strictly FYI.
