It is always the same clip. A cat is eating, head down in its bowl. Someone slides a cucumber onto the floor behind it, steps back, and films. The cat turns, finds a long green object in a place that was empty ten seconds earlier, and leaves the ground. The caption explains that the cat took the cucumber for a snake, because cats carry an inherited fear of snakes.

The leap is real and well documented. The inherited fear of snakes behind it has never been shown in a domestic cat by anyone.

## Why are cats afraid of cucumbers?

Cats show a startle response, not a cucumber phobia. The prank happens to be built to produce one:

- **The cat is eating.** Attention is committed to the bowl, and a feeding cat is about as unguarded as it gets.
- **The object arrives behind it.** That is ground the cat had already checked and filed as clear.
- **It arrives in silence.** No sound explains the change, so the next glance finds an effect with no cause.
- **It is unfamiliar.** Size and novelty do the work, and the cucumber is simply the prop the internet settled on.

## The snake evidence was recorded in monkeys

There is real science underneath the snake claim, and it is not about cats. In December 2014, Quan Van Le, Lynne Isbell and colleagues published pulvinar recordings from [two adult Japanese macaques](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0114258) in PLOS ONE, work done at the University of Toyama. Of **821 neurons** sampled, 115 responded to the images, and the ones that answered snakes did so at latencies near **55 milliseconds**. The timing did not separate danger from safety. Those neurons fired at a snake in threat display at 55.3 ms and at a snake in a harmless posture at 56.2 ms, a gap the authors report as not significant.

The authors say plainly what that supports. Their results are "consistent with the Snake Detection Theory, which posits that snakes were instrumental in the evolution of primate visual systems." A domestic cat is a carnivore rather than a primate, and a PubMed search for pulvinar snake responses in cats returns zero records. Applied to a cat, the snake story is unverified: the one measurement it leans on was taken from a different order of mammal.

Fast, error-prone visual guessing is a real feature of human vision, which is [why wall sockets and car grilles keep looking like faces](/blog/why-your-brain-sees-faces/). Whether a cat does anything similar with a vegetable is a question nobody has put to a cat.

## Cats hunt snakes

Pamela Perry, the behavior expert at Cornell's Feline Health Center, was asked about the videos and did not hedge. The cats may be reacting to the sudden appearance of a novel object, she said, and then added the line the pet-care pages tend to leave out: ["Cats don't have a natural fear of snakes. In fact, a lot of them hunt snakes."](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/about-center/fhc-updates/cats-and-cucumbers-our-behavior-expert-talks-about-why-cats-are-freaking-out)

Feeding records point the same way. A December 2023 synthesis in Nature Communications by Christopher Lepczyk and colleagues pooled [533 studies of what free-ranging cats actually eat](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10716121/) and counted **2,084 species** on the menu: 981 birds, **463 reptiles**, 431 mammals, 119 insects and 57 amphibians. Cats, the paper concludes, are "extreme generalist predators."

> The pulvinar recordings everyone cites come from two Japanese macaques, and the theory they support describes how primate vision evolved. The animal in the video is a predator with 463 reptile species on its recorded menu.

## Nobody has run the experiment

Search PubMed, the US National Library of Medicine's index of biomedical research, for cucumbers and cat behavior and 17 papers come back: cucumber seedlings under salt stress, melon ripening, sea cucumbers in aquaculture, a fungicide tested on zebrafish larvae. Not one involves a cat.

So the entire evidence base is footage. Cornell's own write-up cites no study, and none turns up in the index either. A highlight reel is a sample selected for drama: the cats that glanced at the cucumber and kept chewing were never uploaded. The internet keeps a small permanent collection of animal spectacles and supplies each one with a confident reason afterwards, [the exploding beached whale included](/blog/why-do-whales-explode/).

| Explanation | What it rests on | Where it stands |
|---|---|---|
| The cat sees a snake | Neuron recordings in two macaques | No feline equivalent published |
| Cats innately fear snakes | Repetition | Rejected by Perry; 463 reptile species in the diet record |
| Startle at a silent novel object | Perry's reading of the clips, plus Goldman's flight-then-reassess description | Fits the footage, still untested |

## The prank costs the cat something

What happens after the camera stops is the part Cornell wants people to hear. A frightened cat runs a physiological stress response, and Perry warns that a timid one attaches the fear to whatever else was present: "Cats who are timid or fearful may associate other stimuli with the frightening event, such as the food dish, the location, etc." The cat can end up wary of the bowl, of the kitchen, or of the person who set it up. Cornell puts its position in a single line: "we recommend abstaining from this trend."

When National Geographic [covered the trend on 17 November 2015](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/151117-cats-cucumbers-videos-behavior), John Bradshaw of the University of Bristol called the videos "despicable" and "an incitement for people to scare their cats and then invite people to laugh at them." Certified animal behaviorist Jill Goldman was blunter with the same reporter. "If you cause stress to an animal that's probably not a good thing," she said. "If you do it for laughs it makes me question your humanity." Goldman also described the leap itself: a startled cat "will often try to get out of there as quickly as possible and then reassess from a distance."

The honest answer is duller than the snake and more useful. A cat at its bowl is the least prepared it will be all day, and a cucumber is a change that arrives without a sound. Everything bolted on afterwards, the ancestral memory and the reptile in the vegetable, is a caption written for a video, and its citation trail ends with two monkeys in a Japanese laboratory. A tidy reason sticks to a striking sight and outlives the checking, which is worth holding onto the next time someone explains [why a city would decide to keep its dead in tunnels](/blog/why-were-the-catacombs-built/).
