It is always the same clip. A cat is eating, head down in its bowl. Someone slides a cucumber onto the floor behind it, steps back, and films. The cat turns, finds a long green object in a place that was empty ten seconds earlier, and leaves the ground. The caption explains that the cat took the cucumber for a snake, because cats carry an inherited fear of snakes.

The leap is real and well documented. The inherited fear of snakes behind it is much harder to stand up.

## Why are cats afraid of cucumbers?

Cats show a startle response, not a cucumber phobia. The prank happens to be built to produce one:

- **The cat is eating.** The cucumber often goes down by the food bowl, and [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/151117-cats-cucumbers-videos-behavior), paraphrasing Pam Johnson-Bennett, author of Think Like a Cat, reported that cats "often associate those areas with safety and security."
- **The object arrives behind it.** Cornell's behavior specialist offers it as a possibility in [her reading of the videos](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/about-center/fhc-updates/cats-and-cucumbers-our-behavior-expert-talks-about-why-cats-are-freaking-out): "It might be the fearful ones who are most likely to react to the appearance of a strange object behind them that was not there a minute earlier."
- **It arrives suddenly.** The cucumber is set down while the cat's head is in the bowl, so the next glance finds something that was not there before.
- **It is out of place.** Jill Goldman, a certified animal behaviorist, told [the same magazine](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/151117-cats-cucumbers-videos-behavior) that the cucumbers trigger the cats' natural startle responses, since they "would not normally see cucumbers on the floor."

## The snake evidence was recorded in monkeys

Of the experts National Geographic quoted, Goldman alone allowed that the cats may associate the cucumber with snakes, which can be deadly predators. There is real science on snake detection, and it is not about cats.

In [a 2013 paper in PNAS](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24167268/), Quan Van Le of the University of Toyama, Lynne Isbell of UC Davis and colleagues recorded neurons in the pulvinar of macaques shown snakes, monkey faces, monkey hands and simple shapes, and snakes "elicited the strongest, fastest responses." The two monkeys were raised in an enclosure on Amami Island, and the authors believe they had ["no chance to encounter snakes before the experiment."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3839741/)

The anatomy is the part that matters for cats. The snake-sensitive neurons sat in parts of the pulvinar that the paper calls distinctive in primates, ["with no homologous structures found in the visual systems of nonprimate mammals."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3839741/) A domestic cat is a nonprimate mammal.

A December 2014 follow-up from the same group, again in [two adult Japanese macaques](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0114258) and published in PLOS ONE, asked whether those neurons could tell a snake poised to strike from one in a less threatening posture. Every picture showed a live venomous viper, three with the mouth open in threat display and three with it closed. Of **821 neurons** recorded, 115 responded to the images, and of the 78 tested with every picture, 45 fired harder at the open-mouthed threat displays. Speed did not separate the two: mean latencies were **55.3 ms** for the threat displays and 56.2 ms for the closed-mouth, non-threat postures, a gap the authors report as not significant.

The authors say plainly what their work supports: results ["consistent with the Snake Detection Theory, which posits that snakes were instrumental in the evolution of primate visual systems."](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0114258) Applied to a cat, the snake story is unverified, because both sets of recordings come from a different order of mammal.

Whether a cucumber fools a cat's eyes is a question the clips cannot answer, but illusions have been put to cats. In [a 2021 citizen-science study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applanim.2021.105338), owners printed shapes, laid them on the floor in pairs and filmed what their cats did: a square outline, a Kanizsa square whose edges are only implied by its corners, and a control. The study reached over 500 cats and 30 finished all six daily trials. Of those, nine sat inside a shape, and they chose the illusory square just as often as the real one and more often than the control. The authors flag the usual weakness of citizen science, owners dropping out, and call for the test to be repeated in controlled settings.

## Cats hunt snakes

Pamela Perry, the behavior expert at Cornell's Feline Health Center, disagrees with the snake theory. The cats may be reacting to the sudden appearance of a novel object, she said, and then added: ["Cats don't have a natural fear of snakes. In fact, a lot of them hunt snakes."](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/about-center/fhc-updates/cats-and-cucumbers-our-behavior-expert-talks-about-why-cats-are-freaking-out)

Stomach contents show that cats will eat even venomous snakes. In southwest Western Australia, six of **358 feral and stray cats** whose stomach contents were analyzed had eaten venomous snakes, the youngest of them only 10 months old, according to [a 2020 Murdoch University study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7222765/). The authors' verdict: "Cats are therefore clearly a threat to even venomous snakes."

The global record points the same way. A December 2023 synthesis in Nature Communications by Christopher Lepczyk and colleagues pooled [533 studies of what free-ranging cats actually eat](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10716121/) and counted **2,084 species** on the menu: 981 birds, **463 reptiles**, 431 mammals, 119 insects and 57 amphibians. The reptile count lumps lizards in with snakes, but the conclusion is broad: cats are "extreme generalist predators."

> The snake-sensitive neurons were recorded in two Japanese macaques, in a part of the brain the 2013 paper says exists only in primates. The animal in the video belongs to a species that eats venomous snakes.

## Neither write-up cites an experiment

Neither Cornell's write-up nor National Geographic's cites a study of cats and cucumbers, so the evidence behind both is footage. A highlight reel is a sample selected for drama. National Geographic noted that some cats ["couldn't care less about the vegetable intrusion,"](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/151117-cats-cucumbers-videos-behavior) while the clips that went viral, as Cornell describes them, show cats leaping high into the air. A cat that ignores the cucumber gives the camera nothing to share.

## The prank costs the cat something

What happens after the camera stops is the part Cornell wants people to hear. Frightened cats go through physiological and behavioral stress responses, and [Cornell's write-up](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/about-center/fhc-updates/cats-and-cucumbers-our-behavior-expert-talks-about-why-cats-are-freaking-out), crediting Perry, warns that a timid one may attach the fear to other things present: "Cats who are timid or fearful may associate other stimuli with the frightening event, such as the food dish, the location, etc." The cat can also come to associate its owner with the prank. On whether the prank can have lasting effects, the write-up answers "Put simply, yes," and its advice is short: "we recommend abstaining from this trend."

When National Geographic [covered the trend on 17 November 2015](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/151117-cats-cucumbers-videos-behavior), John Bradshaw of the University of Bristol called the videos "despicable" and "an incitement for people to scare their cats and then invite people to laugh at them." "If you cause stress to an animal that's probably not a good thing," Goldman told the same reporter. "If you do it for laughs it makes me question your humanity." A startled cat, she said, "will often try to get out of there as quickly as possible and then reassess from a distance."

The honest answer is duller than the snake and more useful. A cat at its bowl has its head down in a place it treats as safe, and a cucumber is a change that arrives without warning. Everything bolted on afterwards, the ancestral memory and the reptile in the vegetable, is a caption written for a video, and the nearest real science was done on two monkeys in a Japanese laboratory. A tidy reason sticks to a striking sight and outlives the checking. Strictly FYI.
