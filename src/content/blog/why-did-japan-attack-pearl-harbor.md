[Executive Order 8832](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-8832-amendment-executive-order-no-8389-april-10-1940-amended), the order that cut Japan off from American oil, is one sentence long. Signed by Franklin Roosevelt on **July 26, 1941**, it amends an earlier order "by changing the period at the end of subdivision (j) of Section 3 of such Order to a semicolon and adding the following new subdivision thereafter: (k) June 14, 1941 - China, and Japan."

The word oil appears nowhere in it. A [White House statement](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/statement-the-executive-order-freezing-japanese-and-chinese-assets-the-united-states) explained that the new line put all Japanese "financial and import and export trade transactions" under government control.

Louis Morton's [*Strategy and Command*](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USA/USA-P-Strategy/Strategy-4.html), a volume of the US Army's official history of the Pacific war, calls it an order "freezing Japanese assets in the United States and establishing a de facto oil embargo." Four months later, Japanese carrier planes attacked Pearl Harbor.

## Why did Japan attack Pearl Harbor?

Japan attacked Pearl Harbor to knock out the US Pacific Fleet while its armies seized Southeast Asia, above all British Malaya and the oil-rich Netherlands Indies. The American freeze of **July 1941** had cut off Japan's oil, and its leaders chose to take oil by force rather than meet the US price for a settlement. Pro-Chinese feeling in the United States, the [State Department's historians](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1937-1945/pearl-harbor) write, made it difficult to reach any deal that would not "involve a Japanese withdrawal from China," a condition Japan's military leaders would not accept.

The war plan's immediate objective, [Morton writes](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USA/USA-P-Strategy/Strategy-4.html), was "the capture of the rich Dutch and British possessions in southeast Asia." Securing them, Japan's planners believed, meant knocking out the fleet at Pearl Harbor and the American base in the Philippines. The army could give the south only **11 of its 51 divisions**, with a large force left in Manchuria and an even larger one in China.

It was to be a [limited war](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USA/USA-P-Strategy/Strategy-5.html): take the south, then fortify it until the Allies "would prefer a settlement to the long and costly war" needed to break through.

> "Perhaps the major error of the Japanese was their decision to attack the United States when the main objective of the war was to gain the strategic resources of Southeast Asia." - Louis Morton, *Strategy and Command*

## A freeze on dollars became an oil embargo

Japan had been [at war in China](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USA/USA-P-Strategy/Strategy-2.html) since **July 1937**. When its troops moved into southern French Indochina in **July 1941**, gaining bases within reach of Singapore, the Philippines and the Netherlands Indies, Roosevelt answered with [the freeze](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USA/USA-P-Strategy/Strategy-4.html). Britain and the Netherlands backed it, and since Japan no longer had the dollars to buy the materials of war, its effect, in Morton's phrase, "was to create an economic blockade of Japan."

[Japan could not fight on its own oil](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USA/USA-P-Strategy/Strategy-2.html). In 1940 it produced **3,163,000 barrels**, "less than 12 percent of the nation's peacetime requirements." Its 1941 reserves of **43 million barrels** would last two years of war at best, and only if topped up from resources within the empire.

On **August 1**, a [White House announcement](https://archive.org/details/sim_department-of-state-bulletin_1941-08-02_5_110) barred exports of "motor fuels and oils suitable for use in aircraft" to anywhere but the Western Hemisphere, the British Empire and the unoccupied territories of countries resisting aggression, and held other oil exports to "usual or pre-war quantities." It never named Japan.

On **November 29**, the former prime minister Fumimaro Konoe asked why Japan could not carry on "with broken economic relations but without war." Prime Minister Hideki Tojo [replied](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USA/USA-P-Strategy/Strategy-5.html) that the final consequence would be "gradual impoverishment."

## Japanese troops reached Malaya before the planes reached Hawaii

Pearl Harbor was one blow in an opening phase [synchronized](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USA/USA-P-Strategy/Strategy-6.html) "to obtain the maximum advantage of surprise." Morton writes: "Almost simultaneously the Japanese attacked Hawaii, the Philippines, Wake, Guam, Singapore, Hong Kong, Malaya and Thailand."

The battle for [Kota Bharu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Kota_Bharu), in British Malaya, began shortly after midnight local time on December 8, **more than an hour before** the attack on Pearl Harbor started. Southeast Asia, where the resources were, got the offensive's [main strength](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USA/USA-P-Strategy/Strategy-6.html): three armies, with supporting air and naval forces.

## The admiral who planned it had no confidence in a long war

Isoroku Yamamoto, commander of the Combined Fleet, opposed this war. In 1940, after Japan joined the Tripartite Pact, he told Konoe, in words an [NSA history](https://archive.org/details/1993-priceless-advantage-parker-nsa) quotes from Konoe's memoirs: "If I am told to fight regardless of the consequences, I shall run wild considerably for the first six months or a year, but I have utterly no confidence for the second and third years."

The plan was still his. In a [letter of **January 7, 1941**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prelude_to_the_attack_on_Pearl_Harbor), he outlined it to the navy minister, Koshiro Oikawa, arguing that the navy should try, in the words a [National Park Service study](https://archive.org/details/submergedcultura01ussa) quotes, "to decide the fate of the war on the very first day." He threatened to resign to get it adopted, arguing, in [Morton's](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USA/USA-P-Strategy/Strategy-4.html) words, that "the success of the southward drive depended on the destruction of the American fleet."

## The carriers sailed before the Hull note arrived

Tokyo had already set its own deadline. An [Imperial Conference](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USA/USA-P-Strategy/Strategy-5.html) on **November 5** confirmed, in substance, the decision of September 6: keep negotiating with Washington and, if no settlement came, open hostilities, with a deadline of **November 25** that was later pushed to the 29th. Of the two offers it approved, Proposal A promised a withdrawal from China and French Indochina only once a peace treaty with Chiang Kai-shek was signed, and even then troops would stay in parts of China for a "suitable period," informally estimated at about **twenty-five years**.

On **November 26**, Secretary of State Cordell Hull handed Japan's envoys a [proposal](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1931-41v02/d409), marked "Tentative and Without Commitment," that Japan "withdraw all military, naval, air and police forces from China and from Indochina," with the asset freezes lifted on both sides.

The strike force was already at sea. Its six carriers had left [Tankan Bay](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USA/USA-P-Strategy/Strategy-6.html) in the Kurils at 6:00 a.m. on November 26, Tokyo time, by the [National Park Service study](https://archive.org/details/submergedcultura01ussa)'s account. In Washington it was still the afternoon of November 25. Their [orders](https://www.ibiblio.org/pha/myths/jm-097.html) were to "advance to the standby point" in the North Pacific by the evening of December 3.

A Liaison Conference in Tokyo [rejected](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USA/USA-P-Strategy/Strategy-5.html) Hull's note the next day. Even so, a [naval directive of December 2](https://www.ibiblio.org/pha/myths/jm-097.html) provided that if the talks seemed certain to "reach an amicable settlement" before hostilities began, all elements of the Combined Fleet were to be "assembled and returned to their bases."

## The sleeping giant line, which nobody can trace to Yamamoto before 1970

In the 1970 film *Tora! Tora! Tora!*, Yamamoto's character says, "I fear all we have done is to awaken a sleeping giant and fill him with a terrible resolve." William Safire's [*Political Dictionary*](https://archive.org/details/safirespolitical0000safi), citing the Library of Congress collection *Respectfully Quoted*, traces the line to that film and adds: "The attributed remark, however, has no printed evidence to support Yamamoto's use."

Yet a [2006 commentary](https://web.archive.org/web/20150219102245/http://www.pacaf.af.mil/news/story.asp?id=123034638) by the commander of Pacific Air Forces, General Paul Hester, still said Yamamoto "spoke the now famous words."

In a real letter of **January 9, 1942**, to the journalist Taketora Ogata, quoted in Hiroyuki Agawa's [*The Reluctant Admiral*](https://archive.org/details/reluctantadmiral00agaw), Yamamoto wrote that a military man "can scarcely pride himself on having 'smitten a sleeping enemy'," and that the enemy, "angered and outraged," would "soon launch a determined counterattack."

## The attack missed the carriers and the oil tanks

In under two hours the raid sank or put out of action **8 battleships**, 3 light cruisers and 3 destroyers, and killed **2,403** Americans, by [Morton's count](https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USA/USA-P-Strategy/Strategy-6.html). But "the richest prize, the three carriers and their escort," was not in port, and the attackers overlooked "the repair shops, the dry dock, and the oil tanks then filled to capacity." That afternoon, Morton writes, Roosevelt's War Council agreed that the attack "had mobilized the nation as nothing else could have."

Japan's [declaration of war](https://archive.org/details/tokyo-gazette-5.7-1942.1), dated December 8, blamed America and Britain for "a direct severance of economic relations, menacing gravely the existence of Our Empire." It was issued in the name of an emperor "seated on the Throne of a line unbroken for ages eternal."

It never mentions Pearl Harbor, and the order that cut off Japan's oil never mentions oil. Strictly FYI.
