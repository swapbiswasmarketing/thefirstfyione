A man named Thomas Running gets the internet's credit for inventing running, which he is supposed to have managed by trying to walk twice at the same time. The joke has traveled far enough that a foot-care company's July 2025 [history of running](https://thefeats.com/blogs/source/when-was-running-invented) opens by denying it: "No, running was NOT invented by Thomas Running in 1748 despite the online meme myth."

[Know Your Meme](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/twice-at-the-same-time) traces the joke to a tweet of April 10, 2017, which gave it a different year: "Running was invented in 1612 by Thomas Running when he tried to walk twice at the same time." A Reddit comment in March 2018 [moved the date to 1837](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/twice-at-the-same-time), and the template spread to other inventions, among them lying, invented in 1912 "by John Lie who tried to sit twice at the same time." Know Your Meme describes the meme as ["a series of fake historical facts."](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/twice-at-the-same-time) Even a game with real, named inventors can carry more than one birth year, as [pickleball does](/blog/when-was-pickleball-invented/).

## When was running invented?

Running was never invented: legged animals ran long before there were people, and Thomas Running is a character in a joke. The question scientists argue over is when the human body became adapted for running long distances. One influential answer, still disputed, is [**about 2 million years ago**, in the early species of our own genus](https://carlzimmer.com/faster-than-a-hyena-470/), *Homo*.

The case rests on stamina rather than speed. "We can't beat most animals in a sprint, but we best many of them in endurance running," the Harvard anthropologist Daniel Lieberman told the [*Harvard Gazette*](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2004/11/running-paced-human-evolution/) in 2004. "Most mammals can't sustain a gallop over 10 to 15 minutes," he [told *Science*](https://carlzimmer.com/faster-than-a-hyena-470/), while people can keep running for hours.

| When | What happened |
|---|---|
| About 2 million years ago | [Running traits appear in early *Homo*](https://carlzimmer.com/faster-than-a-hyena-470/), by Bramble and Lieberman's reading of the fossils |
| About 2094 to 2046 BC | [Shulgi rules Ur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shulgi) and claims, in a hymn, a run from Nippur to Ur and back in one day |
| 776 BC | The traditional starting date of the Olympic Games, whose [only event was a sprint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stadion_(running_race)) until 724 BC |

## The evidence is in the neck, the tendons and the rear

"It all started with pigs on a treadmill," the [*Gazette*](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2004/11/running-paced-human-evolution/) reported. Lieberman was testing pigs when Dennis Bramble, a University of Utah biologist, showed him why, in Lieberman's words, "pigs are terrible runners": they lack a nuchal ligament, the elastic band from the base of the skull to the spine that [keeps an animal's head steady when it runs](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2004/11/running-paced-human-evolution/). [Horses, dogs and cheetahs have one](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2004/11/running-paced-human-evolution/), as do people, but "Apes don't have it," Bramble [told *Science*](https://carlzimmer.com/faster-than-a-hyena-470/).

Bramble and Lieberman set out the case in a paper in [*Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature03052), listing [**26 traits**](https://web.archive.org/web/20200918025735/https://www.sfgate.com/news/article/Unlike-apes-humans-were-born-to-run-study-says-2671133.php) of the human body that suit running. Tendons in the legs, the long Achilles among them, work as springs that [store **about half of the energy** of each stride](https://carlzimmer.com/faster-than-a-hyena-470/) and release it in the next; "Chimps don't have these springs," Lieberman said.

Most researchers had [credited the tall, long-legged build of early *Homo* to efficient walking](https://carlzimmer.com/faster-than-a-hyena-470/), "the standard story you'll get in most textbooks," Lieberman said. Bramble went further, [telling *Science*](https://carlzimmer.com/faster-than-a-hyena-470/) that endurance "running is the only known behavior that would account for the different body plans in *Homo* as opposed to apes or australopithecines."

The gluteus maximus, the muscle behind the large human rear, [stays mostly quiet in walking but works far harder in running](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.02255), where a 2006 study by Lieberman, Bramble and colleagues found that one of its main jobs is to keep the trunk from pitching forward. Hairlessness and a high capacity for cooling by sweat have also been [suggested as results of selection for endurance running](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4736035/). As with [wisdom teeth](/blog/why-we-have-wisdom-teeth/), the evidence is the body itself, read as a record of how distant ancestors lived.

> "Today, [endurance running] is primarily a form of exercise and recreation, but its roots may be as ancient as the origin of the human genus." - Dennis Bramble and Daniel Lieberman, [*Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature03052)

## Critics called the evidence trivial or incorrect

In the [*San Francisco Chronicle*](https://web.archive.org/web/20200918025735/https://www.sfgate.com/news/article/Unlike-apes-humans-were-born-to-run-study-says-2671133.php) on the day the paper came out, C. Owen Lovejoy of Kent State University went through its evidence point by point and dismissed the traits as either "trivial" or "incorrect." "How can one even conceive of evolving a 'walking' strategy that was entirely decoupled from a 'running' strategy?" he [asked](https://web.archive.org/web/20200918025735/https://www.sfgate.com/news/article/Unlike-apes-humans-were-born-to-run-study-says-2671133.php).

Tim White of the University of California, Berkeley, said the pair offered "no new ideas" and had "abused" the fossil data, the [*Chronicle*](https://web.archive.org/web/20200918025735/https://www.sfgate.com/news/article/Unlike-apes-humans-were-born-to-run-study-says-2671133.php) added. His Berkeley colleague F. Clark Howell [called the report](https://web.archive.org/web/20200918025735/https://www.sfgate.com/news/article/Unlike-apes-humans-were-born-to-run-study-says-2671133.php) "very important and very provocative."

David Carrier of the University of Utah, whose earlier research Bramble and Lieberman [cited for the idea that endurance running evolved in human ancestors](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2004/11/041123163757.htm), later tested part of the case himself. In 2015 he and two colleagues recorded **13** back and leg muscles during eight tasks, from walking and steady running to sprints, jumps and punches. In every muscle, [jumping or punching drew the highest median activity](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4736035/), and the muscles' size, they concluded, seemed "more related to the demands of explosive behaviors rather than those of high speed sprinting or sustained endurance running."

Bramble [admitted to *Science*](https://carlzimmer.com/faster-than-a-hyena-470/) in 2004 that the "sketchiest part" of the hypothesis was why early humans ran long distances. A 2024 study in [*Nature Human Behaviour*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-01876-x) offered one answer, persistence hunting: it gathered **nearly 400** accounts of hunters running down game from **272** locations around the world and argued that such hunts can pay off about as well as other pre-modern hunting methods in the right conditions.

## A Sumerian king bragged about his running long before Olympia

Shulgi, who ruled the Sumerian city of Ur from about 2094 to 2046 BC, claimed in a hymn to have run from Nippur to Ur, [**about 100 miles** away](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shulgi), and back in one day. In an [Oxford translation](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section2/tr24201.htm) of the hymn, "the fast runner" sets off to prove his speed, races back to Nippur through a hailstorm and celebrates a festival in both cities on the same day. "Truly I am not boasting!" he [adds](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section2/tr24201.htm).

The scholar Samuel Noah Kramer [titled a chapter on him](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shulgi) "The First Long-Distance Champion" in the 1981 edition of *History Begins at Sumer*, a book of "thirty-nine firsts in recorded history." Shulgi's seventh year on the throne was even named for the feat, as the year ["the king made a round trip between Ur and Nippur."](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shulgi)

The ancient Olympic Games are traditionally dated to **776 BC**, and until 724 BC their [only event was the stadion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stadion_(running_race)), a sprint of about 200 yards. "There was first the foot-race, and Coroebus an Elean was victor," the Greek travel writer Pausanias wrote in his [account of Olympia](https://www.theoi.com/Text/Pausanias5A.html), and a long race was added at the 15th Festival. Olympia's opening event was the kind of running at which people lose to most animals, and the [marathon](/blog/why-is-a-marathon-26-2-miles/) came much later still.

Thomas Running's method treats running as walking done twice at once, and Lieberman answered that idea years before the joke: "Running is not fast walking," he [told *Science*](https://carlzimmer.com/faster-than-a-hyena-470/) in 2004. "You do not use the same mechanics." Nobody invented running, but if Bramble and Lieberman are right, running helped make the people who joke about its invention. Strictly FYI.
