A man named Thomas Running gets the internet's credit for inventing running, which he is supposed to have managed by trying to walk twice at the same time. The joke has traveled far enough that a foot-care company's July 2025 [history of running](https://thefeats.com/blogs/source/when-was-running-invented) opens by denying it: "No, running was NOT invented by Thomas Running in 1748 despite the online meme myth."

[Know Your Meme](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/twice-at-the-same-time) traces the joke to a tweet of April 10, 2017, which gave it a different year: "Running was invented in 1612 by Thomas Running when he tried to walk twice at the same time." A Reddit comment in March 2018 [moved the date to 1837](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/twice-at-the-same-time), and the template spread to other inventions, among them lying, invented in 1912 "by John Lie who tried to sit twice at the same time." Know Your Meme describes the meme as ["a series of fake historical facts."](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/twice-at-the-same-time)

## When was running invented?

Running was never invented: legged animals ran long before there were people, and Thomas Running is a character in a joke. The question scientists argue over is whether, and when, the human body became adapted for running long distances. One influential answer, still disputed, is [**about 2 million years ago**, in early members of our own genus](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature03052), *Homo*.

The case rests on stamina rather than speed. "We can't beat most animals in a sprint, but we best many of them in endurance running," the Harvard anthropologist Daniel Lieberman told the [*Harvard Gazette*](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2004/11/running-paced-human-evolution/) in 2004.

| When | What happened |
|---|---|
| About 2 million years ago | Running traits appear in early *Homo*, by Bramble and Lieberman's reading of the fossils |
| About 2094 to 2046 BC | [Shulgi rules Ur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shulgi) and claims, in a hymn, a run from Nippur to Ur, about 100 miles, and back in one day |
| 776 BC | The traditional starting date of the Olympic Games, whose [only event was a sprint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stadion_(running_race)) until 724 BC |
| April 10, 2017 | [A tweet](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/twice-at-the-same-time) credits Thomas Running with inventing running in 1612 |

## The evidence is in the neck, the tendons and the rear

"It all started with pigs on a treadmill," the [*Gazette*](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2004/11/running-paced-human-evolution/) reported. Lieberman was testing pigs when Dennis Bramble, a University of Utah biologist, showed him why, in Lieberman's words, "pigs are terrible runners": they lack a nuchal ligament, the elastic band from the base of the skull to the spine that [keeps an animal's head steady when it runs](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2004/11/running-paced-human-evolution/). Horses, dogs and cheetahs have one, and the [ridge it leaves on the skull](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2004/11/running-paced-human-evolution/) turns up in fossil skulls of early *Homo* but not in chimpanzees or the earliest prehumans.

Bramble and Lieberman set out the case in a paper in [*Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature03052) that [examined **26 traits** of the human body](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2004/11/041123163757.htm) suited to running. Tendons in the legs, the long Achilles among them, work as springs that [store **about half of the energy** of each stride](https://carlzimmer.com/faster-than-a-hyena-470/) and release it in the next; "Chimps don't have these springs," Lieberman said.

Most researchers had [credited the tall, long-legged build of *Homo ergaster*, an early African species of our genus, to efficient walking](https://carlzimmer.com/faster-than-a-hyena-470/), "the standard story you'll get in most textbooks," Lieberman told *Science*, but Bramble argued that endurance "running is the only known behavior that would account for the different body plans in *Homo* as opposed to apes or australopithecines."

The gluteus maximus, the muscle behind the large human rear, [stays mostly quiet in walking but works far harder in running](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.02255), where a 2006 study by Lieberman, Bramble and colleagues found that one of its main jobs is to keep the trunk from pitching forward. Hairlessness and a high capacity for evaporative cooling have also been [suggested as results of selection for endurance running](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4736035/), an idea David Carrier put forward in 1984.

> "Today, endurance running is primarily a form of exercise and recreation, but its roots may be as ancient as the origin of the human genus, and its demands a major contributing factor to the human body form." - Dennis Bramble and Daniel Lieberman in *Nature*, as quoted by the [University of Utah](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2004/11/041123163757.htm)

## Critics called the evidence trivial or incorrect

In the [*San Francisco Chronicle*](https://web.archive.org/web/20200918025735/https://www.sfgate.com/news/article/Unlike-apes-humans-were-born-to-run-study-says-2671133.php) on the day the paper came out, C. Owen Lovejoy of Kent State University went through its evidence point by point, dismissed the traits as either "trivial" or "incorrect," and asked: "How can one even conceive of evolving a 'walking' strategy that was entirely decoupled from a 'running' strategy?"

Tim White of the University of California, Berkeley, said the pair offered "no new ideas" and had "abused" the fossil data, while his Berkeley colleague F. Clark Howell called the report "very important and very provocative," the [*Chronicle*](https://web.archive.org/web/20200918025735/https://www.sfgate.com/news/article/Unlike-apes-humans-were-born-to-run-study-says-2671133.php) reported.

David Carrier of the University of Utah, whose idea that human ancestors evolved to run long distances in pursuit of animals, long before bows and arrows, Bramble and Lieberman [cited as one possibility](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2004/11/041123163757.htm), later tested part of the case himself. In a 2015 study in [*Biology Open*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4736035/), he and two colleagues recorded **13** back and leg muscles during eight tasks, from walking and steady running to sprints, jumps and punches. In every muscle, [jumping or punching drew the highest median activity](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4736035/), and the muscles' size, they concluded, seemed "more related to the demands of explosive behaviors rather than those of high speed sprinting or sustained endurance running." They wrote that the result is consistent with [selection on aggressive behavior](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4736035/) having played an important role in the evolution of *Homo*.

Bramble [admitted to *Science*](https://carlzimmer.com/faster-than-a-hyena-470/) in 2004 that the "sketchiest part" of the hypothesis was why early humans ran long distances, and Lieberman's best guess then was scavenging: "If you get [to the carcass] before the hyenas and the other hominids, you would have a lot of protein and fat at your disposal." Bramble leaned the same way, telling the [University of Utah](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2004/11/041123163757.htm) that running animals down in the heat is "a lot more work and the payoffs are less reliable."

A 2024 study in [*Nature Human Behaviour*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-024-01876-x) by Eugene Morin and Bruce Winterhalder took up that cost objection and backed Carrier's hunting answer in the form of persistence hunting: it gathered **nearly 400** accounts of hunters running down game from **272** locations around the world and used foraging theory to argue that such hunts can pay off about as well as other pre-modern hunting methods in the right conditions.

## A Sumerian king bragged about his running long before Olympia

Shulgi, king of the Sumerian city of Ur, [claimed in a hymn](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section2/tr24201.htm) to have run from Nippur to Ur and back in one day. In an [Oxford translation](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section2/tr24201.htm) of the hymn, "the fast runner" sets off to prove his speed, races back to Nippur through a hailstorm, covers "a distance of fifteen double-hours" before sunset and celebrates a festival in both cities on the same day. "Truly I am not boasting!" he [adds](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section2/tr24201.htm).

The scholar Samuel Noah Kramer [titled a chapter on him](https://www.pennpress.org/9780812212761/history-begins-at-sumer/) "Shulgi of Ur: The First Long-Distance Champion" in *History Begins at Sumer*, a book subtitled "Thirty-Nine Firsts in Recorded History." Shulgi's seventh year on the throne was even named for the feat, as the year "the king made a round trip between Ur and Nippur," according to the [year-name list](https://web.archive.org/web/20120222033806/http://cdli.ucla.edu/tools/yearnames/HTML/T6K2.htm) of the Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative.

The ancient Olympic Games are traditionally dated to **776 BC**, and until 724 BC their [only event was the stadion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stadion_(running_race)), a sprint of about 200 yards. "There was first the foot-race, and Coroebus an Elean was victor," the Greek travel writer Pausanias wrote in his [account of Olympia](https://www.theoi.com/Text/Pausanias5A.html), adding that the double foot-race came in at the fourteenth Festival and the long course at the next. Olympia's opening event was the kind of running at which people lose to most animals.

Thomas Running's method treats running as walking done twice at once, and Lieberman answered that idea years before the joke: "Running is not fast walking," he [told *Science*](https://carlzimmer.com/faster-than-a-hyena-470/) in 2004. "You do not use the same mechanics." Nobody invented running, but if Bramble and Lieberman are right, running helped make the people who joke about its invention. Strictly FYI.
