You take one enthusiastic bite of ice cream, and a few seconds later a cold dagger stabs you right between the eyes. Nothing touched your skull. The ice cream is sitting on your tongue. Yet your forehead is the part that's yelling.

The answer starts with a mix-up in one of your face's busiest nerves, and ends, for now, in an argument among headache researchers about blood vessels.

## Why do we get brain freeze?

Brain freeze is a short headache set off when something cold passes over the roof of your mouth or the back of your throat, and the brain places the pain in the forehead or temples. The International Headache Society's classification describes it as pain ["induced in susceptible people by passage of cold material (solid, liquid or gaseous) over the palate and/or posterior pharyngeal wall."](https://ichd-3.org/other-primary-headache-disorders/4-5-cold-stimulus-headache/4-5-2-headache-attributed-to-ingestion-or-inhalation-of-a-cold-stimulus/) Nerves in the palate fire, and the ache is felt somewhere else. What makes those nerves fire is still being worked out.

It is also brief. To meet the diagnostic criteria the headache must be gone [within 10 minutes](https://ichd-3.org/other-primary-headache-disorders/4-5-cold-stimulus-headache/4-5-2-headache-attributed-to-ingestion-or-inhalation-of-a-cold-stimulus/) of the cold being removed, and most are far shorter: in one school trial, [17 of the 29 headaches lasted less than 10 seconds](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC139031/).

## The famous long name is not the diagnosis

Health sites like to announce, as [Healthline does](https://www.healthline.com/health/sphenopalatine-ganglioneuralgia-brain-freeze), that "The medical term for brain freeze is sphenopalatine ganglioneuralgia." The name is built from real anatomy. Its first part points to the [sphenopalatine ganglion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pterygopalatine_ganglion), also called the pterygopalatine ganglion, a parasympathetic nerve cluster deep in the face. Its ending is [neuralgia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuralgia), pain in the distribution of a nerve, so taken literally the label means nerve pain from that ganglion.

Headache specialists write something plainer. In the ICHD-3, the classification the International Headache Society publishes, brain freeze is code 4.5.2, **"Headache attributed to ingestion or inhalation of a cold stimulus,"** and the [previously used terms](https://ichd-3.org/other-primary-headache-disorders/4-5-cold-stimulus-headache/4-5-2-headache-attributed-to-ingestion-or-inhalation-of-a-cold-stimulus/) it lists are "Ice-cream headache; brain-freeze headache." The ganglion name is not among them. The nearest match in the classification belongs to a different disorder: cluster headache lists sphenopalatine neuralgia among its own [older names](https://ichd-3.org/3-trigeminal-autonomic-cephalalgias/3-1-cluster-headache/).

## An early victim mistook an ice for a peach

The complaint is older than the science. In 1773 Patrick Brydone published [*A Tour through Sicily and Malta*](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_a-tour-through-sicily-an_brydone-p-patrick_1773_2), and one of its letters tells of a sea captain at a minister's dinner who was served an ice molded as a fine large peach. He cut it through the middle, put one half in his mouth, puffed out his cheeks, rolled it around with his eyes streaming and finally spat it out [with a horrid oath](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_a-tour-through-sicily-an_brydone-p-patrick_1773_2), calling it a painted snowball.

Retellings often call this [the first written account](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold-stimulus_headache) of a cold-stimulus headache and set it in Sicily. Brydone himself places the dinner [at the house of a certain minister](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_a-tour-through-sicily-an_brydone-p-patrick_1773_2) "of your acquaintance," a digression from the Sicilian feast he was describing, and he records a frozen mouth and tears, with no word about a headache.

## A nerve that files the pain under the wrong address

The roof of the mouth is wired to the [trigeminal nerve](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10144624/), and the same nerve [controls sensation, including pain, in your face](https://www.houstonmethodist.org/blog/articles/2020/jun/what-causes-brain-freeze/). Swallowing adds the pharynx and esophagus, which report through the [glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10144624/), so a gulp may recruit nerves that a lick on the palate does not.

The usual explanation for the misplaced ache is **referred pain**, [pain perceived at a location other than the site of the painful stimulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Referred_pain). In brain freeze it means, in Houston Methodist's words, ["causing you to feel the effects of brain freeze in your forehead and temples instead of your mouth."](https://www.houstonmethodist.org/blog/articles/2020/jun/what-causes-brain-freeze/) A heart attack is the serious version of the same trick: the NHS warns that its chest pain ["may also spread to your arm, neck and jaw"](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/heart-attack/symptoms/).

> Your brain gets a genuine pain signal from the roof of your mouth, but it reads the return address wrong and delivers the ache to your forehead.

## The blood-flow story is still unsettled

What sets those nerves off is the disputed part. A [2023 systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10144624/) of cold-stimulus headache in children and adolescents names two main hypotheses, vascular changes and direct stimulation of cold receptors, and on the vascular side says a cold substance on the palate "may trigger rapid constriction and dilation of vessels with activation of nociceptors of the vessel wall." It adds that the mechanism is not completely understood yet.

Other researchers looked inside the skull, and their results point in different directions. A 1997 letter to the BMJ reported that [in two out of three volunteers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6611440/), flow velocity in the middle cerebral artery fell during an ice-cream headache.

The 2012 study usually cited for the brain-artery idea was a conference abstract. Jorge Serrador of Harvard Medical School and colleagues presented it at Experimental Biology 2012 in San Diego, after having [13 healthy adults](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/04/120422231742.htm) sip ice water through a straw pressed against the upper palate while transcranial Doppler tracked their brain arteries. A press release from the American Physiological Society said the anterior cerebral artery dilated as the pain began and constricted as it faded, and the team speculated that the dilation, then quick constriction, ["may be a type of self-defense for the brain."](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/04/120422231742.htm)

The abstract itself reports less. Ice water produced [a tendency towards increased cerebral flow velocity in the ACA (P=0.078)](https://doi.org/10.1096/fasebj.26.1_supplement.685.4), none in the middle cerebral artery, and significantly greater cerebrovascular resistance than room-temperature water. The self-defense idea remains a hypothesis.

Neurologists in Halle, Germany, ran a bigger test and [published its Doppler readings in 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6611440/). [Of 77 healthy volunteers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6611440/) who drank 200 ml of ice water as fast as they could, 39 got a headache, and in them flow velocity in the middle cerebral arteries rose by an average of **9.3 percent**, against 4.6 percent in those spared. About half of the headache group also had watery eyes, which the authors suggest may point to a reflex linking the trigeminal nerve to the blood vessels. Their own summary of the field is blunt: ["At present, the pathogenesis of HICS is unknown."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6611440/) HICS is their shorthand for the official name.

## Speed matters, and so does the person

A randomized test of eating speed came from a middle school in Hamilton, Ontario. After a Medline search for earlier trials of ice cream and headache produced no results, Maya Kaczorowski and her father Janusz, an associate professor at McMaster University, randomized [145 students at Dalewood Middle School](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC139031/) to eat 100 ml of ice cream either in under 5 seconds or over more than 30. [Twenty of 73](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC139031/) fast eaters got a headache (27%), against 9 of 72 cautious ones (13%). The sessions ran in December and January, to test the old claim that brain freeze strikes only in hot weather, and it struck anyway.

The ice-cream headache trial, published in the BMJ in 2002 with a grade 8 student as first author, lists its funding as ["an unrestricted grant from mum and dad."](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC139031/)

Slowing down helps, but the classification adds that ["eating ice-cream even slowly can do so."](https://ichd-3.org/other-primary-headache-disorders/4-5-cold-stimulus-headache/4-5-2-headache-attributed-to-ingestion-or-inhalation-of-a-cold-stimulus/) Swallowing the cold seems to work better than holding it against the palate: the same Halle volunteers also had ice cubes pressed there, and while [ice water brought it on in 39 of 77 volunteers](https://doi.org/10.1177/0333102416650704), the cubes managed only 9.

Susceptibility varies. In a 1997 BMJ editorial the neurologist Joseph Hulihan cited a figure of [one third of a randomly selected population](https://www.bmj.com/content/314/7091/1364), adding that children know it best by the term "brain freeze." A survey of [8,359 adolescents in Taiwan](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1468-2982.2003.00620.x) found that 40.6% had had one, and that it was more common in students with migraine.

## Warming the palate is the usual fix

Hospital advice follows from the cause. [Houston Methodist](https://www.houstonmethodist.org/blog/articles/2020/jun/what-causes-brain-freeze/) calls a brain freeze "actually harmless" and suggests getting the cold food or drink out of your mouth, taking a sip of warm water, and pressing your tongue against the roof of your mouth.

None of the trials cited here tested a remedy, and the headache passes on its own either way. One that lasts longer than the 10-minute limit, or that arrives with nothing cold involved, is a different kind of headache and worth asking a doctor about.

So the ache between your eyes starts in your mouth. A nerve in your palate raises the alarm, your forehead takes the blame, and the blood vessels involved are still being argued over in the journals. Strictly FYI.
