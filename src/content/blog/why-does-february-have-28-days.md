Augustus usually gets the blame for February. The story runs that the emperor wanted his month, August, to be as long as Julius Caesar's July, so he took a day from February to make up the difference.

By 1919 the Encyclopaedia Britannica had carried that story for nearly a century. Its [1911 edition](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Calendar/Civil_Calendar) says Caesar had given February **29 days, and 30 every fourth year**, until his scheme "was interrupted to gratify the vanity of Augustus." It closes the account with a verdict: "For so frivolous a reason was the regulation of Caesar abandoned."

The Roman writers who list Caesar's changes month by month tell it differently. [Censorinus](https://elfinspell.com/ClassicalTexts/Maude/Censorinus/DeDieNatale-Part2.html) and [Macrobius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Macrobius/Saturnalia/1*.html#14.7) both leave February at **28 days** through Caesar's reform, and both credit Caesar himself with the two days that brought Sextilis, the future August, up to 31.

## Why does February have 28 days?

February has 28 days because, Roman writers say, King Numa built a **355-day year** from lucky odd-length months of 29 or 31 days. Twelve odd numbers cannot add up to an odd total, so one month had to be even, and February, the month of offerings to the dead, got **28**. Julius Caesar's 365-day reform left it alone.

Caesar's reform, decreed in **46 BC** and in force from 45 BC, reached **365 days** by adding a day or two to seven months and nothing to February. Macrobius gave the reason, here in [a translation published in 1919](https://archive.org/stream/sim_popular-astronomy_1919-11_27_9/sim_popular-astronomy_1919-11_27_9_djvu.txt):

> "He added nothing to February in order that the religious rites in honor of the gods of the nether world might not be changed." - Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, translated by Roscoe Lamont

Macrobius lists where Caesar's ten days went:

| Months | Before Caesar | After Caesar |
|---|---|---|
| January, Sextilis (August), December | 29 days | 31 days |
| April, June, September, November | 29 days | 30 days |
| March, May, Quintilis (July), October | 31 days | 31 days |
| February | 28 days | 28 days, 29 in leap years |
| The year | 355 days | 365 days, 366 in leap years |

## Twelve odd months cannot make an odd year

The first Roman year, as Censorinus recorded it, had **10 months and 304 days**, running from March to December. Numa, according to one of his sources, or Tarquin, according to another, replaced it with a 12-month year of 355 days by adding January and February, although 12 months of the moon come to 354 days. Censorinus thought the extra day most likely came from a superstition that held odd numbers to be more perfect and more fortunate.

[Macrobius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Macrobius/Saturnalia/1*.html#13.5) is the source for the arithmetic. Numa, he writes, wanted an odd number of days in the year and in every month but one, because twelve months of odd length would add up to an even total: *unus pari numero institutus universam putationem inparem fecit*, one month given an even number made the whole count odd.

Seven months got **29 days** and four got **31**. February alone kept 28, *quasi inferis et deminutio et par numerus conveniret*, as if a shortfall and an even number both suited the dead. Censorinus records the price: February "alone remained" short, "and was on that account regarded as more unlucky than the others."

## February was named for purification

"The fathers of Rome called purification februa," Ovid wrote in the [Fasti](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/OvidFastiBkTwo.php), his verse calendar of Roman festivals, in Kline's translation. In February, [Plutarch](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Numa*.html#19.5) adds, the Romans "make offerings to the dead and celebrate the festival of the Lupercalia, which, in most of its features, resembles a purification."

February was also once the end of the year. It "was once last in the ancient year," in Ovid's line, and Plutarch says it "had been twelfth and last" before it became the second month.

## The leap day sits where Rome's extra month went

A 355-day year falls about 10 days short of the sun's, so Rome added a month of **22 or 23 days** every other year, Censorinus says. Macrobius explains why it went into February: *quoniam is ultimus anni erat*, because February was the last month of the year. The extra month followed the Terminalia, the festival of **February 23**, and February's last five days came after it. Plutarch calls the extra month Mercedinus, a name also spelled Mercedonius.

The priests who ran the system abused it. Most of them, in Roscoe Lamont's 1919 translation of Censorinus, "on account of enmity or friendship, shortened or lengthened the term of a magistrate." Caesar reset the calendar by giving **46 BC** a total of **445 days**, Censorinus says, then scrapped the extra month.

His leap day went "in the same month and in the same place the ancients had intercalated, that is, before the last five days of February," Macrobius says in Lamont's version. The Romans gave it no number of its own: they counted the sixth day before the Kalends, or first, of March twice. [Smith's Dictionary of Greek and Roman Antiquities](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/secondary/SMIGRA*/Calendarium.html) equates that day with our February 24, and the doubled sixth, *bis sextum*, is why a leap year is still called bissextile.

The 1911 Britannica, wrong about Augustus, has the modern placement right: the day is added "as the 29th."

## The stolen day traces back to a book of 1232

Writing in [*Popular Astronomy*](https://archive.org/stream/sim_popular-astronomy_1919-11_27_9/sim_popular-astronomy_1919-11_27_9_djvu.txt) in **1919**, Lamont traced the Britannica's account to Thomas Galloway, who wrote its Calendar article for the seventh edition of about 1830, and from Galloway to Johannes de Sacrobosco. Sacrobosco's *De anni ratione*, which he said he wrote in **1232**, is a computus, a manual of medieval calendar reckoning, the same tradition in which Bede [gave the moment its 90 seconds](/blog/how-long-is-a-moment/). A [1543 printing](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_wo8zvcIBn68C) gives its common name as *Computus ecclesiasticus*.

In Sacrobosco's version, Caesar gave alternate months 31 and 30 days and left February 29, or 30 in leap years. Then, in Lamont's translation, "Augustus, envious because his month was shorter than the month Julius, stole one day from February and added it to Augustus (August), hence February remained with 28 days." To keep three 31-day months from running together, the story has September give a day to October and November one to December.

Lamont found nothing behind it. Sacrobosco "gives no authority for any of his statements, which are altogether different from anything the Roman writers say," he wrote, and he judged it probable that Sacrobosco had set down the arrangement he thought Caesar ought to have made, with a theory of how it came to be disturbed. The extra 385 yards of the Olympic marathon get pinned on a royal wish in much the same way, and [no contemporary source backs that story either](/blog/why-is-a-marathon-26-2-miles/).

A surviving Roman calendar disagrees too. Lamont cites one inscribed in **31-30 BC**, by the historian Mommsen's dating, more than 20 years before Sextilis was renamed, and in it the month after July already has **31 days**.

## Augustus cancelled leap days to fix a counting error

What Augustus changed was the leap year. The priests had been inserting Caesar's extra day as each fourth year began instead of once it had ended, [Macrobius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Macrobius/Saturnalia/1*.html#14.13) says, in effect every third year. After 36 years they had added **12** leap days instead of **9**, so Augustus ordered **12 years** without one, which absorbed the three extra days.

The renaming came with that repair. In setting the calendar right, [Suetonius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Suetonius/12Caesars/Augustus*.html#31) writes, Augustus "called the month Sextilis by his own surname, rather than his birth-month September, because in the former he had won his first consulship and his most brilliant victories." The Senate's decree, which [Macrobius preserves](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Macrobius/Saturnalia/1*.html#12.35), adds three triumphs, the conquest of Egypt and the end of the civil wars, and says nothing about the month's length.

## Gregory XIII trimmed the leap years in 1582

Caesar's average year of 365 1/4 days runs about 11 minutes longer than the solar year, [Smith's dictionary](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/secondary/SMIGRA*/Calendarium.html) notes. In **1582** Pope Gregory XIII cut 10 days and dropped the leap day from century years not divisible by 400. England kept Caesar's rule until 1752, [Pilgrim Hall](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/old-new-style-date/) records, which is why [the Mayflower's dates run 10 days behind ours](/blog/when-did-the-mayflower-land/), and the older reckoning still [sets the Easter date of most Orthodox churches](/blog/why-does-easter-change-every-year/). In both calendars the extra day stays in February.

Augustus did shorten February, by three leap days spread over 12 years, to repair a counting error. The day he is famous for taking was never there: February had 28 days in an ordinary year both before Caesar's reform and after it. Strictly FYI.
