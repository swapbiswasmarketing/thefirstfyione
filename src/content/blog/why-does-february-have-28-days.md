Augustus usually gets the blame for February. The story runs that the emperor wanted his month, August, to be as long as Julius Caesar's July, so he took a day from February to make up the difference.

The story once had the authority of the Encyclopaedia Britannica behind it. Its [1911 edition](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Calendar/Civil_Calendar) says Caesar had given February **29 days, and 30 every fourth year**, until his scheme "was interrupted to gratify the vanity of Augustus." It closes the account with a verdict: "For so frivolous a reason was the regulation of Caesar abandoned."

The Roman writers who list Caesar's changes month by month tell it differently. [Censorinus](https://elfinspell.com/ClassicalTexts/Maude/Censorinus/DeDieNatale-Part2.html) and [Macrobius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Macrobius/Saturnalia/1*.html#14.7) both leave February at **28 days** through Caesar's reform, and both credit Caesar himself with the two days that brought Sextilis, the future August, up to 31.

## Why does February have 28 days?

February has 28 days because, [Roman writers say](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/secondary/SMIGRA*/Calendarium.html#year_of_Numa), King Numa built a **355-day year** from lucky odd-length months of 29 or 31 days. Twelve odd numbers cannot add up to an odd total, so one month had to be even, and February, the month of offerings to the dead, got **28**. Julius Caesar's 365-day reform kept it at 28 in ordinary years.

Caesar's reform, decreed in **46 BC** and in force from 45 BC, reached **365 days** by adding a day or two to seven months and nothing to February. Macrobius explains the choice, here in [a translation published in 1919](https://archive.org/stream/sim_popular-astronomy_1919-11_27_9/sim_popular-astronomy_1919-11_27_9_djvu.txt):

> "He added nothing to February in order that the religious rites in honor of the gods of the nether world might not be changed." - Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, as quoted by Roscoe Lamont in 1919

[Macrobius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Macrobius/Saturnalia/1*.html#14.7) lists where Caesar's ten days went:

| Months | Before Caesar | After Caesar |
|---|---|---|
| January, Sextilis (August), December | 29 days | 31 days |
| April, June, September, November | 29 days | 30 days |
| March, May, Quintilis (July), October | 31 days | 31 days |
| February | 28 days | 28 days, 29 in leap years |
| The year | 355 days | 365 days, 366 in leap years |

## Twelve odd months cannot make an odd year

Rome's original year had **10 months and 304 days**, running from March to December, in the account [Censorinus](https://elfinspell.com/ClassicalTexts/Maude/Censorinus/DeDieNatale-Part2.html) preferred: Licinius Macer and Fenestella gave Rome 12 months from the start, but he sided with Varro and others. Numa, according to Fulvius, or Tarquin, according to Junius, replaced it with a 12-month year of 355 days by adding January and February, although 12 months of the moon come to 354 days. Censorinus thought the extra day most likely came from a superstition that held odd numbers to be more perfect and more fortunate. February, he adds, "alone remained" short, "and was on that account regarded as more unlucky than the others."

[Macrobius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Macrobius/Saturnalia/1*.html#13.5) is the source for the arithmetic. Numa, he writes, wanted an odd number of days in the year and in every month but one, because twelve months of odd length would add up to an even total: *unus pari numero institutus universam putationem inparem fecit*, one month given an even number made the whole count odd. Seven months got **29 days** and four got **31**. February alone kept 28, *quasi inferis et deminutio et par numerus conveniret*, as if a shortfall and an even number both suited the dead.

The king in this story may be a later attribution. Thomas Hewitt Key, writing the calendar article in [Smith's Dictionary of Greek and Roman Antiquities](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/secondary/SMIGRA*/Calendarium.html#p229), a Victorian reference work, judged that the year of 31-, 29- and 28-day months probably came in at the time of the decemvirs' legislation, and that Censorinus had "erroneously allotted" it to Numa. Either way, Censorinus and Macrobius agree that February was the one even month before Caesar.

## February was named for purification

"The fathers of Rome called purification februa," Ovid wrote in the [Fasti](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/OvidFastiBkTwo.php), his verse calendar of Roman festivals, in Kline's translation. In February, [Plutarch](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Numa*.html#19.5) adds, the Romans "make offerings to the dead and celebrate the festival of the Lupercalia, which, in most of its features, resembles a purification."

February was also once the end of the year. It "was once last in the ancient year," in [Ovid's line](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/OvidFastiBkTwo.php), and [Plutarch](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Numa*.html#18.3) says it "had been twelfth and last" before it became the second month.

## The leap day sits where Rome's extra month went

A 355-day year falls about ten days short of the sun's, so Rome added a month of **22 or 23 days** every other year, [Censorinus](https://elfinspell.com/ClassicalTexts/Maude/Censorinus/DeDieNatale-Part2.html) says, between the Terminalia and the Regifugium. It went into February *quoniam is ultimus anni erat*, [Macrobius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Macrobius/Saturnalia/1*.html#13.14) explains, because February closed the year. The extra month followed the Terminalia, the festival of **February 23**, and February's last five days came after it. Plutarch calls the extra month [Mercedinus](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Numa*.html#18.2).

The priests who ran the system abused it. Most of them, in [Maude's translation of Censorinus](https://elfinspell.com/ClassicalTexts/Maude/Censorinus/DeDieNatale-Part2.html), were "influenced by motives of resentment, or else of friendship," so a magistrate's term was cut short or stretched "as the pontiffs willed." Caesar reset the calendar by giving **46 BC** a total of **445 days**, Censorinus says, then scrapped the extra month.

His leap day went "where the month had been hitherto inserted, that is, immediately after the Terminalia," in [the Dictionary's translation of Censorinus](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/secondary/SMIGRA*/Calendarium.html#bissextum). The Romans gave it no number of its own: they counted the sixth day before the Kalends, or first, of March twice, so the Dictionary's table of a Roman leap year names both February 24 and 25 that way. In Key's words, "From the fact that the intercalated year has two days called ante diem sextum, the name of bissextile has been applied to it."

The [1911 Britannica](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Calendar/Civil_Calendar), wrong about Augustus, has the modern placement right: the day is still added to February, "not, however, between the 24th and 25th, but as the 29th."

## The stolen day traces back to a book its author dated 1232

Writing in [*Popular Astronomy*](https://archive.org/stream/sim_popular-astronomy_1919-11_27_9/sim_popular-astronomy_1919-11_27_9_djvu.txt) in **1919**, Lamont noted that the Britannica's account had stood for nearly a century, since Thomas Galloway wrote its Calendar article for the seventh edition of about 1830. He judged that Galloway had probably taken it from Johannes de Sacrobosco, whose book Galloway cited. Sacrobosco's *De anni ratione*, which he said he wrote in **1232**, is a computus, a manual of medieval calendar reckoning. A [1543 printing](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_wo8zvcIBn68C) gives its common name as *Computus ecclesiasticus*.

In Sacrobosco's version, which Lamont [quoted in English from an Antwerp edition of 1551](https://archive.org/stream/sim_popular-astronomy_1919-11_27_9/sim_popular-astronomy_1919-11_27_9_djvu.txt), Caesar gave the odd months 31 days and the even ones 30, then took a day from February to complete January, leaving February 29, or 30 in leap years. Then "Augustus, envious because his month was shorter than the month Julius, stole one day from February and added it to Augustus (August), hence February remained with 28 days." To keep three 31-day months from running together, the story has September give a day to October and November one to December.

Lamont found nothing behind it. Sacrobosco "gives no authority for any of his statements, which are altogether different from anything the Roman writers say," [he wrote](https://archive.org/stream/sim_popular-astronomy_1919-11_27_9/sim_popular-astronomy_1919-11_27_9_djvu.txt), and he judged it probable that Sacrobosco had set down the arrangement he thought Caesar ought to have made, with a theory of how it came to be disturbed. A surviving Roman calendar disagrees too: Lamont cites one that the historian Mommsen dated to **31-30 BC**, more than 20 years before Sextilis was renamed, and in it the month after July already has **31 days**.

## Augustus cancelled leap days to fix a counting error

What Augustus changed was the leap year. The priests had been inserting Caesar's extra day as each fourth year began instead of once it had ended, [Macrobius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Macrobius/Saturnalia/1*.html#14.13) says, in effect every third year. After 36 years they had added **12** leap days instead of **9**, so Augustus ordered **12 years** without one, which absorbed the three extra days.

The renaming came with that repair. In setting the calendar right, [Suetonius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Suetonius/12Caesars/Augustus*.html#31) writes, Augustus "called the month Sextilis by his own surname, rather than his birth-month September, because in the former he had won his first consulship and his most brilliant victories." The Senate's decree, which Macrobius preserves and [Smith's dictionary translates](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/secondary/SMIGRA*/Calendarium.html#July_and_August), adds three triumphs, the conquest of Egypt and the end of the civil wars, and says nothing about the month's length.

## Gregory XIII trimmed the leap years in 1582

Caesar's average year of 365 1/4 days is too long by about 11 minutes, as the [1911 Britannica](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Calendar/Civil_Calendar) notes. In **1582** Pope Gregory XIII [cut 10 days and dropped the leap day from century years not divisible by 400](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Calendar/Civil_Calendar). England kept Caesar's rule until 1752, [Pilgrim Hall](https://www.pilgrimhall.org/old-new-style-date/) records. In both calendars the extra day stays in February.

Augustus did shorten February for a while, by skipping its leap days to repair a counting error. The day he is famous for taking was never there: February had 28 days in an ordinary year both before Caesar's reform and after it. Strictly FYI.
