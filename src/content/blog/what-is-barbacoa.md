Out back of a little yellow house in Brownsville, Texas, [about a mile from the Rio Grande](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/952807078), sits a brick-lined pit **seven feet long**, filled with triangular foil-wrapped cow heads. "This is where the magic happens," the owner, Armando Vera, [told NPR's John Burnett](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/952807078) in the smokehouse of Vera's Backyard Bar-B-Que, where his family had been making barbacoa for **65 years** when the story aired in 2021. At Vera's the mesquite wood has to be burned down to charcoal, and [that alone takes eight hours](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/952807078).

Vera's is [believed to be the last restaurant in Texas](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/952807078) to cook barbacoa the old-fashioned way, in a pit. At other restaurants that serve it, [Burnett reported](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/952807078), the beef heads "are cooked in big kettles on a stovetop, not underground."

## What is barbacoa?

Barbacoa is **Mexican pit-cooked meat**: sheep, goat or beef [wrapped in maguey (agave) leaves and slow-cooked in a hole dug in the ground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbacoa), often [overnight](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/952807078). In the United States it is [commonly **beef cheek**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbacoa). The Spanish word, probably from **Taino**, first named a [wooden frame for cooking or drying meat](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/barbecue), and gave English barbecue.

Barbacoa names a way of cooking as much as a meat, the way [french fries](/blog/why-are-french-fries-called-french-fries/) name a way of frying. The dictionary of the [Real Academia Espanola](https://dle.rae.es/barbacoa) defines its sense in Mexico, Bolivia, Guatemala and Peru as a set of green-wood sticks laid like a grill in a hole in the ground, for roasting meat.

## Barbacoa cooks overnight in a pit lined with maguey

In **1887** the American writer Fanny Chambers Gooch called barbacoa "one of the principal articles of food known to the Mexican market" and "good enough for the table of a king," in [*Face to Face with the Mexicans*](https://archive.org/details/facetofacewithme00igleuoft). The cook, [she wrote](https://archive.org/details/facetofacewithme00igleuoft), took a quartered mutton, "using also head and bones," heated a hole in the ground with a fire and stone slabs, lined it with maguey leaves and covered the meat with more, and "the process of cooking goes on all night." By morning it was "a delicious, brown, crisp, barbecued mutton," [Gooch wrote](https://archive.org/details/facetofacewithme00igleuoft), and because the work was difficult and tedious, even the wealthiest families bought it ready-cooked at the market.

Lamb is still the [meat of choice in central Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbacoa). In South Texas a pit is [the traditional way to cook whole beef heads](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/952807078), and the food historian Adan Medrano told NPR in 2021 that barbacoa de pozo, pit barbacoa, is "iconic to our culture."

On the Mexican ranches where the method began, Vera [told NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/952807078), "you dug a hole in the ground," put the beef head in and cooked it "about 10 or 12 hours overnight." The tradition is now kept alive mostly by backyard cooks, [Medrano said](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/952807078): "People in Houston and in San Antonio actually dig holes in their backyard to this day."

## In South Texas, barbacoa is a cow's head

At Vera's the meat is ordered by the part, [NPR's John Burnett explained](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/952807078): cachete, cheek meat, and lengua, the tongue, with the palate, eyes and sweetbreads also on the menu. Cheek meat, head meat, tongue and brains all appear in the USDA's [specifications for variety meats and edible by-products](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/LSimps700.pdf), the series that also lists [oxtail](/blog/where-do-oxtails-come-from/).

So barbacoa can include brains: barbacoa de cabeza, a whole cow's head cooked in an earth oven, [takes in the tongue and brains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbacoa). The pound that Burnett's guide, a Brownsville taco connoisseur, [ordered](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/952807078) was cachete. The meat is "shredded to the consistency of pulled pork," [Burnett said](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/952807078), but pulled pork is [shredded pork shoulder, typically slow-smoked over wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulled_pork), a dish of the American South.

| Region | Regional style | Usual meat | How it cooks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Central Mexico | Barbacoa | Lamb | In a pit, wrapped in maguey leaves |
| South Texas | Barbacoa de cabeza | A cow's head: cheek, tongue, palate, eyes | Traditionally in a pit; in restaurants, steamed or in kettles |
| Jalisco | Birria | Goat, beef or lamb | Marinated in a chile adobo, cooked in broth |
| Yucatan | Cochinita pibil | Pork | Wrapped in banana leaf, in an earth oven called a piib |

In Jalisco and nearby states, birria was historically [the regional name](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbacoa) for what other regions of Mexico call barbacoa. In the Yucatan, [the traditional version](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbacoa) is cochinita pibil, pit-style pork.

## The word first named a frame of sticks

In **1526** the Spanish chronicler Gonzalo Fernandez de Oviedo [wrote](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/Sumario_de_la_natural_y_general_historia_de_las_Indias/X) that the Indians of Tierra Firme, the [Spanish mainland on the Caribbean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Main), roasted deer, wild pigs and fish on sticks set up like grills or trivets, with the fire underneath, and called these frames barbacoas. In his longer history of the Indies, [Oviedo described](https://archive.org/details/historiageneraly01fern) boys posted above the maize fields of Hispaniola, in trees and on scaffolds of wood and cane roofed against sun and rain, who kept shouting to drive off the parrots and other birds; these scaffolds, too, were barbacoas.

The [Real Academia's dictionary](https://dle.rae.es/barbacoa) still lists that lookout scaffold as a sense of the word in Cuba, Ecuador and Mexico. Its other regional senses include a small house built high on trees or stakes (Cuba, Ecuador and Peru), a rough loft for grain and fruit (Cuba, Ecuador and the Dominican Republic) and a frame of wattle on props used as a bed (Ecuador and Peru).

On the word's origin [the dictionary](https://dle.rae.es/barbacoa) hedges, *quiza del taino*, perhaps from Taino, which [at the time of Spanish contact was the most common language spoken throughout the Caribbean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta%C3%ADno_language). Why the Spanish in Mexico gave the name to an earth oven is unknown, according to [Wikipedia's article on barbacoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbacoa), which adds that a rival derivation from a Maya phrase has no evidence behind it.

## English used the word for smoking fish and a bed of sticks

A [1648 pamphlet](https://www.virtualjamestown.org/exist/cocoon/jamestown/fha/J1053) promoting a colony called New Albion reported that "the Indians in stead of salt doe barbecado or dry and smoak fish." In **1681** the English buccaneer William Dampier slept on a barbecue, which in his English meant a frame of sticks raised about 3 feet off the ground, according to his [*New Voyage Round the World*](https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_a-new-voyage-round-the-w_dampier-william_1697). He was [crossing the Isthmus of Darien on foot](https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_a-new-voyage-round-the-w_dampier-william_1697) that May when his party came to a river too deep to cross.

> "We built Hutts upon its Banks and lay there all night, upon our Barbecu's, or frames of Sticks, raised about 3 foot from the ground." - William Dampier, [*A New Voyage Round the World*](https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_a-new-voyage-round-the-w_dampier-william_1697), 1697

By morning the river "had overflowed its Banks, and was 2 foot deep in our Hutts," [Dampier wrote](https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_a-new-voyage-round-the-w_dampier-william_1697). In English the word also came to mean the meat cooked over the fire and the gathering around it: [Merriam-Webster's](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/barbecue) noun covers a large animal roasted whole over an open fire or a fire in a pit, and a social gathering, especially in the open air, where barbecued food is eaten.

## The beard-to-tail story is folk etymology

[A popular story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbecue) derives barbecue from French barbe a queue, "from beard to tail," for an animal roasted whole. "Leaving aside the question that pigs don't have beards (though the allusion would work for goats), the true origin is well authenticated, and the story is just another example of folk etymology," Michael Quinion wrote at [World Wide Words](https://www.worldwidewords.org/qa/qa-bar1.htm).

A second story skips the frame and runs the Mexican pit straight into English. Reporting from Vera's, [NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/952807078) said that subterranean cooking "is how barbacoa became the root of the word barbecue." Barbacoa is the root, but [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/barbecue) traces barbecue to the American Spanish word for a wooden frame, and the English of the New Albion pamphlet and of Dampier used it for smoking fish and for a bed of sticks.

The cow heads in the brick pit at Vera's and the bed of sticks that Dampier's party slept on above a rising river go back to one word. Mexico gave it to the pit, and English gave it to the roast and the party. Strictly FYI.
