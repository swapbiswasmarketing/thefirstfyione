Out back of a little yellow house in Brownsville, Texas, [about a mile from the Rio Grande](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/952807078), sits a brick-lined pit **seven feet long**, filled with triangular foil-wrapped cow heads. "This is where the magic happens," the owner, Armando Vera, told NPR's John Burnett in the smokehouse of Vera's Backyard Bar-B-Que, where his family had been making barbacoa for **65 years** when the story aired in 2021. At Vera's the mesquite wood has to be burned down to charcoal, and that alone takes eight hours. At the time, Vera's was believed to be the last restaurant in Texas to cook barbacoa the old-fashioned way, in a pit. Barbacoa is on the menus of some serious Mexican restaurants, Burnett reported, but there the beef heads "are cooked in big kettles on a stovetop, not underground."

## What is barbacoa?

Barbacoa is **Mexican pit-cooked meat**: sheep, goat or beef [slow-cooked in a hole dug in the ground and covered with maguey (agave) leaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbacoa). In the United States it is often made from [parts of a cow's head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbacoa), such as the **cheeks**. The Spanish word, probably from the **Taino** of Hispaniola, named a [wooden framework for supporting meat while it cooked or dried](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/barbecue), and gave English barbecue.

In Mexico, Bolivia, Guatemala and Peru, according to the dictionary of the [Real Academia Espanola](https://dle.rae.es/barbacoa), one sense of the word is a set of green-wood sticks laid like a grill in a hole in the ground, for roasting meat.

## Barbacoa cooks overnight in a pit lined with maguey

In **1887** the American writer Fanny Chambers Gooch called barbacoa "one of the principal articles of food known to the Mexican market" and "good enough for the table of a king," in [*Face to Face with the Mexicans*](https://archive.org/details/facetofacewithme00igleuoft). The cook, [she wrote](https://archive.org/details/facetofacewithme00igleuoft), took a quartered mutton, "using also head and bones," heated a hole in the ground with a fire and stone slabs, lined it with maguey leaves and covered the meat with more, and "the process of cooking goes on all night." By morning it was "a delicious, brown, crisp, barbecued mutton," [Gooch wrote](https://archive.org/details/facetofacewithme00igleuoft), and because the work was difficult and tedious, even the wealthiest families bought it ready-cooked at the market.

Lamb is the favorite in central Mexico, where states such as Hidalgo have a fine reputation for barbacoa, [UnoTV reported](https://www.unotv.com/turismo/barbacoa-en-hidalgo-ve-donde-comerla/) in 2022. [Mexico's culture ministry](https://www.cultura.gob.mx/noticias/patrimonio-cultural-arquitectura-y-turismo/10981-la-tradicion-gastronomica-de-hidalgo-ha-dado-fama-mundial-a-la-cocina-mexicana.html) describes the Hidalgo dish as lamb cooked in an earth pit under maguey leaves.

In July 2021 [*Revista del Consumidor*](https://www.gob.mx/cms/uploads/attachment/file/650948/PLATILLO_SABIO_BARBACOA.pdf), the magazine of Mexico's federal consumer agency, watched the Hidalgo cook Cresencio Castillo, whose family has kept the method for five generations, prepare lamb to cook for **8 to 10 hours** in a hole dug in the earth and walled with brick. The fire is built with firewood, dried maguey leaves and stones, and fresh maguey leaves line the pit and cover the meat, much as Gooch described.

In South Texas a pit is the traditional way to cook whole beef heads, and the food historian Adan Medrano [told NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/952807078) in 2021 that barbacoa de pozo, pit barbacoa, is "iconic to our culture." The method, Vera said, "originated in the ranches in Mexico": "you dug a hole in the ground," put the beef head in and cooked it "about 10 or 12 hours overnight." The tradition is now kept alive mostly by backyard cooks, Medrano said: "People in Houston and in San Antonio actually dig holes in their backyard to this day."

## In South Texas, barbacoa is a cow's head

At Vera's, [Burnett reported](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/952807078), the meat is "shredded to the consistency of pulled pork," the [slow-smoked pork shoulder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulled_pork) of the American South. It is ordered by the part: cachete, cheek meat, and lengua, the tongue, with the palate, eyes and sweetbreads also on the menu.

Cheek meat even has a purchasing definition: the USDA's [specifications for variety meats](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/LSimps700.pdf) describe it as "the group of muscles lying external to the upper and lower jaw bones," and list tongue, head meat and brains in the same series. A whole head roasted in an earth oven, barbacoa de cabeza, [takes in the tongue and the brains too](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbacoa).

| Region | Regional style | Usual meat | How it cooks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hidalgo, central Mexico | Barbacoa | Lamb | In a pit, under maguey leaves |
| South Texas | Barbacoa de cabeza | A cow's head: cheek, tongue, palate, eyes | Traditionally in a pit; at some restaurants, in kettles on a stovetop |
| Jalisco | Birria | Goat, beef or lamb | Marinated in a chile adobo, cooked in broth |
| Yucatan | Cochinita pibil | Pork | Wrapped in banana leaf, in an earth oven called a piib |

In and around Jalisco, birria was originally [the regional name for a form of barbacoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birria), though many people, mostly in the United States, now treat it as a distinct dish. On the Yucatan Peninsula a traditional Maya pit dish is [cochinita pibil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochinita_pibil), pork marinated in sour citrus juice and annatto, wrapped in banana leaf and roasted in a piib.

## The word first named a frame of sticks

In **1526** the Spanish chronicler Gonzalo Fernandez de Oviedo [wrote](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/Sumario_de_la_natural_y_general_historia_de_las_Indias/X) that the Indians of Tierra Firme, the [Spanish mainland on the Caribbean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Main), roasted deer, wild pigs and fish on sticks set up like grills or trivets, with the fire underneath, and called these frames barbacoas. In his longer history of the Indies, [Oviedo described](https://archive.org/details/historiageneraly01fern) boys posted above the maize fields of Hispaniola, in trees and on scaffolds of wood and cane roofed against sun and rain, who kept shouting to drive off the parrots and other birds; these scaffolds, too, were barbacoas.

The [Real Academia's dictionary](https://dle.rae.es/barbacoa) still lists that lookout scaffold as a sense of the word in Cuba, Ecuador and Mexico. Its other regional senses include a small house built high on trees or stakes (Cuba, Ecuador and Peru), a rough loft for grain and fruit (Cuba, Ecuador and the Dominican Republic) and a frame of wattle on props used as a bed (Ecuador and Peru).

On the word's origin [the dictionary](https://dle.rae.es/barbacoa) hedges, *quiza del taino*, perhaps from Taino, the [first Indigenous language encountered by Europeans in the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta%C3%ADno_language). A rival story derives the Mexican word from a Maya phrase, Baalbak'Kaab, glossed as meat covered with soil in a [photo story on the barbacoa of Coscomatepec, Veracruz](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/barbacoa-colectivo-rokunin/KAWxjVPsBbaMFw?hl=en). Neither the Real Academia nor [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/barbecue) mentions it; both give Taino.

## English used the word for smoking fish and a bed of sticks

A [1648 pamphlet](https://www.virtualjamestown.org/exist/cocoon/jamestown/fha/J1053) promoting a proposed colony called New Albion reported that "the Indians in stead of salt doe barbecado or dry and smoak fish." In **1681** the English buccaneer William Dampier slept on a barbecue, which in his English meant a frame of sticks raised about 3 feet off the ground, according to his [*New Voyage Round the World*](https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_a-new-voyage-round-the-w_dampier-william_1697). His party had landed on the Pacific side of the Isthmus of Darien on May 1 and [set a northeast course by pocket compass](https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_a-new-voyage-round-the-w_dampier-william_1697) for the Caribbean, and on the twelfth day they came to a river too deep to cross.

> "We built Hutts upon its Banks and lay there all night, upon our Barbecu's, or frames of Sticks, raised about 3 foot from the ground." - William Dampier, [*A New Voyage Round the World*](https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_a-new-voyage-round-the-w_dampier-william_1697), 1697

By morning the river "had overflowed its Banks, and was 2 foot deep in our Hutts," [Dampier wrote](https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_a-new-voyage-round-the-w_dampier-william_1697). In English the word also came to mean the meat cooked over the fire and the gathering around it: [Merriam-Webster's](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/barbecue) noun covers a large animal roasted whole or split over an open fire or a fire in a pit, and a social gathering, especially in the open air, where barbecued food is eaten.

## The beard-to-tail story is folk etymology

[Many people believe](https://www.worldwidewords.org/qa/qa-bar1.htm) that barbecue comes from French barbe a queue, "from beard to tail," for an animal roasted whole. "Leaving aside the question that pigs don't have beards (though the allusion would work for goats), the true origin is well authenticated, and the story is just another example of folk etymology," Michael Quinion wrote at [World Wide Words](https://www.worldwidewords.org/qa/qa-bar1.htm).

A second story skips the frame and runs the Mexican pit straight into English. Reporting from Vera's, [NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/952807078) said that subterranean cooking "is how barbacoa became the root of the word barbecue." Barbacoa is the root, but [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/barbecue) traces barbecue to the American Spanish word for a wooden framework, and the English of the New Albion pamphlet and of Dampier used it for smoking fish and for a bed of sticks.

The cow heads in the brick pit at Vera's and the bed of sticks that Dampier's party slept on above a rising river go back to one word. Mexico gave it to the pit, and English gave it to the party. Strictly FYI.
