Each of a scallop's eyes, and there can be [**up to 200 of them**](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/scallops-amazing-eyes-use-millions-tiny-square-crystals-see), focuses light with a mirror at the back of the eye rather than with its lens. The eyes line the edge of the mantle, the soft tissue that shows at the rim of the shell when it opens, and each is [about a millimeter wide](https://www.aip.org/inside-science/understanding-how-scallops-view-the-world). In the king scallop, each eye holds "a cornea, a weakly refracting lens, and a concave mirror," in the words of [a 2017 study in *Science*](https://weizmann.ac.il/molgen/gur/sites/molgen.gur/files/2024-10/Palmer%20et%20al%202017.pdf), and the image forms when light bounces off that mirror onto the retina in front of it.

The meat sold as scallops is another part of the same animal: the adductor muscle, which holds the two shells together and is [the part of the scallop Americans typically eat](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/atlantic-sea-scallop), NOAA Fisheries says. The scallop also swims with it.

## What are scallops?

Scallops are **bivalve mollusks**, saltwater shellfish with two hinged shells, [like clams and oysters](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/atlantic-sea-scallop). Most species can swim by clapping their shells together, and most have [a ring of eyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scallop) around the edge of the mantle, though [deep-sea species have lost theirs](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13127-013-0165-z). As seafood, the word also means the **adductor muscle**, the round white muscle that closes the shell.

Scallops make up the family Pectinidae, which has [over 300 living species in 60 genera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scallop), found [in all of the world's oceans, although never in fresh water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scallop). Like the [oysters and mussels that grow pearls](/blog/where-do-pearls-come-from/), they are mollusks, not fish. Shellfish is a [colloquial and fisheries term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shellfish) for aquatic invertebrates with hard outer coverings that people eat, and it takes in crustaceans such as shrimp, crabs and [barnacles](/blog/what-are-barnacles/) as well as mollusks such as clams, oysters and scallops.

## The meat is the muscle the scallop swims with

A scallop has a single adductor muscle, larger and more developed than an oyster's [because scallops are active swimmers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scallop). Snapping its shells open and shut with that muscle, a sea scallop "can propel itself through the water," [NOAA Fisheries says](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/atlantic-sea-scallop), and that "helps them escape predators, such as sea stars, that other bivalves like mussels, clams, and oysters can't avoid."

A swimming scallop travels with its open edge forward. It [sucks water in through the gap between its valves, called the gape, and squirts it out through small holes near the hinge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scallop), snapping "open and shut like Pac-Man," as [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/scallops-amazing-eyes-use-millions-tiny-square-crystals-see) put it. Bursts often last [several seconds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scallop), and then the scallop closes up and sinks back to the bottom.

The muscle has two parts, divided by a sheet of connective tissue. A fast, striated part contracts quickly for swimming, while a smooth catch muscle [contracts for long periods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scallop), "keeping shells closed with little expenditure of energy."

Outside the United States, scallops are commonly [sold whole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scallop), roe and all. The roe, called the coral, is soft and often bright reddish-orange.

## The king scallop's mirrors are tiled with square crystals

The British biologist Michael Land found in the mid-1960s that a curved mirror at the back of the king scallop's eye focuses light onto the retina, "similar to how a reflecting telescope might focus light onto a detector," as [Inside Science](https://www.aip.org/inside-science/understanding-how-scallops-view-the-world) summed up his finding. The mirror is made of [layers of guanine crystals](https://www.aip.org/inside-science/understanding-how-scallops-view-the-world), and guanine is best known as [one of the four main bases in DNA and RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guanine). The compound was [named in 1846](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guanine) for guano, the [seabird droppings](/blog/why-is-bird-poop-white/) it was first isolated from.

For the 2017 study, Benjamin Palmer of the Weizmann Institute of Science and his colleagues [flash-froze the mirrors under high pressure and examined them with a scanning electron microscope](https://www.aip.org/inside-science/understanding-how-scallops-view-the-world). The guanine crystals in the king scallop's mirror turned out to be **flat squares**, [each about 1.2 micrometers across and about 74 nanometers thick](https://weizmann.ac.il/molgen/gur/sites/molgen.gur/files/2024-10/Palmer%20et%20al%202017.pdf), fitted together "edge-to-edge like bathroom tiles," [Science News reported](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/scallops-amazing-eyes-use-millions-tiny-square-crystals-see). "It's the first time we've seen a perfect square!" Palmer told [the magazine](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/scallops-amazing-eyes-use-millions-tiny-square-crystals-see).

> "Paradoxically, the guanine squares don't reflect light on their own - they're transparent. But their arrangement turns them into a collective mirror." - [Carl Zimmer](https://carlzimmer.com/the-scallop-sees-with-space-age-eyes-hundreds-of-them-2/), *The New York Times*

The mirror stacks [**20 to 30 layers** of tiles](https://www.aip.org/inside-science/understanding-how-scallops-view-the-world), and the spacing between them is tuned to reflect the blue-green light that reaches the scallop underwater. "You have a mirror that basically reflects a hundred percent of the blue light it receives," Palmer told [Carl Zimmer](https://carlzimmer.com/the-scallop-sees-with-space-age-eyes-hundreds-of-them-2/). A cat also carries a mirror behind its retina, the tapetum lucidum, but focuses with its cornea and lens and uses the mirror to [give light a second pass through the retina](/blog/can-cats-see-in-the-dark/).

Each eye also has two retinas, one above the other. By the study's ray-tracing, light from straight ahead focuses mainly on one layer and [light from the periphery on the other](https://www.aip.org/inside-science/understanding-how-scallops-view-the-world).

Land, by then an emeritus professor at the University of Sussex, was not persuaded: his own experiments had convinced him that the mirror focused light on one layer only, and he wrote that he would need ["a lot more reassurance"](https://www.aip.org/inside-science/understanding-how-scallops-view-the-world) that preparing the eyes had not distorted them. Palmer noted that the team had scanned both fresh and chemically preserved eyes and found the mirrors' shapes similar in both.

Eye color varies by species. The bay scallop's eyes are iridescent blue and the sea scallop's are black, and a [2019 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6833330/) found the likely source of the blue: a layer of nanospheres, [about 180 nanometers across](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6833330/), in the cells covering each bay scallop eye, which the sea scallop's eyes lack. Its authors speculated that the nanospheres help keep ultraviolet light from damaging the inside of the eye.

Scallops rely on their eyes mainly as an early-warning system, [scanning for movement and shadows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scallop) that could mean a predator. They are filter feeders that do not stalk prey, so why they carry so many eyes, each so elaborately built, was [still a mystery when the study appeared](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/scallops-amazing-eyes-use-millions-tiny-square-crystals-see). "It's still a puzzle why they see so well," Daniel Speiser of the University of South Carolina, who was not involved in the study, told [Carl Zimmer](https://carlzimmer.com/the-scallop-sees-with-space-age-eyes-hundreds-of-them-2/).

## Sea scallops and bay scallops are different species

The Atlantic sea scallop, *Placopecten magellanicus*, supports what NOAA calls [the largest and most valuable wild scallop fishery in the world](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/atlantic-sea-scallop). The bay scallop, *Argopecten irradians*, is a smaller animal of [shallow water, usually among eelgrass beds](https://www.chesapeakebay.net/discover/field-guide/entry/bay-scallop), with [30 to 40 blue eyes](https://www.chesapeakebay.net/discover/field-guide/entry/bay-scallop) around the edge of its shell.

| Shell size | [Typically no larger than 6 inches](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/atlantic-sea-scallop) | [Grows to 3 inches](https://www.chesapeakebay.net/discover/field-guide/entry/bay-scallop) |
| Lifespan | [Up to 20 years](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/atlantic-sea-scallop) | [About two years](https://www.chesapeakebay.net/discover/field-guide/entry/bay-scallop) |
| Eye color | [Black](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6833330/) | [Blue](https://www.chesapeakebay.net/discover/field-guide/entry/bay-scallop) |

Sea scallop meats are graded by how many make a pound, and [**U10** means fewer than 10 meats per pound](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/atlantic-sea-scallop). Sea scallops "have a sweet, rich taste that can be mild or briny," [NOAA says](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/atlantic-sea-scallop).

## The skate-wing scallop is a rumor of unknown size

A persistent story holds that cheap "scallops" are really skate wing, stamped into rounds. The Monterey Bay Aquarium's Seafood Watch program took it up in its [report on Atlantic skates](https://web.archive.org/web/20130930070404/http://www.montereybayaquarium.org/cr/cr_seafoodwatch/content/media/MBA_SeafoodWatch_AtlanticSkatesReport.pdf), first issued in 2005, citing a 1996 book on Pacific fishes and a 2004 NPR interview: there are "ongoing reports that round pieces of skate wing, stamped out with a device like a cookie cutter, are or have been offered for sale as scallops," it says, but "the extent of this practice remains unknown." The report calls the swap a "rumored practice" and suggests that genetic testing of seafood could show how common it is, if it happens at all.

Most boats in the sea scallop fishery land their catch as [shucked meats](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/atlantic-sea-scallop), the adductor muscle alone, so the eyes stay behind with the rest of the animal. The white disc on the plate is the muscle that clapped the shell shut, and the animal it came from watched the water through a row of eyes, each backed by a tiny mirror. Strictly FYI.
