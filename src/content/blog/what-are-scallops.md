Each of a scallop's eyes, and there can be [**up to 200 of them**](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/scallops-amazing-eyes-use-millions-tiny-square-crystals-see), focuses light with a mirror at the back of the eye rather than with its lens. The eyes line the edge of the mantle, the soft tissue that shows at the rim of the shell when it opens, and each is [about a millimeter wide](https://www.aip.org/inside-science/understanding-how-scallops-view-the-world). In the king scallop, each eye holds "a cornea, a weakly refracting lens, and a concave mirror," in the words of [a 2017 study in *Science*](https://weizmann.ac.il/molgen/gur/sites/molgen.gur/files/2024-10/Palmer%20et%20al%202017.pdf), and the image forms when light bounces off that mirror onto the retina in front of it.

The meat sold as scallops is another part of the same animal: the adductor muscle, which holds the two shells together and is [the part of the scallop Americans typically eat](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/atlantic-sea-scallop), NOAA Fisheries says. The scallop also swims with it.

## What are scallops?

Scallops are **bivalve mollusks**, saltwater shellfish with two hinged shells, like clams and oysters. [Most species can swim](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3129317/) by clapping their shells together, and most have [a ring of eyes](https://carlzimmer.com/the-scallop-sees-with-space-age-eyes-hundreds-of-them-2/) around the edge of the mantle, though [deep-sea species have lost theirs](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13127-013-0165-z). As seafood, the word also means the **adductor muscle**, the round white muscle that closes the shell.

Scallops make up the family Pectinidae, and counts of its species differ: a 2014 study of their eyes put it at [more than 300 species worldwide](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13127-013-0165-z). A [2011 study gave 264 recognized species, living from the intertidal zone to depths of 7000 meters](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3129317/), always in the sea. Shellfish is a [colloquial and fisheries term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shellfish) for aquatic invertebrates with hard outer coverings that people eat, and it takes in crustaceans such as shrimp and crabs as well as mollusks such as clams, oysters and scallops.

## The meat is the muscle the scallop swims with

Snapping its shells open and shut with the adductor, a sea scallop "can propel itself through the water," [NOAA Fisheries says](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/atlantic-sea-scallop), and that "helps them escape predators, such as sea stars, that other bivalves like mussels, clams, and oysters can't avoid."

A swimming scallop travels with its open edge forward. It [sucks water in through the gap between its valves, called the gape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scallop), and squirts it out through small holes near the hinge, in bursts that often last several seconds before the scallop closes up and sinks back to the bottom. [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/scallops-amazing-eyes-use-millions-tiny-square-crystals-see) likened the motion to snapping "open and shut like Pac-Man."

The muscle has two parts, divided by a sheet of connective tissue. A fast, striated part contracts quickly for swimming, while a slow, smooth catch part contracts for long periods, "keeping shells closed with little expenditure of energy," as [a 2018 study of the fast and slow parts](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5963113/) puts it.

Outside the United States, [scallops are often sold whole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scallop), roe and all. The roe, called the coral, is soft and often bright reddish-orange.

## The king scallop's mirrors are tiled with square crystals

The British biologist Michael Land showed in [a 1965 paper on the eye of the scallop *Pecten maximus*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1357328/) that a curved mirror at the back of the king scallop's eye focuses light onto the retina, "similar to how a reflecting telescope might focus light onto a detector," as [Inside Science](https://www.aip.org/inside-science/understanding-how-scallops-view-the-world) summed up his finding. The mirror is made of layers of guanine crystals, and guanine is best known as [one of the four main bases in DNA and RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guanine).

Benjamin Palmer of the Weizmann Institute of Science and his colleagues, who froze the eyes under high pressure and examined them with a cryogenic scanning electron microscope, found in the 2017 study that the guanine crystals in the king scallop's mirror are **flat squares**, [each about 1.2 micrometers across and about 74 nanometers thick](https://weizmann.ac.il/molgen/gur/sites/molgen.gur/files/2024-10/Palmer%20et%20al%202017.pdf). The squares fit together "edge-to-edge like bathroom tiles," [Science News reported](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/scallops-amazing-eyes-use-millions-tiny-square-crystals-see), quoting Palmer: "It's the first time we've seen a perfect square!"

> "Paradoxically, the guanine squares don't reflect light on their own - they're transparent. But their arrangement turns them into a collective mirror." - [Carl Zimmer](https://carlzimmer.com/the-scallop-sees-with-space-age-eyes-hundreds-of-them-2/), *The New York Times*

The mirror stacks [**20 to 30 layers** of tiles](https://www.aip.org/inside-science/understanding-how-scallops-view-the-world), and the spacing between them is tuned to reflect the blue-green light that reaches the scallop underwater. "You have a mirror that basically reflects a hundred percent of the blue light it receives," Palmer told [Carl Zimmer](https://carlzimmer.com/the-scallop-sees-with-space-age-eyes-hundreds-of-them-2/).

Each eye also has two retinas, one above the other. By the study's ray-tracing, the mirror forms images on [a double-layered retina "used for separately imaging the peripheral and central fields of view"](https://weizmann.ac.il/molgen/gur/sites/molgen.gur/files/2024-10/Palmer%20et%20al%202017.pdf), light from straight ahead focusing mainly on one layer and light from the periphery on the other. Land, by then an emeritus professor at the University of Sussex, was not persuaded: his own experiments had convinced him that the mirror focused light on one layer only, and he wrote that he would need ["a lot more reassurance"](https://www.aip.org/inside-science/understanding-how-scallops-view-the-world) that preparing the eyes had not distorted them. Palmer noted that the team had scanned both fresh and chemically preserved eyes and found the mirrors' shapes similar in both.

Eye color varies by species. The bay scallop's eyes are iridescent blue and the sea scallop's are black, and a [2019 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6833330/) found the likely source of the blue: a layer of nanospheres, about 180 nanometers across, in the cells covering each bay scallop eye, which the sea scallop's eyes lack. Its authors speculated that the nanospheres help keep ultraviolet light from damaging the inside of the eye.

Earlier experiments had shown that the upper retina responds to "relatively dark, moving features, triggering defense or escape reflexes," [the 2017 paper notes](https://weizmann.ac.il/molgen/gur/sites/molgen.gur/files/2024-10/Palmer%20et%20al%202017.pdf), and its authors suggested the lower retina's well-focused peripheral view could help guide a scallop while it swims. Scallops are filter feeders that do not stalk prey, so why they carry so many eyes, each so elaborately built, was [still a mystery when the study appeared](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/scallops-amazing-eyes-use-millions-tiny-square-crystals-see). "It's still a puzzle why they see so well," Daniel Speiser of the University of South Carolina, who was not involved in the study, told [Carl Zimmer](https://carlzimmer.com/the-scallop-sees-with-space-age-eyes-hundreds-of-them-2/).

## Sea scallops and bay scallops are different species

The Atlantic sea scallop, *Placopecten magellanicus*, [ranges from Newfoundland to Cape Hatteras, North Carolina, is generally found about 100 to 300 feet deep on Georges Bank and in the Mid-Atlantic, typically grows no larger than 6 inches and can live up to 20 years](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/atlantic-sea-scallop), and it supports what NOAA calls the largest and most valuable wild scallop fishery in the world. The bay scallop, *Argopecten irradians*, is a smaller animal of shallow water, usually among eelgrass beds, that [grows to 3 inches, lives about two years and has 30 to 40 blue eyes](https://www.chesapeakebay.net/discover/field-guide/entry/bay-scallop) around the edge of its shell.

|---|---|---|
| Species | *Placopecten magellanicus* | *Argopecten irradians* |

Most boats land sea scallops as shucked meats, the adductor muscle alone, [graded by how many make a pound, so **U10** means fewer than 10 meats per pound](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/atlantic-sea-scallop), and NOAA says they "have a sweet, rich taste that can be mild or briny."

## The skate-wing scallop is a rumor of unknown size

A persistent story holds that cheap "scallops" are really skate wing, stamped into rounds. The Monterey Bay Aquarium's Seafood Watch program took it up in its [report on Atlantic skates](https://web.archive.org/web/20130930070404/http://www.montereybayaquarium.org/cr/cr_seafoodwatch/content/media/MBA_SeafoodWatch_AtlanticSkatesReport.pdf), first issued in 2005, citing a 1996 book on Pacific fishes and a 2004 NPR interview: there are "ongoing reports that round pieces of skate wing, stamped out with a device like a cookie cutter, are or have been offered for sale as scallops," it says, but "the extent of this practice remains unknown." The report calls the swap a "rumored practice" and suggests that genetic testing of seafood could show how common it is, if it happens at all.

Because most of the sea scallop catch is landed as shucked meat, the eyes usually stay behind with the rest of the animal. The white disc on the plate is the muscle that clapped the shell shut, and the animal it came from watched the water through a row of eyes, each backed by a tiny mirror. Strictly FYI.
