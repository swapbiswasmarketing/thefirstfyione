["Snakes don't have ears; most people don't know that,"](https://www.npr.org/2011/08/08/139086119/in-india-snake-charmers-are-losing-their-sway) Kartick Satyanarayan, a co-founder of the animal rescue group Wildlife SOS, told NPR in 2011. The reporter, Corey Flintoff, had watched a cobra rise from a basket and sway in front of a charmer's gourd flute in India, and wrote that ["the snake can't hear a thing."](https://www.npr.org/2011/08/08/139086119/in-india-snake-charmers-are-losing-their-sway)

Psalm 58 in the King James Bible put the deaf snake and the charmer together long before, saying of the people it condemns that ["they are like the deaf adder that stoppeth her ear,"](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm+58%3A4-5&version=KJV) an adder that ["will not hearken to the voice of charmers, charming never so wisely."](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm+58%3A4-5&version=KJV) The psalmist's adder at least has an ear to stop. Satyanarayan is right that snakes have no outer ears, but they keep the inner ear, and they are not deaf.

## Can snakes hear?

Yes, though only low sounds. Snakes have no outer ear and no eardrum, but they keep the inner ear, and the columella, the middle-ear bone that matches our stapes, [connects to the jawbone](https://nationalzoo.si.edu/animals/news/do-snakes-have-ears-and-other-sensational-serpent-questions). Ground vibrations reach the inner ear that way, and so does airborne sound strong enough to [shake the head](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22189777/). The Smithsonian's National Zoo puts snakes' range at [**50 to 1,000 hertz**](https://nationalzoo.si.edu/animals/news/do-snakes-have-ears-and-other-sensational-serpent-questions).

## The jaw stands in for the eardrum

In most four-limbed animals, the outer ear and an eardrum-based middle ear [match the air to the inner ear's fluids](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22189777/) and so improve hearing in air, and snakes have lost both. What they kept is a bony link from jaw to ear. The columella and the quadrate bone are [attached to the jawbone](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0281285), a team led from the University of Queensland writes, and their vibrations are thought to carry on to the inner ear.

The lower jaw usually lies on the ground, and its two sides move independently. In a 2008 paper in Physical Review Letters, Friedel, Young and van Hemmen described the jaw's connection to the inner ear as ["a loss-free osseous lever system"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18352341/), and modeled how the footfall of prey sends small, slow waves through the soil that move each side of the jaw. The jaw, their model showed, could give a snake ["a neuronal representation of the auditory world with realistic sensitivity and stereo precision."](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18352341/)

## A python hears airborne sound by feeling its skull shake

Pressure waves in the air are a harder case. In a 2012 study in the Journal of Experimental Biology, Christensen and three colleagues recorded brainstem responses in **11 royal pythons**, and found the snakes [most sensitive at 80-160 Hz](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22189777/) both to vibration and to sound pressure. They then measured how much threshold-level sound made the pythons' heads vibrate, and found that, in general, it was enough to explain the response.

Their verdict was that pythons, ["and possibly all snakes, lost effective pressure hearing"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22189777/) along with the outer and middle ear, while keeping an acute sense of vibration. A python registers a sound in the air by the vibration it sets up in the head, and vibration is the sense it does well.

The band is narrow, and it sits far below where people hear best:

| Listener | Range or best sensitivity |
|---|---|
| Snakes, overall | Vibrations between 50 and 1,000 Hz |
| Humans, overall | 20 to 20,000 Hz |
| Royal pythons, best | 80 to 160 Hz |
| Humans, best | 2,000 to 5,000 Hz |
| Sea snakes (Hydrophis stokesii, 2 animals), underwater | Responses from 40 Hz (lowest tested) to 600 Hz, best at 60 Hz |

The overall ranges are the [National Zoo's](https://nationalzoo.si.edu/animals/news/do-snakes-have-ears-and-other-sensational-serpent-questions), the python band is from Christensen's study, the human best band is from the [Queensland paper](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0281285), and the sea snake figures come from a 2019 study that measured [auditory evoked potential (AEP) audiograms for two individuals](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31345949/).

## Death adders moved away from sound in a soundproof room

Christina Zdenek of the University of Queensland and Damian Candusso of the Queensland University of Technology, with three co-authors, wanted to know what free-moving snakes do when a sound plays. They ran [**304 trials on 19 snakes from five genera**](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36787306/) in a soundproof room 4.9 metres square, with filtered pink noise in three bands: 0-150 Hz, which an accelerometer showed was shaking the floor, and 150-300 Hz and 300-450 Hz, which did not.

Every snake was captive-bred and Australian, and the speakers were set to [85 dB at the centre of the room](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0281285). The responses split by genus:

- **Woma pythons** were the only genus whose probability of moving rose with sound.
- **Death adders, taipans and brown snakes** were more likely to move away from it, though for brown snakes the effect was weak and showed in one band only.
- **Taipans** also became more likely to show defensive or cautious behavior.

Death adders moved away from the 150-300 Hz sound, which left no trace on the floor's accelerometer. The authors suggest that the adders' [flattened lower jaw](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0281285) may feel ground vibrations too faint for the instrument, so the line between hearing through air and feeling through the floor is blurry even in a controlled room.

The university's press release went a step past the paper. It quoted Zdenek saying that ["Only the woma python tended to move toward sound"](https://news.uq.edu.au/2023-02-15-snakes-can-hear-more-you-think), and that the snakes reacted to soundwaves in the air "and possibly human voices."

In the paper, the woma's movement toward the speakers is [a trend in the 300-450 Hz band](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0281285) only, against a trend away from them in the 0-150 Hz band, and neither was a difference the statistics could separate from zero. The trials were also scored live by observers who [knew whether a sound was playing](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0281285).

> "The common perception of snakes being deaf likely derives from a combination of 1) snakes' limited hearing ability (regarding frequency range and sensitivity), and 2) peoples' limited ability to notice and interpret many subtle snake behaviors." - [Zdenek and colleagues, PLOS ONE, 2023](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0281285)

## The charmer's cobra is watching the pipe

Satyanarayan's explanation of the dance does not need deafness. The charmer's pipe, he told NPR, is ["something which is menacing, above him, which is swaying"](https://www.npr.org/2011/08/08/139086119/in-india-snake-charmers-are-losing-their-sway), so the cobra fixes on it and moves along with it. Smithsonian's National Zoo is blunter about the act: it is ["often a sad con game in which an exhausted cobra is put on the defensive, yet conditioned (with pain) not to strike the flutist."](https://nationalzoo.si.edu/animals/king-cobra)

None of the studies above put a cobra in front of a gourd flute, so they leave open how much of the tune a cobra registers. What they rule out is the word deaf.

The real snake has no ear to stop, only an inner ear wired to its jaw, and it picks up the low end of the world through bone. Strictly FYI.
