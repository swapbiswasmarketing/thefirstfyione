In 2001, the evolutionary biologist Marlene Zuk and her colleagues worked their Kauai field sites listening for the field cricket *Teleogryllus oceanicus*, a species they had been [studying in Hawaii since 1991](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1834006/). Across intensive searches they heard **a single calling male**. Two years later the crickets were far more abundant than before, and none of them were singing.

Working out why that is strange starts with what a chirp actually is, and most people have that part wrong.

## Why do crickets chirp?

Adult male crickets chirp to broadcast a calling song that draws females in from a distance. The [Singing Insects of North America](https://orthsoc.org/sina/) catalogue, founded by Thomas J. Walker, says the males of most species of crickets and katydids make "loud, persistent calls that attract sexually ready, conspecific females." The sound is stridulation: a male rubs his forewings together, raking a hardened scraper on one wing across a toothed file on the other. Female crickets do not chirp, because their wings [carry no file and no scraper](https://askdruniverse.wsu.edu/2023/06/01/crickets-make-sound/).

## The leg-rubbing version belongs to grasshoppers

The picture most people carry is a cricket sawing its back legs together. That describes a different insect: crickets rake one wing against the other.

The Amateur Entomologists' Society glossary [describes the grasshopper's equipment](https://www.amentsoc.org/insects/glossary/terms/stridulation) as "a series of small pegs on the inside of their hind legs" that are "rubbed against the fore wing to produce the call". A cricket keeps both parts on its forewings: Rich Zack, an insect scientist at Washington State University, [describes a file and scraper](https://askdruniverse.wsu.edu/2023/06/01/crickets-make-sound/), a line of bumps like comb teeth on one wing and the hardened edge of the other wing dragged across them. Zack told Ask Dr. Universe, the university's science column, that only adult male crickets make sound; the column adds that males sometimes chirp to defend a territory, but "the main reason they chirp is to find a mate."

## She is listening through her shins

The audience is equipped to match. Cricket ears [sit on the tibia of the front legs](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5681576/), just below the knee, so a cricket hears the world through its shins.

Schneider and colleagues, [writing in Scientific Reports in 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5681576/), describe the ear of the field crickets *Gryllus* and *Teleogryllus* as two oval translucent membranes, one at the front of the leg and one at the back. A spiracle on each side of the thorax and an internal sound-guiding tracheal system feed them, making "a system of internally-coupled ears that are important for sound localization". Only the rear membrane is regarded as the main sound receiver.

That arrangement is what lets a female locate a singing male across a dark field. It comes with a matched transmitter: the same paper [puts most cricket calling songs between 2 and 8 kHz](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5681576/), and the Singing Insects of North America catalogue gives a dominant frequency for each species' recordings.

| Species | Dominant frequency in SINA's reference recording | Recorded at |
|---|---|---|
| [Fall field cricket (Gryllus pennsylvanicus)](https://orthsoc.org/sina/489a.htm) | 4.7 kHz | 24.4 C |
| [House cricket (Acheta domesticus)](https://orthsoc.org/sina/487a.htm) | 4.8 kHz | 26.5 C |
| [Snowy tree cricket (Oecanthus fultoni)](https://orthsoc.org/sina/585a.htm) | 2.9 kHz | 24.8 C |

The same male keeps a second song in reserve. [SINA's description of a fall field cricket courting a female](https://orthsoc.org/sina/489a.htm) has soft shuffling sounds and loud two-pulse chirps pitched like his 4.7 kHz calling song, plus **brief periodic ticks strongest around 12 kHz**. One song is the advertisement, the other is what he plays once she is within close range.

## The chirp thermometer works best on one cricket

In 1897 Amos Dolbear, who had [chaired the physics and astronomy department at Tufts College since 1874](https://web.archive.org/web/20140104231056/http://dl.tufts.edu/catalog/tei/tufts:UA069.005.DO.00001/chapter/D00047), published a two-page note in *The American Naturalist* titled ["The Cricket as a Thermometer"](https://archive.org/details/sim_american-naturalist_1897-11_31_371) (volume 31, pages 970 to 971). He began with a contrast: a lone cricket "chirps with no great regularity", but at night "one may hear all the crickets in a field chirping synchronously, keeping time as if led by the wand of a conductor." He then wrote that "the rate of chirp seems to be entirely determined by the temperature", gave [80 chirps a minute at 60 degrees Fahrenheit and 120 at 70](https://archive.org/details/sim_american-naturalist_1897-11_31_371), and turned that into a formula for reading air temperature off the chirp rate. Nowhere in those two pages does he name the cricket he had been listening to.

[SINA's species page](https://orthsoc.org/sina/585a.htm) says the **snowy tree cricket** is sometimes called the "thermometer cricket" "because its chirps are easily countable and their rate correlates well with the temperature at the cricket." The working rule: in the eastern United States, count chirps for 13 seconds and add 40 to get degrees Fahrenheit; west of the Great Plains, count for 12.5 seconds and add 38. The same page adds a caveat: "if you are really interested in using the snowy tree cricket as a thermometer, you should calibrate your local crickets against a thermometer placed near where they sing."

So the arithmetic changes with the species, and even with the region. The rasping field cricket under a porch step is not the insect that rule was worked out for.

## Everything that can hear the song is listening

A calling song has to carry. That makes it useful to more than the intended recipient, and on Kauai the extra listener was *Ormia ochracea*, a small parasitoid fly. Zuk, Rotenberry and Tinghitella, [reporting in Biology Letters in 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1834006/), note that the fly is North American in origin, uses song to find its hosts, and has larvae that "burrow into the cricket and develop inside, killing the host upon emergence". Kauai had always had the worst of it, with **nearly 30% of calling males** carrying the fly.

## Kauai switched the song off in 12 to 20 generations

Somewhere between the very late 1990s and 2003, [a mutation swept the island's population](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1834006/). In these "flatwing" males the file "is reduced in size and relocated at an angle precluding sound production", and the paper's verdict is blunt: "Flatwings are thus unable to call." In 2004 the team heard only a handful of callers, and of 133 males collected, 12 had normal wings. Zuk and her co-authors put the sweep at 12 to 20 generations, assuming four generations a year. Silence worked as armour: out of 121 flatwings the team dissected, only one harboured parasitoid larvae.

> On Kauai the calling song that brought a female to a male also brought the fly that killed him, and within a few years almost no male on the island could make it.

Silent males still find mates, and a field test points to how. When the team played a calling song from a speaker, Kauai's flatwings [stopped at a mean distance of 75.5 cm](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1834006/) from it, and Kauai males got closer to the speaker than males on the other two islands; male crickets drawn to another male's song usually settle at least 1 m away, the authors note. That fits the authors' hypothesis that silent males act as satellites, waiting beside the few remaining singers to intercept the females drawn in. Flatwings seen mating at the speakers suggest Kauai females will accept a male without hearing his courtship song.

Sonia Pascoal and colleagues, [reporting in Current Biology in 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24881880/), note that two years after Kauai the silent morph appeared on neighbouring Oahu, and found that the two islands' flatwings show "different genomic architectures consistent with the timing of two mutational events on the X chromosome". The count has since grown. In 2021 Xiao Zhang and colleagues, writing in [Nature Communications](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7782688/), reported "distinct flatwing phenotypes in populations on three islands, localized to different loci", the third being the Big Island, where flatwings turned up five years after Kauai. The same silence had evolved more than once.

Step outside tonight and the noise in the grass is males dragging a wing across a comb, a fall field cricket near 4.7 kHz, aimed at females listening through their front legs. On one Hawaiian island the same kind of broadcast worked as a delivery address for a fly, and in under twenty generations most of the population had gone off the air. A calling song is a location as much as an invitation. Strictly FYI.
