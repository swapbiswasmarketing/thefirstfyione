[The Times of 2 August 1838](https://www.johnmcquaid.co.uk/p/painting-town.html) reported a trial heard at Derby on 31 July before a special jury. The Marquis of Waterford and three friends were charged with a riot at Melton Mowbray, in Leicestershire, "on the morning of the 6th of April, 1837."

The [paper's summary of the evidence](https://www.johnmcquaid.co.uk/p/painting-town.html) includes a watchman named Barnes, thrown down in the street: "They then painted his face and throat with red paint. A little gentleman held the paint pot, while the Marquis used the brush and laid it on thick." A second watchman saw Barnes's face and throat "all over red" and "supposed he was covered with blood."

The jury found all the defendants guilty, but only of common assault, and each was fined 100 pounds, though none of them was in court to hear it. The report, [transcribed in full](https://www.johnmcquaid.co.uk/p/painting-town.html) by the Melton history site Yesterday in Melton Mowbray, never uses the phrase.

## What does paint the town red mean?

To paint the town red is to go out and celebrate loudly, usually with plenty to drink. The Oxford English Dictionary, as [quoted by Grammarphobia](https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2007/01/paint-the-town-red.html), defines it as "to enjoy oneself flamboyantly; go on a boisterous or exuberant spree." The phrase is American, and its earliest known example is from Omaha in **1874**.

## The Melton Mowbray story has the paint but not the phrase

*A Concise Dictionary of Phrase and Fable* by B.A. Phythian (1993) calls the most probable origin "an actual piece of drunken vandalism by the Marquis of Waterford and a bunch of his chums," as [Word Histories](https://wordhistories.net/2016/08/26/paint-the-town-red/) quotes it.

A radical evening paper's account, [reprinted on the Melton site](https://www.johnmcquaid.co.uk/p/painting-town.html), has the party out after the first day of the Croxton Park races with "a large tin of red paint," and the Marquis lifted up to "the figure of the Old White Swan," whose "colour was quickly altered to red." By July 1837, the [Phrase Finder](https://www.phrases.org.uk/meanings/paint-the-town-red.html) shows, the New Sporting Magazine was announcing Ackermann's prints of these "disgraceful proceedings termed 'sprees,'" one showing gentlemen in scarlet coats "painting the sign of the White Swan red."

None of that coverage uses the words *paint the town red*.

## The earliest known printing is an Omaha boast from 1874

On 31 July 1874 the *Omaha Daily Republican* looked ahead to the day "when all the Iowa roads will run their trains into Omaha's mammoth depot, and in that day, as Harry Deuel says, 'we'll paint the whole town red and sing the hallelujah chorus.'" Bonnie Taylor-Blake [posted the passage](https://listserv.linguistlist.org/pipermail/ads-l/2023-October/164278.html), from page 4, to the American Dialect Society's mailing list on 12 October 2023, and [Grammarphobia](https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2007/01/paint-the-town-red.html), which describes her as a neuroscience researcher, now gives it as the earliest example its authors know. That is **37 years** after the Melton spree.

A looser form is older. [Green's Dictionary of Slang](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/uowyn6q) cites an 1865 letter, quoted in Bell Irvin Wiley's *The Life of Johnny Reb* (1943), whose writer hoped to "visit the Theaters may be get on a big Whope & Paint the thing red."

The exact phrase took off in the **1880s**: [Grammarphobia's authors](https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2007/01/paint-the-town-red.html) have seen "a couple of examples from 1880, and dozens from later in the '80s," and they give the OED's first reference as the Stanford, Kentucky, line of March 1882 in the table below. A few early examples:

## By 1884, papers were inventing origins

By September 1884 [a sketch in the *Wheeling Daily Intelligencer*](https://wordhistories.net/2016/08/26/paint-the-town-red/), set on a Detroit street corner, had a puzzled stranger ask: "Almost every paper I pick up has something in it about somebody painting the town red." Pascal Treguer's Word Histories collects **seven** explanations as folk etymologies, the Melton spree among them. In one, from the *Harrisburg Telegraph* of November 1884, a minstrel manager says the phrase began when his advance agent covered Adrian, Michigan, in posters "printed in bright red." Treguer found no newspaper record of the incident.

A Danbury paper, [quoted in North Carolina](https://wordhistories.net/2016/08/26/paint-the-town-red/) in December 1884, credited Connecticut's governor, Thomas Waller, quoting him telling a winning boat crew: "I hope, gentlemen, you will paint this town red." What he told the Harvard crew in **June 1883**, as New Haven's *Morning Herald and Courier* printed it, was a request not to: "I hope you will not, as some say you intend, paint the whole town red." He spoke nearly nine years after the Omaha paper.

## The red-light and frontier-bullies stories have no early evidence

The frontier version was circulating by 1885. Writing to Chicago's *Daily Inter Ocean* in January 1885 as ["Johannes Factotum,"](https://wordhistories.net/2016/08/26/paint-the-town-red/) a reader claimed the phrase began "on the far frontier," where "a set of bullies armed to the teeth" threatened "to paint the town red before the next morning." Treguer notes that no evidence supports it.

The *Morris Dictionary of Word and Phrase Origins*, as the [Phrase Finder](https://www.phrases.org.uk/meanings/paint-the-town-red.html) summarises it, links the phrase to the red-light district. That term is younger than the Omaha paper: red-light districts are mentioned in [the 1882 minutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red-light_district) of a Woman's Christian Temperance Union meeting, and the Oxford English Dictionary's earliest printed example is from an **1894** Ohio newspaper. None of the early examples in the table mentions a brothel.

## By 1890 the slang dictionaries had entered it

John S. Farmer's [Americanisms, Old and New](https://archive.org/details/americanismsoldn00farmuoft), printed in London in **1889**, defines the phrase as "To go on a drunken spree," and generally "to act the fool." One of his quotations, from the *Washington Critic* of 1888, has a Boston maiden say "He stains the town incarnadine," and a western maid put it back into slang: "You mean he paints the whole town red." Under *bender*, Farmer describes a drinking bout in which "the town is painted red."

The second volume of Albert Barrere and Charles Leland's [A Dictionary of Slang, Jargon & Cant](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofslan02barriala), dated 1890, files the phrase as American and explains it with a passage from Cumberland's *The Queen's Highway*: "a Western expression" for "the height of reckless debauch," earned by a cowboy who has shot holes through hats, smashed the saloon's glasses and ridden "with a wild whoop down the principal street."

## The choice of red is still unexplained

> [Grammarphobia's authors](https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2007/01/paint-the-town-red.html), who report dozens of examples from the 1880s, write: "We haven't seen any published examples that explain why the color red was used rather than, say, green or blue or orange."

Treguer's hypothesis is a town lit up by torches or fireworks. In November 1884 the [*Hawaiian Gazette*](https://wordhistories.net/2016/08/26/paint-the-town-red/) reported that a Honolulu crowd "became hilarious, and it was proposed to 'paint the town red' by means of a torchlight procession," and Nashville's *Daily American* had Tennessee Democrats who "painted the town red with fireworks."

A second hypothesis ties the phrase to drink. *Paint* could mean to drink: [Green's Dictionary of Slang](https://greensdictofslang.com/entry/h7xrweq) records that sense from the 17th century, and in [Digby Grand](https://archive.org/details/digbygrandanaut00melvgoog/page/n82/mode/1up), an **1853** novel by George Whyte-Melville, Treguer points out, it belongs to "a real Yankee character" who says at every hotel they pass, "I guess I shall go in and paint." Barrere and Leland [glossed that verb](https://archive.org/details/dictionaryofslan02barriala) as "to drink, alluding to a red nose caused by over-indulgence," which puts the red in the drinker's face rather than on the town. Both ideas remain hypotheses.

The Marquis of Waterford and his friends really did paint doors, a watchman and an inn sign red, and a court convicted him and three of them of common assault. The words the phrase books credit to them first turn up in print 37 years later in Nebraska, in a newspaper looking forward to more trains. Strictly FYI.
