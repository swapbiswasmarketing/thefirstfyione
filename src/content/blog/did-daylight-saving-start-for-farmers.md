Ask who daylight saving time was invented for and the answer usually comes back the same: farmers. They wanted more daylight in the fields, so the government moved the clocks forward to give it to them, and the change we still perform twice a year is a favour to agriculture that has outlived the farms it was meant for.

That explanation is tidy, and it comes around every March and every November. It is also backwards. The people credited with asking for daylight saving are the people who got rid of it.

## Did daylight saving time start for farmers?

No. National daylight saving began as a wartime fuel measure. [Germany adopted it on **April 30, 1916** to conserve electricity](https://www.cbc.ca/news/science/daylight-saving-time-1.4567430), and the United States copied it in [a law enacted **March 19, 1918**](https://www.webexhibits.org/daylightsaving/usstat.html). [American farmers did not lobby for it: the agriculture industry opposed the change](https://www.history.com/articles/8-things-you-may-not-know-about-daylight-saving-time), and agrarian interests led the fight that repealed national daylight saving in **1919**.

## The farm lobby is what ended it

The switch began on **March 31, 1918**, and [History.com's account of the rollout](https://www.history.com/articles/8-things-you-may-not-know-about-daylight-saving-time) records the agriculture industry as strongly opposed from that first season. A year later the opposition had its way. In 1919 Congress voted to repeal the daylight saving section of the 1918 law, and President **Woodrow Wilson** vetoed the repeal twice: [on **July 12, 1919**](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/message-the-house-representatives-returning-without-approval-act-making-appropriations-for) he returned the agriculture appropriations bill "because of the clause" that carried it, and [on **August 15**](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/message-the-house-representatives-returning-without-approval-act-for-the-repeal-the) he returned the standalone repeal while conceding "the very considerable and in some respects very serious inconveniences to which the daylight-saving law subjects the farmers of the country". [Congress overrode the second veto](https://www.webexhibits.org/daylightsaving/e.html), and the national clock change was gone.

What survived was patchy. The [WebExhibits Daylight Saving Time exhibit](https://www.webexhibits.org/daylightsaving/e.html) records that the practice continued as a local option in a few states, among them **Massachusetts and Rhode Island**, and in cities including **New York, Philadelphia and Chicago**. The [Congressional Research Service](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R45208.html) dates the national gap precisely: no federal daylight saving time observance from **1920 through 1941**.

## Dew, cows and hired hands all kept solar time

The farm objection was mechanical rather than sentimental. [History.com sets out the practical problems](https://www.history.com/articles/8-things-you-may-not-know-about-daylight-saving-time): farmers had to wait an extra hour for the dew to evaporate before cutting hay, hired hands still left at the same time for dinner and so worked less, and cows were not ready to be milked an hour earlier to meet shipping schedules. The dew answers to sunrise, so an advanced clock added an hour of standing around.

Legislating the clock does not move the sun. It moves the farm out of step with the towns it sells to, and in 1918 the towns had the schedule while the farms had the sunlight.

## Franklin proposed cannon fire, not clock changes

The other half of the origin story credits **Benjamin Franklin**, and reading what he actually wrote is the quickest way to retire it.

[His **1784** letter to the *Journal de Paris*](https://www.webexhibits.org/daylightsaving/franklin3.html) reads as satire aimed at Parisians who slept through the morning. The remedies escalate. [He proposes](https://www.webexhibits.org/daylightsaving/franklin3.html) a tax of **a louis per window** on every window fitted with shutters to keep out the light of the sun, guards in the shops of the wax and tallow chandlers so that no family could be supplied with more than **one pound of candles per week**, and more guards stopping every coach in the streets after sunset, except those of physicians, surgeons and midwives. Then the finish, in [Franklin's own words](https://www.webexhibits.org/daylightsaving/franklin3.html): "Every morning, as soon as the sun rises, let all the bells in every church be set ringing; and if that is not sufficient, let cannon be fired in every street, to wake the sluggards effectually, and make them open their eyes to see their true interest."

Not one line proposes moving a clock. [History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/8-things-you-may-not-know-about-daylight-saving-time) states the misattribution plainly: Franklin "only proposed a change in sleep schedules".

## The same 1918 act created America's time zones

The statute that gave the United States daylight saving is titled ["An Act to save daylight and to provide standard time for the United States"](https://www.webexhibits.org/daylightsaving/usstat.html), and it did both jobs in one text. [The Congressional Research Service records](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R45208.html) that railroads had used four standard time zones since **1883**, and that the 1918 law, also called the Calder Act, established **five** for the continental United States and Alaska, set the summer clock change alongside them, and authorized the **Interstate Commerce Commission** to fix the zone boundaries and change them as necessary. Zones are the reason 12:00 on a modern clock comes from the time zone rather than [the sun's midday](/blog/what-does-am-stand-for/), and daylight saving moves solar noon an hour later on the clock.

> When the farm lobby won in 1919, it repealed only the daylight saving section. The zones stayed. Eastern, Central, Mountain and Pacific are the surviving half of the same law that produced the clock change everyone credits to farming.

## Eighty minutes, in four steps, and the main rules since

The scheme Britain debated was stranger than the one the world settled on. **William Willett**'s 1907 pamphlet ["The Waste of Daylight"](https://www.webexhibits.org/daylightsaving/willett.html) asked for **80 minutes**, advanced 20 minutes on each of four Sundays in April and given back the same way on four Sundays in September. [The Summer Time Act](https://statutes.org.uk/site/the-statutes/twentieth-century/1916-6-7-george-5-c-14-summer-time-act/) received Royal Assent on **May 17, 1916**, and the country moved its clocks four days later, on **May 21**. The American windows below come from [the 1918 statute](https://www.webexhibits.org/daylightsaving/usstat.html) and [WebExhibits' history of US law](https://www.webexhibits.org/daylightsaving/e.html).

| Law | The daylight saving window it set |
|---|---|
| Standard Time Act, 1918 | Last Sunday in March to last Sunday in October |
| Repeal, 1919 | None nationally, local option only |
| Uniform Time Act, 1966 | Last Sunday in April to last Sunday in October |
| 1986 amendment | First Sunday in April to last Sunday in October |
| Energy Policy Act, 2005 | Second Sunday in March to first Sunday in November |

[NIST](https://www.nist.gov/pml/time-and-frequency-division/popular-links/daylight-saving-time-dst) puts the current window at **238 days, about 65% of the year**, running from 2:00 a.m. on the second Sunday in March to 2:00 a.m. on the first Sunday in November. [The Congressional Research Service lists](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R45208.html) **Hawaii, most of Arizona, American Samoa, Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands, Puerto Rico and the US Virgin Islands** as sitting it out, and [NIST notes](https://www.nist.gov/pml/time-and-frequency-division/popular-links/daylight-saving-time-dst) the exception inside Arizona: the Navajo Indian Reservation keeps it.

Even that is short of the two spells of year-round daylight saving: [Congress ran it as War Time](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R45208.html) from **1942** until the last Sunday in September **1945**, and tried it again from **January 6, 1974** until standard time returned on **October 27, 1974**, under an amendment Congress passed that month; daylight saving resumed on February 23, 1975, and the trial period ended on April 27, 1975.

The fuel case that started everything has never quite closed either. A [Department of Energy study of the extended window](https://www1.eere.energy.gov/ba/pba/pdfs/epact_sec_110_edst_report_to_congress_2008.pdf) found it cut national electricity consumption by **0.03 percent** in 2007, which works out at roughly **0.02 percent** of all the energy the United States used that year.

So the complaint that arrives every March has the wrong defendant. The farm lobby's position was firm enough to break a wartime statute and beat a sitting president, and the only piece of that law still standing is the reason a call from Boston to Denver crosses two hours. Strictly FYI.
