In one ancient account of the Trojan War, nobody shoots Achilles at all. He is at the temple of Thymbraean Apollo during a festival truce, unarmed, going over King Priam's terms for the Trojan princess Polyxena. In the Journal of the Trojan War credited to Dictys of Crete, the Trojan prince Deiphobus embraces him and hangs on until Paris, [with sword drawn, "rushed forward and thrust two blows in the victim's sides."](https://www.theoi.com/Text/DictysCretensis4.html)

Dying, Achilles names his killers, Deiphobus and Paris, under Paris's other name: ["Deiphobus and Alexander overpowered me."](https://www.theoi.com/Text/DictysCretensis4.html) There is no arrow in that version and no heel, and the Journal, a prose work that [purports to be "a first hand account of the Trojan War"](https://www.theoi.com/Text/DictysCretensis1.html) by a Cretan in the Greek camp, is only one of several deaths antiquity gave its greatest fighter. The famous one, a single arrow into the one spot his mother missed when she dipped him in the Styx, was put together in pieces, centuries apart.

In the oldest Greek sources, **Paris and the god Apollo** kill Achilles in battle at Troy. Later writers put the arrow in his ankle. The story that his mother dipped him in the Styx first appears in a Roman poem of the late 1st century AD, which never says which spot she missed.

## Homer names the killers and never shows the killing

The Iliad ends before Achilles dies. Hector, with his last breath, warns him of ["the day when Paris and Phoebus Apollo shall slay thee, valorous though thou art, at the Scaean gate."](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HomerIliad22.html) Earlier, Achilles complains that his mother told him he would die beneath the wall of Troy ["by the swift missiles of Apollo."](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HomerIliad21.html)

Nor is he invulnerable in the poem. A spear thrown by Asteropaeus [smote "the right forearm of Achilles a grazing blow, and the black blood gushed forth."](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HomerIliad21.html) Barbara Care, in a 2026 paper in the Bulletin of the Institute of Classical Studies, puts it plainly: ["he is never depicted as invulnerable in the epic poem."](https://academic.oup.com/bics/article/69/1/202/8539880)

## The Odyssey holds the funeral and leaves out the wound

In book 24 of the Odyssey, the ghost of Agamemnon tells the ghost of Achilles that he was ["slain in the land of Troy far from Argos"](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HomerOdyssey24.html), that "the best of the sons of the Trojans and Achaeans" fell fighting for his body, and that he lay "in the whirl of dust" while the Greeks fought on until Zeus stopped them with a storm.

Gods and men mourned him for [**seventeen days** and burned him on the eighteenth](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HomerOdyssey24.html). His white bones went into a golden urn from his mother, mixed with the bones of Patroclus, under a great tomb on a headland by the Hellespont.

## The sequel to the Iliad killed him as he stormed the city

The poem that picked up the story, the Aethiopis, is lost. What survives is chiefly a prose summary credited to Proclus, in which the Iliad ["is followed in turn by the five books of the Aethiopis"](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/348/348-h/348-h.htm), and in which Achilles kills Memnon, then ["routs the Trojans, and, rushing into the city with them, is killed by Paris and Apollo."](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/348/348-h/348-h.htm)

Aias carries the body to the ships, and Thetis later snatches her son from the pyre and takes him to the White Island. Paris dies in the next poem of the cycle, the Little Iliad, where Philoctetes ["fights in single combat with Alexandrus and kills him."](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/348/348-h/348-h.htm)

Vase painters showed the wound: on a Chalcidian amphora of about **540 BC**, formerly in the Pembroke-Hope collection and now lost, Care writes, ["Achilles looks injured at the back of the lower leg, just above the ankle."](https://academic.oup.com/bics/article/69/1/202/8539880) She calls "even more significant" a second arrow stuck in his flank. The painter gave him **two arrows**, and only one of them is near the ankle.

In writing, the ankle turns up in the handbook of myths that goes under the name of Apollodorus: ["Achilles was shot with an arrow in the ankle by Alexander and Apollo at the Scaean gate."](https://www.theoi.com/Text/ApollodorusE.html) Quintus of Smyrna, whose Greek epic is now dated to [the third century](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ramus/article/transforming-the-homeric-models-quintus-battle-among-the-gods-in-the-posthomerica/5ECE54729D6D32262EA52943CAE26579) AD, writes Paris out of the killing: Apollo, ["cloaked with mist"](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/658/pg658-images.html), fires an arrow that ["leapt to Achilles' ankle"](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/658/pg658-images.html).

## The dip in the Styx first appears in a Roman poem

The story that Thetis dipped the infant Achilles in the Styx first appears in the Achilleid of Statius, a Roman poet of the [**late 1st century AD**](https://www.theoi.com/Text/StatiusAchilleid1A.html) who left the poem unfinished. Care's survey of the sources finds no earlier text: the dip makes ["its first appearance in the works of Statius."](https://academic.oup.com/bics/article/69/1/202/8539880)

Even there it comes up only in passing, in three brief allusions in the first book. In one, Thetis, coaxing her son into a disguise, reminds him of the dip at his birth and breaks off with a regret:

> "I fortified thee with the stern waters of Styx ... ay, would I had wholly!" - Thetis to Achilles, in Statius's Achilleid

The line names no heel. In the theoi.com translation, a footnote supplies it, saying the dip made his body immune [all "except the left heel by which she held him."](https://www.theoi.com/Text/StatiusAchilleid1A.html) Older tellings had used fire instead: in the Argonautica of Apollonius of Rhodes, a poet of the [3rd century BC](https://www.theoi.com/Text/ApolloniusRhodius1.html), Thetis ["ever encompassed the child's mortal flesh in the night with the flame of fire"](https://www.theoi.com/Text/ApolloniusRhodius4.html) until Peleus saw his son ["gasping in the flame"](https://www.theoi.com/Text/ApolloniusRhodius4.html) and cried out.

The Latin handbook of Hyginus has Apollo, angry, [taking "the form of Alexander Paris"](https://topostext.org/work/206) and shooting Achilles in the spot "which was said to be vulnerable." The translator calls it the heel. The Latin word is [**talum**](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/hyginus/hyginus5.shtml), and Care, who notes that Hyginus and the later Fulgentius ["specify the talus as the vulnerable spot"](https://academic.oup.com/bics/article/69/1/202/8539880), reads it as the astragalus, the ankle bone.

## Two prose chronicles swapped the arrow for an ambush

The Dictys temple scene has a twin. The History of the Fall of Troy credited to Dares of Phrygia, [probably written in the early 6th century AD](https://www.theoi.com/Text/DaresPhrygius.html), also lures Achilles to the temple of Apollo with the promise of Polyxena, where Paris ["slaughtered Achilles, dealing him many a blow."](https://www.theoi.com/Text/DaresPhrygius.html)

## The tendon had his name by 1693

The anatomy came last. In his textbook Corporis humani anatomia, [printed in **1693**](https://archive.org/details/corporishumanian00verh), the anatomist Philip Verheyen describes the tendon of the calf that attaches to the back of the heel bone, and says it is commonly called *chorda Achillis*, the cord of Achilles, and by Hippocrates *tendo magnus*, the great tendon.

Hector's dying warning named Paris and Apollo, and every later teller who names a killer kept at least one of them. What changed over the centuries is the wound: two arrows on a vase, an ankle in Apollodorus and Quintus, the talus in Hyginus and, in the English translations, a heel. Strictly FYI.
